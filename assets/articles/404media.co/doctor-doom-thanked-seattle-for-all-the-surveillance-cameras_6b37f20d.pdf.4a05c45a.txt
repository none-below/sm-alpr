Doom is actually Clifford Cawthon, a policy advisor for the Washington State
Department of Commerce. Cawthorn donned the silver mask and gloves to draw
attention to tech-driven mass surveillance in Seattle, an issue he’s been
passionate about for years. Cawthon is also a member of Community Not
Cameras, a local activist group that’s attempting to reign in Seattle’s use of
surveillance systems sold by Flock and Axon.
Seattle’s PD has access to 62 surveillance cameras around the city, many of
them hooked up to a real-time crime center (RTCC). “The RTCC takes in
information from a vast pool of sources, including CCTV, [body-worn camera],
geolocation data, automatic license plate recognition (ALPR), alert sources like
shot detection or alarm systems, and computer-aided dispatch (CAD) software,”
according to a description on the surveillance company Axon’s website.

“There is a wall of about nine screens in which they can bring up cameras from
around the city at any given time,” Cawthon said. “They can also log in,
conceivably, even from their home computers with the right credentials [...] I am
not reassured by the kind of dazzling scale of this complex communications
center.”
After the visit to the RTCC and after Vader appeared in California, one of
Cawthon’s colleagues called him with an idea. “What if Doctor Doom went to the
city council?” Cawthon said. Community Not Cameras was looking to drive
attention to the issue of mass surveillance and had just drafted a petition with
specific asks of the city council. A pop-culture infused stunt might get people
talking.
“We thought, wouldn’t it be interesting if the Seattle city council meets Victor
von Doom, the Lord Emperor of Latveria,” Cawthon told me, adopting the
vaguely Eastern European accent of Doctor Doom. “And Victor von Doom shares
with this pathetic American municipal council that they are actually doing
Doom’s bidding without even realizing it.”
Cawthon is a comics fan and knew that Doom was on people’s radar because
he’s the villain in the forthcoming Avengers Doomsday. He told me that pop
culture has taught people to identify with heroes like the Jedi, the Avengers, the
X-Men, and the Fantastic Four. “Would our heroes really use tools of mass
surveillance on a regular basis in a way that’s unaccountable and hurts innocent
people? They wouldn’t,” he said.

“The absurdity of the real time crime center and the absurdity of Axon and Flock
owning these devices and this data being used around the country to capture
and lock up and deport people and to harass women,” Cawthon said in his
normal voice. “It was so clear this is a tool of abuse and being used in such
villainous ways [...] we figured, why not use satire, political theater, to really
illustrate what these tools mean.”
Cawthon grandstanded behind the podium at the Seattle city council meeting on
Tuesday. “Doom is above all. The means are more than justified by the ends,” he
said. “Doom will keep you safe,” and  “We must work to crush any dissent to
Doom’s vision of public safety.”
On the phone with me, Cawthon ran through the list of his problems with
surveillance tech. “The data is not secure, in many cases these companies —
and they’re very open about this — work with ICE, work with the US military, and
border patrol. There are multiple instances of police officers literally using this
data to stalk their partners and abuse everyday people. And, of course, racial
profiling,” he said. “The increased potential for racist harassment of black and
brown folks like myself.”
Cawthon said reactions to his political theater have been positive and that it's
achieved the goal of driving people to look at the Community Not Cameras
petition. The group wants Seattle’s city council to take five actions including
redirecting the flow of money away from surveillance tech and back into
community-based crime prevention programs. 
He said that the council had already agreed to its first demand. Last month a
committee voted to move $250,000 away from CCTV expansion. Now the
measure just needs approval from the full council.

“Doom will be watching you. Glory to Latveria,” Doom said during the city
council meeting on Tuesday.
Last month activist Anthony Ralphs showed up to a San Diego City Council
meeting dressed as Darth Vader and explained how much the Empire loved
Flock cameras. Footage of the stunt went viral. Ralphs told 404 Media that he’d
dressed as Vader after years of attending public meetings as himself and failing
to draw attention to the issues of surveillance. Earlier this month, a woman
attended a public meeting in Texas in full Joker makeup to complain about
decorum at city council meetings.
According to Cawthon, this was all about “donning this pop culture persona and
drawing a connection between what is in reality a massive threat to our civil
liberties and civil rights.”
“These stories, these ideas about fascism, authoritarianism, they can be very
close to home if we let them. Becoming the character and going to city councils
is political theater in the best way. It is informing people in a way that is
accessible because you know these cultural figures are in the zeitgeist,” he said.
“I know plenty of people that, when I talk about public policy, they just throw up
their hands and say ‘Okay, well, that’s cool and thank you for sharing with me
but politics isn’t my thing,” he said. “But I can talk Marvel with them, I can talk
video games, and we have a common language.”
