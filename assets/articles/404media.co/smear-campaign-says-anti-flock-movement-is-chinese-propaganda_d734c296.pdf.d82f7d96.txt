Over the weekend, more than a dozen local TV news stations ran a video report
called “Far-left network behind ‘No Kings’ protests targeting AI data centers,
Flock cams.” The report paints those opposing Flock not as a diverse,
decentralized, bipartisan group of ordinary citizens who oppose mass
surveillance, but as well-funded radical leftists who are spreading propaganda
at the behest of China and rich billionaires. The opposite is true, of course: The
entities defending Flock and painting their opponents as cynical, paid
influencers have a specific agenda, have deep pockets, and are connected to
some of the most powerful people in the world.
Many of the most vocal defenders of Flock are attempting a strategy that Flock
itself tried soon after evidence of the abuse of its automated license plate reader
camera systems was reported by 404 Media and other local news outlets: They
are suggesting that anyone who opposes this technology or points out its
shortcomings must be part of a coordinated group of people who hate the police

The strategy to paint ordinary citizens opposing Flock and the journalists who
report factual information about how Flock has been used as radicals did not
work out well for Langley, who recently apologized for his remarks. But parts of
the conservative media, several well-funded think tanks, the former head of the
National Security Agency, and Flock’s investors are trying to run the playbook
back. 
Last week, a lobbying group called Consumer Action for a Strong Economy
published a report called “The Compute War At Home.” The report itself features
a cover with obvious AI-generated imagery, and the AI writing detector Pangram
detected nearly everything except the end citations of the report as being likely
written by AI. The foreword of the report was written by Rob Joyce, the former
head of the NSA. This report suggests that opposition to Flock (and to data
centers) is being driven by “China-linked activists networks and aligned
organizations connecting opposition to data centers with surveillance, policing,
and immigration.” The report suggests that opposition to data centers and to
Flock is partly being driven by groups it says have received funding from Neville
Roy Singham, an American tech billionaire who now lives in China and who has
become a George Soros-like figure in conservative media (a recent Fox News
headline, for example, has the headline “DOJ launches grand jury probe into
Marxist mogul Neville Roy Singham’s funding of leftist groups.”)

Opposition to Flock, the report says, is the result of “socialist organizers” being
partially driven by the People’s Republic of China who found an issue that
people on the right could agree with them on as a “wedge issue” they could use
to “discredit the broader technologies, companies, and infrastructure
underpinning America’s AI expansion.” As evidence of this, it points to the fact
that Breakthrough News, an organization that has gotten money from Singham,
who happens to live in China, wrote an article about Flock last year. This article
was not particularly popular judging by its performance on social media, was
one in a sea of thousands of articles about Flock, and is one I have not seen
cited in any meaningful way. It also notes that members of the Party for
Socialism and Liberation (PSL), a leftist political party funded by Singham, have
posted about Flock on social media and have held information sessions on it. It
is literally true that these groups have received funding from Singham and that
they have posted about Flock or AI data centers, but there is nothing to suggest
that their posts are particularly influential in a sea of millions of social media
posts and thousands of deeply reported articles about Flock and the ways it has
been abused.
The report is difficult to follow but basically attempts to suggest that far left
socialist organizers have manipulated conservatives into opposing Flock and
data centers. The report says that conservatives now make up the bulk of the
online opposition to Flock: “81 percent of politically classifiable fused Flock-data
center posts came from right-wing/conservative or libertarian accounts,” even
though, it says, opposing Flock is a viewpoint that originated with far-left
interests that align with China. This mechanism, where the left has supposedly
incepted the right into agreeing with them, is “strategically important.”
“An argument introduced through left-wing organizing channels was able to
travel through a much larger conservative audience once packaged around
privacy, surveillance, and distrust of government technology,” the report says.

It should be noted that the “No Kings” protests are definitively not a “far-left”
movement, and that there have been numerous examples of Flock being used
against No Kings protesters. It is true that groups on the left have been posting
about Flock, just as groups on the right have been posting about Flock, because
huge swaths of the internet are posting about Flock, because it has become a
huge, controversial topic.

years. I have watched dozens of city council meetings in small towns, big cities,

Many people who show up to city council meetings to oppose Flock are

database of Flock lookups, was much the same (and has led directly to police

Charles Fain Lehman, a senior fellow for the conservative think tank the
Manhattan Institute associated with billionaire hedge fund manager and staunch

It is impossible to know the motivations of every single person or account who
posts on social media, and it is easy to point at the most extreme people posting
online and use them to fashion a narrative to undermine legitimate concerns
about that thing. The leftists who have posted about Flock are certainly not
driving the conversation any more than any other group, and the same argument
could be made about far right groups; government surveillance centers have

It would be equally possible and equally disingenuous to write an article saying
that the anti-Flock movement is being driven by neo-Nazis. 
It’s simply the case that lots of people are talking about Flock now, which is how
the news and, notably, social media algorithms work. People learn about a thing,
they post about it, people care about that thing and the algorithm amplifies it.
More people begin posting about it and people who see success with that topic
post about it more. This is a very simple phenomenon that has existed for many
years; I would not impugn people who are using the internet in the way it is
currently designed or question their motives, so it is interesting that Lehman and
the Manhattan Institute is levying these accusations about other people’s
interest in Flock considering that the Manhattan Institute had not mentioned
Flock in any serious way until August, when the Institute’s Rafael Mangual wrote
“These Cameras Save Lives, But Some Want Them Banned.” Lehman followed
this up with a piece in The Atlantic called “In Defense of Flock” on August 10.
Since then, Lehman has tweeted about Flock more than a hundred times, which
includes posts accusing people of opposing Flock of being paid to do so: “Posts
about Flock that used to get hundreds of angry responses are now mostly
getting likes. Maybe the money stopped flowing!” a16z employees have also
repeatedly made tweets defending Flock in recent weeks, and a16z venture
capitalist David Ulevitch tweeted implying that 404 Media is funded by, or
working with, the DSA and defund the police movements (we are funded by our
individual subscribers; Ulevitch has since deleted his tweet). 
I have no doubt that The Manhattan Institute’s fellows are genuinely interested in
defending Flock, but this is not an affordance that they make to people they
perceive as having the wrong opinion about Flock.

interests, they should look no further than what Flock itself does. The company
regularly holds private meetings with police about how to defend the company,
how to advocate for the company and ‘own the narrative’ at city council
meetings, and how to handle (or avoid) meetings with journalists. It throws
conferences specifically for police that it does not allow outside journalists into,
and has repeatedly tried to publicly and privately question the motives of people
who oppose it. Flock’s lobbying spending has also skyrocketed, according to a
report in The Intercept. The government and its surveillance apparatus,
meanwhile, surveils people who post negative things about Flock on social

In Joyce’s interview with Fox News, he says that a “foreign influence operation
can simply identify an existing division, amplify the most combustible narratives,
and it pushes Americans farther apart. It polarizes us.” 
But opposition to Flock, and to data centers, is not an issue that “divides”
Americans. These are some of the very few issues in American society that
people across political, class, and regional lines actually agree with each other
on. The people and entities trying to drive Americans against each other are the
ones trying to paint normal American citizens as coordinated paid protesters and
who themselves work for extremely well-funded, politically-slanted
organizations or are directly affiliated with Flock itself. 
Flock CEO Langley’s attempt to smear people who opposed Flock as
propagandists and leftists to the police chief in Staunton, Virginia, didn’t work. 
“As far as your assertion that we are currently under attack, I do not believe that
this is so. I have dedicated the last 41 years of my life to serving the citizens of
the City of Staunton as a police officer, the last 22 as the police chief,” he wrote.
“What we are seeing here is a group of local citizens who are raising concerns
that we could be potentially surveilling private citizens, residents and visitors
and using the data for nefarious purposes. These citizens have been exercising
their rights to receive answers from me, my staff, and city officials, to include
our elected leaders. ln short, it is democracy in action.” 
There’s no indication that this broader campaign will work, either. But that hasn’t
stopped Flock’s proponents from trying.
