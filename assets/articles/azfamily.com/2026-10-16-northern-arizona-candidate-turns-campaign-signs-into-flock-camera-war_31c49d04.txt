running for a seat on the Yavapai College District Governing Board and his strategy of getting eyes on his campaign may be working. By Zach Prelutsky Published : Oct. 15, 2026 at 9:57 PM MST | Updated : 18 hours ago Share Add Us On Google Add as a preferred source on Google PRESCOTT, AZ (AZFamily) — A candidate running for office in Northern Arizona is using his campaign, and Arizona campaign laws, to highlight a controversial topic entirely unrelated to the position he’s running for. “It is absolutely a cheap stunt to get attention to my campaign, but it’s also calling attention to a bigger issue,” explained Ryan Jones. Jones is running for a seat on the Yavapai College District Governing Board , but last week he started putting campaign signs below Flock cameras scattered around Prescott. “It was a risk, it still is a risk. But it’s a big issue,” he said. Jones says he’s never run for office before, but came up with the idea to put up signs calling out Flock cameras after putting up his own campaign signs and understanding the law behind it. The signs read “Flock Cam Here” and “Private AI Cams Tracking YOU”. Jones says he’s placed about 70 signs across the city, and in the process has gone through two tanks of gas and spent hundreds to place and maintain the signs. In Arizona, political signs can be placed in a public right-of-way with some restrictions. The disclaimer at the bottom of the Flock signs including Jones’ campaign information is enough for the signs to be legal. Arizona’s Family spent time with him on Thursday as he replaced signs that had been displaced or had to be changed because they had streamers attached to them. During that time, multiple people honked their horns in support, some screaming out of their windows at red lights in agreement with the signs. Jones says his opposition to Flock cameras doesn’t have anything to do with the work of the Prescott Police Department, instead the broader surveillance concerns. Concerns around ALPR cameras are one of the few issues that span both political parties . On Thursday, Tempe city council voted to permanently ban the city from ever using ALPR technology. “It’s not so much just that the city is using them. It’s the fact that they’re connected across the whole nation,” he explained. “A police officer who I don’t know, in Nebraska let’s say, can look up my license plate and see where I’ve been for 7 to 30 days,” Under Arizona law, campaign signs must be taken down no later than 15 days after the general election.
