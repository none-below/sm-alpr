Report: Former Goodyear officer searched plate of crime victim 36 times

Internal affairs report says Kristopher Jackson used Flock system after a call for service to look up victim’s license plate dozens of times
By Zach Prelutsky
Published: Nov. 10, 2026 at 5:20 AM UTC|Updated: 12 hours ago
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

GOODYEAR, AZ (AZFamily) — An internal affairs investigation report is shedding more light on the reason an officer resigned from the Goodyear Police Department.

Last month, Goodyear police announced that Kristopher Jackson had quit the force amid an investigation into misuse of the department’s Flock camera system.

The report obtained by Arizona’s Family reveals that Jackson used the system to search the license plate of a victim he met during a call for service.

“That’s not right at all. Yeah, he should be penalized for that. Punished, or whatever the case may be,” said Lindsay, who lives in Goodyear.

On Jan. 4, Jackson responded to a call for service from a woman who claimed her ex-boyfriend went into her home, took her cat and was threatening to send partially nude photos of her to her work.

The internal affairs report says Jackson sat with the woman, answering questions about protective orders and talking through the situation.

She told him she didn’t want a report taken that day because she thought the cat might be returned, and asked if she could call him if it wasn’t back by Jan. 8.

About 23 minutes into the call, Jackson can be heard clearing his throat, then reaching up and turning off his body-worn camera, according to the report.

The woman was still talking about calls and messages from her ex-boyfriend at the time. Jackson didn’t clear the call until 40 minutes later, and he never wrote a report.

The report says Jackson also ran the woman’s name through ACJIS, the state’s criminal justice database, five times on the day of the call and once more on Jan. 10.

“The police department found not only that this officer inappropriately accessed a database and then used it for personal reasons,” said police practices expert Michael Gennaco. “But that he also lied during the investigation.”

Thirty-six times between Jan. 4 and Feb. 8, Jackson searched the license plate belonging to the victim, including 14 times in one day. Some of those searches happened outside of Jackson’s normal working hours. The report says Jackson also looked up the plate of the ex-boyfriend a few times.

The department’s internal affairs unit learned of the problem in July, when an internal affairs officer ran a Flock audit that flagged Jackson’s searches.

“When you access the database, it’s fairly easy to be able to figure out what credentials, what username, what password was used to get into the database,” said Gennaco, who also works as an independent auditor for Flock systems.

Glendale police investigated whether Jackson had illegally accessed criminal history records. No charges were filed.

In an interview with a Glendale detective, Jackson said he checked her plate so often to make sure she was safe. He said he called her and went to her home to check on her. A forensic exam of his city-issued phone found no calls between the two before Jan. 31, when she left him a voicemail saying her cat had been killed. The report also notes he gave two different reasons for running her name in ACJIS.

Jackson resigned from Goodyear police before the internal affairs investigation was complete.

In October, Goodyear police announced the internal investigation was complete. Chief Brian Issitt said that if Jackson hadn’t quit, he would have been fired for multiple policy violations: abuse of authority, violation of body-worn camera policy and dishonesty.

This is not the only instance Arizona’s Family has covered of law enforcement misusing automated license plate reader (ALPR) systems. In August, an Apache Junction officer resigned after misusing Flock cameras to track his wife.

The Institute for Justice has found dozens of instances of officers across the country misusing automated license plate reader systems to track women.

“For somebody to use that, that way, in their own personal, whatever, is just wrong in every sense of the word,” said Lindsay.
