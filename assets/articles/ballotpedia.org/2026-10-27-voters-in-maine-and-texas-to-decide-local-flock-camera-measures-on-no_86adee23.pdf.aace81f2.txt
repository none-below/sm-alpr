Voters in Maine and Texas to decide local
Flock camera measures on Nov. 3

On Nov. 3, voters will decide two local ballot measures related to
automated license plate readers (ALPRs). These measures will be
decided in York, Maine, and League City, Texas.
ALPRs, sometimes referred to as Flock cameras, are surveillance
cameras made by Flock Safety that photograph passing vehicles
and record license plate numbers and vehicle characteristics. The
systems also record the date, time, and location of the vehicle
sighting. Law enforcement agencies can search the information
collected by these cameras to identify vehicles during
investigations. The systems can also generate alerts when a
vehicle matches an entry in a law enforcement database or other
designated list.
The measure in York, Maine, is a citizen initiative, and made the
ballot after over 100 residents signed the petition. If voters pass the
measure, ALPRs would be banned in the town, and voter approval
would be required for any future installations of surveillance
technology.
Lt. Nick Piskopanis, a police officer in York, previously said that
ALPRs were beneficial, saying, “For us, it's been fantastic. We've
solved multiple crimes, crimes that we wouldn't have normally been
able to solve.”
At a public hearing in September, York residents expressed their
views on the use of ALPRs. York Town Manager Peter Joseph said,

--- page 4 ---
“People kind of acknowledge that if you go into a business, whether
it's a public governmental building or another business in town,
they have security cameras up for their own purpose. I think people
expect that in today's society. Watching the roadway and logging
every time a car goes by is what really got people concerned in this
instance.”
Marla Johnson, vice chair of the York Select Board, said, “If I want
to get on the Turnpike and go down to Home Depot or Target, the
idea that my plate is being scanned for no reason is bothersome.”
Currently, Flock cameras in York will remain covered with plastic
until voters decide on the measure on Nov. 3.
Meanwhile, in League City, Texas, voters will decide on an advisory
question, Proposition A, related to ALPRs. Voting ‘yes’ on the
measure would approve the use of ALPRs at fixed locations on
public roadways for law enforcement purposes, while a ‘no’ vote
would oppose the use of ALPRs.
Unlike the York measure, an advisory question is non-binding,
meaning the outcome of the measure has no legal effect on local
statute. However, League City council members unanimously
approved a resolution on Oct. 13 that would require the removal of
ALPRs within 72 hours of the official canvass of election results if
voters reject the advisory question.
League City currently manages 55 Flock cameras, while also
having access to 23 cameras managed by homeowners'
associations and six managed by private businesses. According to
the League City police department, Flock camera footage is
retained for 30 days unless preserved as evidence in a criminal or
administrative investigation, and police department personnel must
be trained and authorized before using the system.
League City Council candidate Seth Rowlands, running for Position
3, supported the use of the ALPR cameras, saying, “Yes, it does
help us catch criminals faster, but it also lets us catch criminals
period, because there are several cases where the only shred of
evidence there was was a hit on a Flock camera.”
Teresa Potter, another candidate for the League City Council,
opposed Flock cameras, saying, “I do not support Flock cameras. I
