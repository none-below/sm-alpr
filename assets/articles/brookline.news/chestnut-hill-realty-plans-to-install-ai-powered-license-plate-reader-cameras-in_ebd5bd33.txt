Photo courtesy Flock Safety Brookline police, like every other modern law enforcement agency, frequently use surveillance cameras owned by private citizens or businesses to help investigate crimes or track suspected criminals. But a new arrangement under which a major real estate company in town is installing its own license plate readers has raised some eyebrows. Chestnut Hill Realty is planning to install license plate readers made by Flock Safety, a rapidly-growing AI camera startup, on Independence Drive near Hancock Village, its South Brookline property on the Boston border. The cameras are intended to act as a “deterrent to help prevent crime in the community,” according to Jennifer Murphy, Chestnut Hill Realty’s chief operating officer. The Brookline Police Department will sign a memorandum of understanding with Flock to use the data, and Murphy said the company plans to make the data available to Boston police as well. Jennifer Paster, Brookline chief of police, said at a meeting last week that the cameras are a “no-brainer” from a law enforcement perspective. “I am really grateful to Chestnut Hill Realty for being proactive and good partners in public safety,” Paster said. Flock, which recently opened an office in Boston and whose data has been used by dozens of Massachusetts police departments, has more than 80,000 AI-powered cameras in use across the country. It started by focusing its business on home owners associations and has expanded to selling cameras to police departments and local governments. Its CEO has offered a bold vision: ending all crime in the U.S. But some privacy and civil rights advocates say that ambition comes with costs . Kade Crockford, director of the Technology for Liberty Program at the ACLU of Massachusetts, has raised concerns about the cameras. In an interview, Crockford said that the more routine uses of Flock systems, like identifying stolen cars or vehicles subject to Amber Alerts, are generally not an issue. What is problematic, Crockford said, is that the growing use of license plate readers, and data shared between law enforcement agencies, essentially creates a mass database of movement of a large number of people. “Unregulated, this technology facilitates the mass tracking of every single person’s movements on the road,” Crockford said. “Oftentimes these technologies are billed as a public safety tool, but they can pose security harms to vulnerable groups,” said Crockford, noting other states have laws requiring local police to engage in civil immigration enforcement. 404 Media reported in May that U.S. Immigrations and Custom Enforcement (ICE) has sourced data from Flock’s cameras using “side door” access from local law enforcement agencies. Chestnut Hill Realty, as the customer, owns the data collected by the readers and can share it with whomever the company wishes, according to Flock. Murphy said the company respects the concerns around privacy and civil rights. She cited a saying: “Locks keep honest people honest.” “This feels like a modern way to accomplish that.” She said that Chestnut Hill Realty is considering using the cameras in other towns where it has properties. How will Brookline police use the cameras? Brookline police will have access to a dashboard that will save license plate information from cars which have passed by the cameras for 30 days. The department can also get alerts when wanted vehicles, for example those flagged by the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, pass by. The cameras will “enhance the abilities of our officers on the street,” Paster said, allowing officers to “go up in a matter of minutes and engage with the car” when the license plates of wanted suspects’ vehicles appear. Because the cameras will be purchased by Chestnut Hill Realty, there is no cost to the town to use the data. Kristen MacLeod, a Flock representative, noted that searching the system requires a unique user ID for the officer using it, and searches must be logged along with a reason. “So any query of the system for historical information is all logged and auditable,” she said. The cameras do not use facial recognition, technology which was banned for use by Brookline police by a Town Meeting vote in 2019. The Police Commissioner’s Advisory Committee voted 5-0 to sign off on the department’s MOU with Flock, but not before member C. Scott Ananian raised concerns. “I am very concerned that someone driving by Chestnut Hill Realty properties … could get tagged, not see the sign and that we could be facilitating abuses which I don’t think we would countenance in our town,” Ananian said. “I’m concerned about it, I’d like to make sure there are guardrails in place.” Paster agreed to work on developing a policy for use of the data while the department is implementing the system.
