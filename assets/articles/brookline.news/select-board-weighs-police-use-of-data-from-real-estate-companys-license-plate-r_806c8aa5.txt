Photo courtesy Flock Safety Controversial license plate readers made by the company Flock Safety are still being installed by Chestnut Hill Realty in South Brookline, but whether the town’s police department will be able to use a stream of real-time information from them remains up in the air. At a meeting on Tuesday, the Select Board, Brookline’s elected five-member executive branch, discussed but did not take a vote on whether to approve a memorandum of understanding (MOU) between the police department and the real estate company. The conversation was punctuated by more than a dozen public commenters who all opposed the memorandum. Brookline . News reported earlier this month that Chestnut Hill Realty was planning to purchase cameras from Flock Safety and share data with Brookline police as a “deterrent to help prevent crime in the community,” according to Jennifer Murphy the real estate company’s chief operating officer. Since then, a wave of opposition from local elected officials, residents and the American Civil Liberties Union has complicated an agreement that Police Chief Jennifer Paster called a “no-brainer” from a law enforcement perspective. The opposition has focused on the ethical and legal track record of Flock, the possibility that federal immigration authorities could get data from Brookline and general concern about mass surveillance. As recently as this summer, Flock was working directly with U.S. Immigrations and Custom Enforcement, a representative acknowledged on Tuesday. Despite that vocal opposition, one thing was clear on Tuesday: Elected officials and town staffers said that the Select Board does not have the ability to stop Chestnut Hill Realty from installing license plate readers or sharing data from them with any law enforcement agency in the country. In a statement , Murphy of Chestnut Hill Realty told Brookline . News that the data would only be shared with local law enforcement agencies, specifically the Brookline and Boston police departments. “This initiative reflects our ongoing commitment to community safety and collaboration with local officials, while maintaining strong privacy protections for all residents,” Murphy said. Town Meeting, Brookline’s legislative branch, might have the power to ban private companies from installing license plate readers, according to Town Administrator Charles Carey. Several Town Meeting members have already discussed proposing legislation to prevent the technology from being used. However, Carey said at Tuesday’s meeting that a local bylaw governing private use of the cameras might not hold up to legal scrutiny. Should police sign memorandum of understanding? In the absence of the immediate ability to regulate Chestnut Hill Realty’s installation of the cameras, the Select Board instead is taking on the question of how Brookline police should use the data from them. The police department and Flock have proposed an agreement that Chief Paster said would let the department get real time alerts when a car with a license plate on the department’s “hot list” passes by the camera. It could help police track down open warrants, stolen vehicles or cars that might be the subject of an Amber Alert for a child abduction, Paster said. She used a recent example of mail thefts at a mailbox near Putterham Circle as the kind of crime where real-time access to the data could aid police responses. The department has also drafted a policy that would govern how it uses the data from the cameras. Select Board members appeared divided on whether to approve the MOU. “Chestnut Hill Realty’s going to do this. They’re going to put this system in,” said member Paul Warren, calling for support for the police chief’s request to gain access to the data captured. Board member John VanScoyoc also signalled support for using the data, and said it would be “irresponsible” for the police department not to take advantage of information made available to it. He also called for the development of a policy that strongly protects civil liberties. The other three board members, Bernard Greene, David Pearlman and Michael Rubenstein, all expressed concerns about the technology. “We live in a scary time, and this is a scary technology that can be accessed by scary people,” said board chair Bernard Greene, calling it “invasive surveillance.” Rubinstein said that the board needs to ask “whether we as a town want to participate in the business model that Flock is establishing.” And Pearlman noted that, while Flock claims Chestnut Hill Realty would own the data produced by the camera, a line in the proposed MOU states that Flock “retains all right, title and interest” to the data provided and can use it “for any purpose.” “The way the MOU language is articulated does give me a lot of pause,” Pearlman said. Public feedback A number of Town Meeting members and other residents spoke at the meeting to oppose an MOU between the police department and Flock. “The town of Brookline should not do business with this company,” said Town Meeting member Amy Newell. She and others pointed to the company’s actions in Evanston, Ill., where it reportedly reinstalled cameras without the permission of local authorities who had removed them after finding out about Flock’s cooperation with federal immigration authorities. Closer to home, the city of Cambridge recently asked Flock to turn off cameras installed there while local officials investigate the company’s practices. “We should sign no agreements with Flock. We should not be aiding and abetting the expansion of a nationwide surveillance network by what is obviously a bad actor,” Newell said. Resident David Butcher said that the town “should not engage in using AI surveillance services provided as a gift by a private real estate company.” Town Meeting member Alec Lebovitz quoted from a letter by an ACLU Massachusetts lawyer opposing the MOU: The agreement would “give Flock unlimited authority to use data collected from Brookline for any purpose whatever without oversight or restriction,” wrote attorney Gideon Epstein. Massachusetts Senate Majority Leader Cindy Creem, who represents Brookline, has previously proposed legislation which would set limits on the uses of license plate readers. “I first filed legislation over a decade ago to regulate automatic license-plate readers because, while these tools can support public-safety efforts, they also carry significant risks to civil liberties and privacy,” Creem said in a statement to Brookline . News. “As Brookline considers new AI-powered systems, it’s essential that we also prioritize protecting sensitive location data and ensuring strong safeguards for immigrant and other historically over-surveilled populations.” What’s next? The board is planning to reconsider the MOU at a future meeting after digesting the input from Tuesday’s meeting. Members said they hope Chestnut Hill Realty will send a representative next time the issue comes under consideration. Board Chair Greene made his feelings clear, referring to a quote by Flock’s CEO which stated that the company’s goal was ending all crime in the United States. “Think about that.
