Credit: Doug Hardy / CTNewsJunkie In the wake of a new law protecting the privacy of Connecticut residents , a group of legislators are seeking answers from two large retailers on their use of automated license plate readers (ALPR) and the data they collect. The readers photograph a vehicle’s license plate and store the images for a set amount of time, usually between 30-90 days. Senate and House Democratic leaders sent a letter Tuesday to the chief executives of Lowe’s Companies and The Home Depot, pressing both retailers to disclose how they collect, store and share ALPR data at their Connecticut store locations, after news reports have highlighted the parking lot cameras. The letter – signed by nine Democrats – is addressed to Lowe’s President and CEO Marvin R. Ellison and Home Depot President and CEO Edward Decker. It poses eight questions, including what policies govern data retention and sharing, whether judicial warrants are required before sharing data with law enforcement and whether out-of-state law enforcement agencies can access the data. Gov. Ned Lamont on May 4 signed Senate Bill 397 into law. The bill regulates state and local use of ALPRs and retention of that data, and Attorney General William Tong has said he will “vigorously defend” this law . The new law prohibits ALPRs from being used for immigration enforcement purposes, but focuses on municipal and state police use of the cameras rather than private companies. “The impetus behind these statutory guardrails on state and local use of ALPRs is protecting the privacy of our residents,” the legislators wrote in their letter. “ALPRs, while a useful tool for security and theft prevention, have the potential to be misused to track Connecticut residents, raising concerns not only about privacy in general, but also concerns about bad actors using ALPR data to enable stalking and harassment.” Privacy advocates, including staff from the American Civil Liberties Union, have expressed concerns over the use of APLRs in Connecticut because they can put residents at risk, especially immigrants, LGBTQ+ people, and those seeking reproductive or gender-affirming care out of state. Home Depot said it has had parking area security cameras in place at its stores for many years, just like many other retailers. “These cameras are used solely as a security measure to prevent theft and protect the safety of our customers and associates in our stores,” a Home Depot spokesperson said Tuesday. “We do not grant access to our license plate readers to federal law enforcement.” According to the company’s privacy policy, the APLR data can be accessed by “select associates and independent contractors with an Authorized Purpose,” which includes staff in security, asset protection, IT and legal departments. Lowe’s details in its privacy policy that it uses ALPRs in some store parking areas “where allowed by law,” for “limited, authorized purposes.” “Personal Information is collected through ALPRs to help ensure security, prevent theft and fraud, assist with parking enforcement, and to help maintain your safety and the safety of individuals and our properties,” the Lowe’s privacy policy states. The company says it will share the data if required by law or a legal process, “or when we believe doing so will help protect the safety, property, or rights of individuals or Lowe’s.” A company spokesperson did not immediately respond to a request for comment via an online contact form Tuesday afternoon. The lawmakers said they are concerned about whether the retailers’ private use of the technology adequately protects Connecticut residents. The letter was signed by Senate President Pro Tempore Martin Looney of New Haven, Senate Majority Leader Bob Duff of Norwalk, House Majority Leader Jason Rojas of East Hartford, state Sen. Gary Winfield of New Haven, state Sen. James Maroney of Milford, state Sen. Mae Flexer of Killingly, state Rep. Roland Lemar of New Haven, state Rep. Steven Stafstrom of Bridgeport and state Rep. Matthew Blumenthal of Stamford.
