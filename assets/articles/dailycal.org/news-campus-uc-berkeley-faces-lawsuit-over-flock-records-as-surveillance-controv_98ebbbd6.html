UC Berkeley faces lawsuit over Flock records as surveillance controversy grows

  • Juliette Damian | Staff

    Juliette Damian

Resistance to UC Berkeley’s employment of controversial surveillance technology company Flock Security is intensifying as campus faces growing criticism and a lawsuit over records related to its data-sharing practices.

The UC Board of Regents is facing a lawsuit over UC Berkeley’s alleged failure to disclose certain public records relating to UCPD’s automated license plate reader system.

At the same time, student organizers have begun calling for campus to DeFlock, or end its contract with Flock.

The petition was filed Friday, Aug. 28 in the Superior Court of Alameda County by Daniel Negrete, director of The Ellis Collective, a student-led research group under the Students’ Civil Liberties Union.

The Ellis Collective previously revealed that the UC system shared data collected by ALPRs at multiple campuses with U.S. Customs and Border Protection and other federal agencies, in apparent violation of state law.

In California, sharing data collected by ALPR systems with out-of-state agencies is illegal under Senate Bill 34.

The suit follows an alleged failure by campus to provide records for a Feb. 17 public records request filed by Negrete seeking information about campus’s ALPR system.

Specifically, Negrete requested audit logs reflecting searches of UCPD’s ALPR system by both UCPD and outside agencies, records that identified agencies and networks with which UCPD shares or receives ALPR data and records that show UCPD’s ALPR sharing settings.

“Our review has just begun, but two things are clear: that the use of UC Berkeley’s ALPR system has been extensive, and the University did not want anyone to see the records,” Negrete alleged in a text message.

Campus spokesperson Dan Mogulof said UC Berkeley had received the suit. He said in an email that campus does not generally comment on litigation but that “the records have been released.”

Five days after Negrete filed his lawsuit, students and community members gathered Wednesday evening at Hearst Field Annex to raise awareness about ALPR surveillance and data sharing.

The event’s organizers described it as an opportunity to grow the DeFlock movement, a growing national movement in resistance to the technology offered by Flock and other surveillance companies.

“Our goal is not just to cut the university contract with Flock but also to win guarantees against data sharing with outside agencies for any ALPR cameras and to stop the university from accepting new ALPR contracts without community input and agreement,” said campus student and event host Catherine Boland in her presentation Wednesday.

During the event, Boland noted that the city of Berkeley maintains 52 Flock cameras, with UC Berkeley maintaining another six Flock ALPR cameras at student housing in Albany and Emeryville under a two-year pilot project.

Boland argued that cutting campus’s Flock contract would prevent sensitive data from being shared with Immigration and Customs Enforcement or other immigration enforcement agencies.

A December 2025 report from the Northern California Regional Intelligence Center, a regional law enforcement intelligence agency, shows an agreement with UC Berkeley to share ALPR data.

The report also shows the intelligence center sharing its data with a number of state agencies that have reportedly shared data with out-of-state or federal agencies in the past.

Mogulof told The Daily Californian in May that UC Berkeley does not permit sharing of ALPR data for immigration enforcement purposes and that it does not share data with federal agencies.

According to Mogulof, campus’s Flock system has been used in more than 100 investigations to generate leads, identify suspects and make arrests. He also noted that Flock cameras have been “instrumental” in closing several “significant” felony investigations.

According to Negrete, a separate lawsuit he filed over UC Riverside’s alleged failure to furnish similar public records remains ongoing.

“Addressing Berkeley’s use of Flock requires a lot more than this single suit,” Negrete wrote. “We will continue to litigate until the remaining records are unredacted and the court declares that the University violated the California Public Records Act. But meaningful efforts require us to confront these practices, and others, at scale.”
