By Alasdair Whitney and Tom Solomon Ever get the feeling you’re being watched? Maybe it’s because you are. No, it’s not paranoia, nor is it the stuff of dystopian fiction. Automatic license plate reader (ALPR) technology has been deployed in cities across Colorado and the nation, actively monitoring the driving habits of every motorist that passes by, feeding sensitive data into networks with nationwide reach where it is stored for years. Right now, information about where, when and with whom you drive is likely sitting in a government database, searchable by law enforcement across the country, and accessible entirely without a warrant. Recently, a bipartisan bill to introduce commonsense statewide guardrails for ALPRs met its end , leaving Coloradans exposed to the continued potential misuse and abuse of this technology. It’s time for communities to step in where the General Assembly was unsuccessful and pass regulations to protect the rights of residents to be free from warrantless mass surveillance. Colorado Senate Bill 26-070 , sponsored by Sen. Judy Amabile (D-Boulder) and Lynda Zamora Wilson (R-El Paso), would have required government agencies to obtain a warrant to access ALPR data that was more than 72 hours old, restricted ALPR data sharing outside of Colorado, and limited how long it could be retained to 30 days. It did not ban ALPRs, nor did it prevent law enforcement from accessing data in emergency situations . In other words, it simply asked agencies to do what they already are required to do for almost every other form of intrusive, tech-powered surveillance. Despite receiving enthusiastic support from across the political spectrum and across Colorado, it ultimately could not garner enough votes to pass the Senate. But this is not the end of the road for ALPR reform. While the bill was making its way through the General Assembly, communities around Colorado were engaged in robust debates about the role of this technology in their cities, towns and counties. Following the recent controversy over Denver’s ALPR contracts, calls came from members of City Council for an ordinance to create safeguards. Local jurisdictions like Denver, or any other municipality in Colorado, need not wait for the state to act. Where possible, policymakers should implement local laws that set reasonable, commonsense safeguards to ensure residents are protected . Stories of the abuse and misuse of ALPR technology in Colorado have splashed across headlines in recent months. Last year, a woman was accused of stealing a package from a porch in Bow Mar because her green truck, which was picked up by the city’s ALPR system, had been erroneously associated with the crime. Earlier this year, a similarly dystopian nightmare befell a man who was twice pulled over by Cherry Hills Village Police because an ALPR system flagged his truck for an active warrant . The warrant wasn’t for him or his truck but was likely connected accidentally within the ALPR system by another agency. And just last month, another story broke identifying two other Colorado residents who had been pulled over in different jurisdictions due to similar errors, including a 76-year-old grandmother. In 2024, Aurora paid a $1.9 million settlement after a family with a young child was pulled over at gunpoint during a stop that reportedly involved ALPR technology when their Colorado plates were associated with a stolen motorcycle from Montana. Sensitive ALPR data can also be easily abused. Research by our organization, the Institute for Justice, has identified at least 14 instances across the country of government agents using ALPR technology to stalk romantic interests. During her speech on the Senate floor, Sen. Zamora Wilson noted the national security risks should ALPR data showing movements of service members, many of whom reside in her district, be exposed. As Sen. Amabile noted in her statement, the reform bill’s failure to advance doesn’t mean local communities should wait to push for these vital reforms. It is up to local policymakers to protect residents from abuses and misuses like these. The time to act is now. Alasdair Whitney (Legislative Counsel) and Tom Solomon (Special Projects Coordinator) work at the Institute for Justice, a national nonprofit protecting Americans from government abuse.
