Area police share license plate reader data for immigration,
unstated reasons, records show
Cornelius Frolik - Staff Writer
May 20, 2026
Law enforcement agencies across the state and country routinely access local police
departments’ automated license plate reader data for immigration and often unstated
purposes, a Dayton Daily News investigation found.
The Dayton Daily News requested ALPR audit records from many local agencies and
received data from police departments in Kettering, Miami Twp. and Tipp City. Miamisburg
and Springboro police also provided limited information.
The records show that law enforcement agencies across Ohio and the United States
conduct thousands of searches of local police data every single day. In January alone,
hundreds of local searches listed “immigration” as a search reason.
Far more often, agencies don’t make it clear why they want local ALPR data, which is
collected by cameras posted on busy roadways that capture images of license plates and
other visual information of passing cars.

The city of Dayton recently suspended its use of fixed-point automatic license plate readers after
disclosing camera data was being accessed by outside agencies for immigration-related searches.
We obtained data from other area police departments to reveal how ALPR data is shared, and for what
reasons.

Law enforcement officers are supposed to state a reason for every search of jurisdictions’
ALPR databases, but network audit records reveal that the reasons provided can be vague
or indecipherable. Some search reasons just include the words “investigation,”
“investigation pending,” “other,” “other law enforcement purpose” or a series of numbers,
sometimes apparently tied to police codes or incident reports.
“It can be whatever you want — you just type it in — there’s literally no safeguard,” said
Melissa Bertolo, a Dayton resident who is a founding member of the Coalition for Public
Protection, a local community advocacy group that pushes for transparency and
accountability for police tools and practices.

Use of surveillance tools for immigration enforcement is divisive. Many people were
outraged to recently learn that the Dayton Police Department allowed other police
agencies to scan its database thousands of times for immigration purposes, violating
policy and breaking promises that police leaders made to the community.

The Dayton Police Department, the Montgomery County Sheriff’s Office and other regional
police departments denied Dayton Daily News requests, made under Ohio public records
law, that seek to obtain audit information showing how those agencies are sharing Flock
ALPR data. Officials say the records are confidential and exempt from public records law.
This news outlet is currently disputing those assertions.
ALPRs are used by law enforcement agencies across southwest Ohio, including Springfield,
Clark County, Hamilton, Middletown, West Chester Twp. and Butler County. This news outlet
is seeking audit data from these agencies.

--- page 4 ---
Kettering changes practice
On May 4, Kettering police changed their practice “so that an agency can no longer access
Kettering’s cameras just by stating it’s for immigration purposes,” according to police Chief
Chip Protsman.
May 4 is the same day the Dayton Daily News emailed questions to the agency noting how
often Kettering’s data was being accessed for immigration investigations and asking if the
agency was OK with outside agencies using ALPR data for immigration purposes.
Kettering Mayor Bryan Suddith declined to answer questions from the Dayton Daily News,
providing only a statement saying: “I am confident that our policy aligns with our
residents’ desire for a safe and secure Kettering while simultaneously protecting their
privacy.”
Other local officials say their ALPR technology is being used appropriately, in accordance
with department policy and Ohio law, and that they have taken steps to ensure proper
oversight and protect people’s privacy.
“The police department maintains policies governing system access and use, conducts
regular audits and reviews system activity to help ensure compliance with applicable laws
and departmental standards,” said Tipp City Manager Eric Mack.
“We understand the public concerns surrounding privacy, data access and accountability,
and we rwemain committed to using this technology responsibly, transparently and in a
manner consistent with both public safety needs and community expectations.”
Tipp City’s policy on ALPR data says it’s accessible “for legitimate law enforcement
purposes only.”
Immigration searches by local agencies
Kettering’s data has been accessed for immigration-related reasons by a variety of local
and out-of-state agencies.
Of the 184 times immigration was listed as a reason for accessing Kettering’s ALPR data in
January, 23 searches were done by the Sharonville Police Department, 19 came from the
Greene County Sheriff’s Office, 10 from the Ohio Bureau of Motor Vehicles, three from the
Montgomery County Sheriff’s Office and two from the Miamisburg Police Department,

according to network audit records. Dozens of other immigration searches were conducted
by the Grant County, Ind., Sheriff’s Office, who included the word “test” as part of the
stated search purpose.
Immigration was the listed reason for another 53 searches in audit data from March 8 to
April 7.
The Greene and Montgomery county sheriffs’ offices listed the reason for some of their
searches of Kettering’s database as “Immigration (civil/administrative).” Neither agency
has an agreement with ICE that allows them to enforce immigration laws.
In an interview last month, Greene County Sheriff Scott Anger said his office does not have
a formal agreement with ICE but staff work cooperatively with federal agents.

--- page 6 ---
“Sometimes I think that by not cooperating with the federal people you’re making it more
unsafe ... because you have people operating independently within your counties,” said
Anger, who did not respond to requests for comment for this story. “We know our county
and we know how to help them take care of what they need to do. That’s always our goal is
to make sure everybody stays safe and that we work together.”
Sharonville police say Flock is a useful tool in solving crimes. They say Sharonville doesn’t
enforce immigration laws and the searches listed in Kettering’s data were traced back to
an officer who “mistakenly selected ‘immigration’ from the “case type” drop down in a
hurried effort from an in-car computer to get information on a vehicle that he was hoping
to locate.”
Miamisburg police Chief Mike Brem said he could not definitively identify the two queries
about immigration that showed up in Kettering police’s network audit based on details
provided by the Dayton Daily News. But he said they might have been related to an
investigation involving two people who were in the country unlawfully who were arrested
in connection to allegations of sexual assault against juvenile family members.
“Following those arrests, detectives were coordinating formal criminal charges and
investigative efforts prior to the possibility of deportation proceedings occurring,” he said.

Out of state searches
Between early March and early April, dozens of law enforcement agencies performed about
475 searches of the Tipp City Police Department’s ALPR database explicitly for immigration-
related reasons, according to network audit records obtained by the Dayton Daily News.
Tipp City police allow thousands of police departments, sheriffs’ offices and other agencies
around the state and nation to access its ALPR data. About 95% of the immigration-related
searches were conducted by out-of-state law enforcement, including police in Texas,
Florida, Alabama, Indiana and Louisiana.

--- page 8 ---
Flock, the company that makes ALPR devices and provides support to users, says U.S.
Immigration and Customs Enforcement and the Department of Homeland Security do not
have direct access to its customers’ data, systems and cameras. However, the company
acknowledges that there is nothing stopping its customers from working with ICE and DHS
and sharing data with feeral authorities.
The Dayton Daily News determined that at least 40% of the agencies that performed
immigration-associated searches of the Tipp City Police Department’s database have
cooperation agreements with ICE that authorize their staff to participate in immigration-
enforcement activities.
This includes the Jefferson County, Ala., Sheriff’s Office, which accessed Tipp City’s data 47
times in a month, listing the reason as “Immigration (civil/administrative).” The Florida Fish
and Wildlife Conservation Commission queried the city’s data 25 times, with the reason
usually listed as “Immigration (criminal) — I.C.E.”
Mack, city manager for Tipp City, said the city takes privacy, transparency, accountability
and the lawful use of technology very seriously. He said ALPR data is collected and used in
compliance with departmental policy, Ohio law and Flock system contractual
requirements.
Flock cameras have helped police make more than 100 arrests for serious criminal
offenses, Mack said. He said the police department performs ALPR audits quarterly to
ensure compliance with department standards and the law. He said immigration searches
of Tipp City’s system were done by external agencies through broader law enforcement
information-sharing networks and were not initiated, directed or accessed by Tipp City
police personnel.
“The purpose of this technology is to assist with legitimate law enforcement functions,
including locating stolen vehicles, identifying suspect vehicles connected to criminal
investigations, locating missing persons and supporting public safety efforts involving
multijurisdictional criminal activity,” Mack said. “In Tipp City, this technology is not used
for generalized surveillance or civil immigration enforcement activities.”
Kettering officials say ALPRs are a vital tool that help police identify and apprehend
suspects in criminal cases that otherwise would go unsolved.

‘Other’, vague reasons
Outside agencies completed more than 120 immigration-related searches of the Miami
Twp. Police Department’s ALPR database in January. There were also about 278 immigration
searches in February and 135 searches in March. All of the agencies doing the searching
were out of state. The Miami Twp. Police Department shares its ALPR data with more than
2,600 organizations.
Records obtained by this news outlet also show that the Miamisburg Police Department
shares its ALPR data with more than 2,000 agencies from all over the country. The
Springboro Police Department shares its ALPR data with more than 225 Ohio-based
agencies.
Miamisburg and Springboro police denied this news outlet’s requests for their network
and organizational audit log records.
Immigration represents a small share of the reasons agencies access local Flock data.
In January, more than 400 agencies accessed Kettering’s data more than 240,000 times for
thousands of different listed reasons. The most common reasons by far were for auto
thefts, robberies, drug investigations, wanted persons and traffic infractions.
In March, outside agencies completed more than 1 million total searches of Tipp City
police’s ALRP database, while authorities with outside departments executed more than
370,000 searches of Miami Twp. police’s database.
However, audit logs show there are many searches where the reasons provided are vague.
The reason given for more than 1,300 searches of Kettering’s ALPR database between early
March and early April was “other — investigation.” Additionally, during the same time
frame, the Columbus Police Department searched Kettering police’s database more than
1,200 times for the stated reason of “other law enforcement purpose.”
More than 3,200 searches of Kettering’s database in January were for the stated reasons of
“investigation,” “information,” “other — investigation,” “other law enforcement purpose,”
“suspect” and “other suspect.”

--- page 10 ---
From early March to early April, the Coweta County Sheriff’s Office in Georgia searched
Miami Twp. police’s ALPR database roughly 2,000 times for stated reasons of “other —
investigation” and “other — info”.” The sheriff’s office has an 287(g) agreement with ICE that
allows its officers to perform certain immigration enforcement functions.
A variety of agencies that have cooperation agreements with ICE have accessed Miami
Twp.’s Flock ALPR network for unclear reasons such as “other investigation,” “other —
resource investigation,” “other — ongoing criminal investigation.”
The Coweta County Sheriff’s Office also searched Tipp City’s ALPR database more than
1,900 times from early March to early April for stated reasons including “other — info,”
“other — intel” and “other — investigation.”
‘It’s far from clear’
Miamisburg police shares its Flock network data with police departments that have
cooperation agreements with ICE, including the Coweta County Sheriff’s Office. Springboro
provides network access to a couple of Ohio police agencies that have ICE agreements.
Miamisburg police Chief Brem said technology used by law enforcement should include
accountability, policy guidance, auditing capability and supervisory oversight.
“We are confident the system is being used by the Miamisburg Police Department
appropriately and for legitimate law enforcement purposes,” Brem said. “Access to the
system is controlled, user activity is logged within the platform and officers are expected
to comply with department procedures and applicable laws.”
Jay Stanley, senior policy analyst and privacy expert with the national ACLU, said audit logs
from police departments across the country reveal that the Flock customers share data
with scores of outside groups without real transparency and accountability.

--- page 11 ---
“We see so many fields that say ‘investigation’ or other vague terms or sometimes things
like, ‘Ha-ha-ha,’” he said. “Clearly there is no effective managerial oversight going on when
you see that kind of stuff.”
Stanley said law enforcement officers who want to perform searches for immigration-
related or other controversial purposes have a strong disincentive not to provide the real
reasons for their searches, since those potentially could result in scrutiny, criticism and
pushback.
“The audit logs have proven valuable in revealing the extent to which local data is very,
very hard to contain once it’s collected on residents’ patterns of life, but its utility as a
check and balance on this technology is slim to nonexistent,” Stanley said.
