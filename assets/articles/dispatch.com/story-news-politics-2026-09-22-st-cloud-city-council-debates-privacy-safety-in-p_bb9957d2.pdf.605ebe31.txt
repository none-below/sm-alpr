Flock ALPR debate focuses on privacy
and safety concerns in St. Cloud
Sept. 22, 2026, 11:05 a.m. ET
Key Points
The St. Cloud city council and police discussed automated license plate readers and privacy in a work session Sept. 21.
St. Cloud police have five Flock ALPRs and five Axon ALPRs installed as well as mobile Axon ALPRs on more than 35
vehicles.
While the police chief defended the use of the readers to find criminal activity, members of the public said trust in the
police department has diminished since the cameras came into use.
After St. Cloud police gave a presentation to city council on its use of automated license
plate readers on Monday, council members discussed concerns about balancing law
enforcement work with personal privacy.
“Some people would prefer more public safety and are willing to give up some personal
privacy,” council member Mark Johnson said. “Others are the reverse of that.”
The technology has been used in St. Cloud since 2015, Police Chief Jeff Oxton said. It
automatically scans license plates and pings law enforcement if one is connected to any
criminal activity, which can range from human trafficking cases to vehicles registered to
drivers with revoked or suspended licenses. 
The session came after Sherburne County decided to remove its Flock Safety ALPRs in
August and an 18-year-old Sauk Rapids man was charged with cutting down a St. Cloud
camera in June.

St. Cloud ALPRs
The St. Cloud Police Department owns five stationary Flock ALPRs, which were
installed in summer 2024, and five stationary Axon ALPRs, which were installed this
June, Oxton said. SCPD also has mobile Axon ALPRs mounted on more than 35 of its
fleet vehicles.
Its Flock cameras cost $15,000 annually, its stationary Axon cameras cost $12,500 and
the mobile ones cost $22,500.
Minnesota state law and Axon policy limit data retention to 60 days from when it was
collected unless it is being used in an investigation. On Aug. 13, Flock Safety lowered the
limit for its own cameras from 30 days to seven.

“An offender attempted to transport a sexual assault victim to Mexico to avoid

He said data safety concerns are often misunderstood, because cloud-based storage is the
same thing Apple, Samsung and Amazon all use, because it's safer than people realize. He
said St. Cloud specific data is also misunderstood because it's held in a closed system; it’s
not part of a larger database for people to freely search. 
“We help other law enforcement,” he said. “They’re required to give us their … agency
name, the case number, why they’re looking at it — it has to be a crime. We make them
document it.”
Public input about ALPR cameras in St. Cloud
The study session was not a public hearing and nothing involving ALPRs were on the
agenda for the regular meeting that followed.
Council Chair Mike Conway said the council is still figuring out when or whether there
will be a regular meeting to discuss the topic farther. He said there hadn’t been any

complaints in the first 10 years the city used the technology.
“As far as the community is concerned,” he said, “I think the trust they have built and has
been built by the police department and the community through (the Community
Policing) Agreement, … I think that’s something that is a foundation that they can build
on.”
Residents who spoke during the public forum after the meeting disagree, with multiple
people saying public trust has gone down since they’ve learned of ALPR use.
“The police department’s been working on it a lot in the community,” St. Cloud resident
Hannah Kosloski said. “But, in the steps they’ve taken related to mass surveillance
cameras, they just keep going with it, and no one has checked in about the process. No
one’s talked about the legality of it.”
Concerns mostly stem from a privacy viewpoint. If police have the ability to see every
car on the road, the cameras could be abused, Kosloski said. Police could track people
within the city or the data could be breached, particularly if the cameras use artificial
intelligence of any kind. Even the biennial audit results and the recently implemented
monthly internal audits aren’t easily accessible online.
Musab Nuh, another resident who spoke, said he requested the biennial audit of the
ALPR’s use along with the contracts through a public information request. He said that
was on Aug. 12, 41 days before the ALPR study session, and has not received anything
from St. Cloud.
At the same time, he requested Sartell’s information, which he received relatively
quickly. He said those results, which included eight cameras capturing almost 10.5
million images over the past year, showed more than 99% flagged nothing and nearly all
it did flag were license status checks. There weren’t any child abduction alerts, but there
were seven stolen vehicles.
“When you do the pure math, pros, cons, cost analysis, you end up with a very pricey
product-slash-service that reduces public trust and doesn’t actually do as well of a job
