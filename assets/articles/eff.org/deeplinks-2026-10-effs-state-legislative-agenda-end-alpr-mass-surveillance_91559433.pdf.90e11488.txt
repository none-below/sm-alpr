EFF’s State Legislative Agenda to
End ALPR Mass Surveillance
OCTOBER 21, 2026
eff.org
Police agencies across the U.S. have deployed Automated License Plate Readers
(ALPRs) to capture images of passing vehicles along with time, date, and GPS
coordinates. While the technology itself isn’t new (ALPRs have existed in
various forms for more than 50 years, and in the U.S. since 1998), the
surveillance landscape has shifted dramatically. Rapid advances in artificial
intelligence and national data sharing have transformed police ALPRs into an
interconnected, nationwide tracking network. It is pitched to city councils by
law enforcement and private vendors like Flock Safety, Motorola Solutions
(Vigilant), and Axon as an essential tool for solving violent crime.
In reality, it is an inherently dangerous infrastructure of mass surveillance.
Since 2012, EFF has worked with others to expose the severe digital rights
harms of ALPR systems. For example, we’ve recently documented how these
camera networks allow law enforcement to search for immigrants, track
peaceful protesters, target Romani communities, and track women seeking
reproductive healthcare. Police also routinely use ALPRs for low-level matters
like enforcing school residency requirements or investigating noise complaints.
Many officers also use ALPR databases to stalk past romantic partners. Recent
EFF analysis revealed officers using the system without legitimate justification
—logging search reasons like "LOL" and "LMAO."
Understandably, the public backlash to police ALPR technology is accelerating,
and state lawmakers across the country are taking action.
New Hampshire enacted the nation’s first state law on ALPR use in 2007, but
technological advances and misconduct have driven even more recent

--- page 2 ---
legislative action. In 2026 alone, six states enacted laws that limit police use of
ALPRs: Connecticut, Kentucky, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon and
Washington.
This is a good start, but far more is needed. This blog presents a framework for
state legislators who want to stop ALPR mass police surveillance:
1. Just ban it. It is incompatible with a free society and cannot be fixed.
2. In the absence of a flat ban, reduce its harms. Most importantly: limit
police use of ALPRs to real-time comparison of passing plates to hotlists;
require a warrant to place a plate on a hotlist; impose strict limits on the
creation and use of those hotlists; and prohibit the storage of ALPR data
(and thus prevent the subsequent search and disclosure of that data).
3. In the absence of a prohibition on storing ALPR data, reduce its harms.
Most importantly, place strict limits on police storage, search, and
disclosure of ALPR data.
4. Ban three especially dangerous police practices: placement of ALPRs near
sensitive locations like hospitals; purchase of ALPR data; and use of ALPR
data to try to predict crime.
5. Require transparency about ALPR mass surveillance, including: a 30-day
public notice before a city procures police ALPRs; a statewide registry of all
police ALPRs; and regular, public audits and statistical reports about police
usage.
6. Ensure effective enforcement of all new ALPR restraints, including by
means of a private right of action and the exclusionary rule.
These policy battles often unfold locally, so state legislators must ensure that
state law is a regulatory floor, never a ceiling. State preemption should never
force unwanted camera networks onto communities that have chosen to reject
them. Local government must retain authority to enact full bans, deny funding,
reject contracts, and decommission existing camera networks.

A Note on Scope
As in our previous post on ALPR policy, our focus here is ALPR
mass surveillance: the continuous dragnet collection and
retention of location data on innocent drivers. Different issues
are raised by automated traffic enforcement (ATE) systems,
such as standalone red-light or speed cameras that solely
photograph and ticket specific traffic violations, without
building a searchable network about uninvolved drivers. As
such, state laws against ALPR mass surveillance should not
cover ATE. This is the approach in at least six states:
Connecticut, Minnesota, North Carolina, Oregon, Virginia, and
Washington. The most common approach is to exclude ATE
from the law’s definition of “ALPR.”
Of course, ATE carries risks and demands legal restrictions too.
This includes tight limits on data collection, retention, usage,
and disclosure—all of which should be addressed in a different
statute. Lawmakers must also actively guard against vendor
efforts to piggyback mass surveillance tools onto ATE
contracts.
The Gold Standard: Statewide Bans on ALPR
Mass Surveillance
Statutory limits cannot make ALPR mass surveillance safe. Because the
technology relies on the indiscriminate recording of every driver’s movements,
the risks are baked into the system itself. Also, innocent motorists will be
menaced by police due to false matches, bad actors will misuse location data,
and sensitive personal movements will be exposed to broad government
overreach. Promising harsher penalties for officer misuse cannot fix an
inherently flawed architecture. Thus, state lawmakers should pursue a
complete, statewide ban on ALPR mass surveillance.
First, lawmakers should prohibit all state-level law enforcement agencies from
acquiring, retaining, accessing, or using ALPRs or any data derived from them.

--- page 4 ---
Second, they must impose the same prohibition on county, municipal, and
other local law enforcement agencies.
These prohibitions should require any agency ending an ALPR program (both
state and local) to fully decommission the programs within a strict timeframe.
This includes requiring agencies to: physically remove all camera hardware;
revoke all user, network, and database access credentials; permanently delete
all stored location data; and require vendor certification of compliance.
Third, ban the use of state funds for state or local agencies to acquire,
maintain, or access ALPR systems. This must include direct appropriations, law
enforcement grants, pass-through funds, and state-administered federal
funds. In August 2026, Texas Governor Greg Abbott banned state agencies from
spending public funds on Flock cameras.
Fourth, ban state and local agencies from installing or operating ALPRs on
state-owned highways, roadways, or public properties. In August 2026,
Florida’s Department of Transportation  ordered the removal of all ALPRs from
the right-of-way on state highways within 30 days. The order revokes all
previously approved permits to install ALPRs, and bars future permits.
An outright prohibition is the only effective way to eliminate this mass
surveillance. This follows the lead of municipalities across the country that
have already enacted bans. However, where there’s political resistance, state
lawmakers might consider a moratorium that prevents further expansion of
ALPR networks.
Strict Limits on Real-time Use of ALPRs
In the absence of a ban on police ALPR mass surveillance, state legislators must
restrict ALPRs to real-time comparison of passing plates to hotlists. ALPR data
is simply too dangerous for police to safely store, search, and disclose. An
effective way to do this, as in New Hampshire, is to require deletion of ALPR
data within three minutes after collection. That means no ALPR data is stored
after comparison to a hotlist, and thus that no ALPR data about unmatched cars
can be searched or disclosed.
A state legislature must also reduce the harms of police use of real-time ALPRs
by strictly limiting the creation and use of law enforcement hotlists.

Limits on creating hotlists
Roughly speaking, there are two kinds of police hotlists. An agency might link
its ALPRs to external hotlists created by another agency, such as the FBI’s
National Crime Information Center (NCIC) or a state’s department of motor
vehicles. Also, a police agency might link its ALPRs to its own internal hotlist,
created by its own officers, of people under investigation. State legislators must
strictly limit both.
First, limit the kinds of external hotlists that police agencies may link to. For
example, ban police agencies from linking to the NCIC’s “immigration violator”
hotlist, which ICE uses to hunt immigrants with the ALPRs operated by police
agencies across the country.
Second, if a police agency links to external hotlists, require the agency to
update that link every day. Otherwise, drivers may be detained even though
they have been removed from the list. This is required by Montana, Nebraska,
North Carolina, and Virginia.
Third, forbid officers from adding a plate on a hotlist, absent a warrant from a
judge based on probable cause and particularity. Without this judicial check on
police power, an officer stalked a potential romantic partner by placing her
plate on a hotlist.
As with other laws requiring a warrant (such as the federal Wiretap Act), there
should be an exception where: (1) there is an emergency involving immediate
danger of death or serious bodily injury to a person; (2) there is no time to
acquire a warrant; and (3) there are grounds upon which a warrant could be
granted. This exception might apply, for example, when police have probable
cause to believe a child has just been abducted. In such cases, the statute must
require police to seek a warrant within 48 hours, and if it is denied, to
immediately remove the plate from the hotlist.
Minnesota requires a warrant or an emergency to use ALPRs “to monitor or
track an individual who is the subject of an active criminal investigation.” In
Maine, an ALPR system may use only information based on reasonable
suspicion.
Fourth, forbid officers from adding a plate to a hotlist, except in connection
with a serious violent crime. This would include investigation of a murder but
not pursuit of a traffic scofflaw.

--- page 6 ---
Fifth, forbid officers from adding a plate on a hotlist based, in whole or in part,
on: a person’s race, ethnicity, national origin, religion, immigration status,
disability, sex, gender identity, or sexual orientation; their political beliefs,
associations, or participation in First Amendment-protected activity; or their
pursuit of reproductive, gender-affirming, or other health care. Connecticut
and Washington forbid use of ALPR systems on these grounds, and Virginia
forbids tracking a person based on their protected speech.
Sixth, require officers to log their entry of a plate into a hotlist. This must
include the officer’s name and agency, the date of the entry, a narrative
description of the reasons for the entry, any case numbers, and an upload of the
warrant authorizing the entry. Oregon requires an officer to log “the specific
law enforcement purpose of the entry,” and the officer’s name.
Limits on using hotlists
First, if the plate of a passing car does not match a hotlist, laws must require
immediate deletion of all ALPR data about that car. New Hampshire state law
requires deletion within three minutes. This is the most effective way to limit
ALPRs to real-time identification of vehicles and drivers of interest to police, as
opposed to amassing a dangerous database of the comings and goings of the
entire driving public, available later for retrospective search by police.
Second, if the plate of a passing car does match a hotlist, then limit storage,
search, and disclosure of its ALPR data to what is strictly necessary to the
investigation that placed the car on the hotlist.
Third, require police officers, before stopping a car because of an apparent
real-time match, to visually and independently confirm that the car’s plate
number, plate issuing state, and other vehicle characteristics in fact do match
the car on the hotlist. Many innocent motorists have been detained and
menaced by police because of ALPR false positives. Confirmation is required by
Kentucky, Montana, New Hampshire, Oregon, Virginia, and Washington.
Strict Limits on Storage, Search, and
Disclosure of ALPR Data
Again, state legislatures should simply ban police use of ALPRs, or at least
police storage of ALPR data. Third best is harm reduction through strict limits
on storage, search, and disclosure of ALPR data.

--- page 7 ---
Storage limits
First, limit the storage period for ALPR data to days, not months, and then
require deletion. More than 99% of the drivers who pass an ALPR are not on a
hotlist. Tracking their movements anyway, just in case they are later relevant to
a police investigation, is criminalization of the entire driving population. Thus,
four states already require deletion after 21 days: Connecticut, Maine, Virginia,
and Washington. Even Flock has reduced its default retention period to seven
days.
By “delete,” we mean permanently erase it, so it is impossible for anybody to
access it again. It is not enough to merely store it in a way that makes it harder
to access, such as archiving it, or transferring it from one database to another.
In the words of the Virginia statute: “System data shall be purged … in such a
manner that such data is destroyed and not recoverable.”
Second, limit the types of ALPR data that can be stored: just a photo of the
plate, the AI-based read of the plate number, the date and time, and the
location. Conversely, ban retention of photos of people in the car, and of
features of the car beyond the plate as well as AI-based reads of those photos
(respectively, “facial recognition” and “vehicle fingerprints”).
Many states have enacted such rules. New Hampshire bans ALPRs from
“photographing or recording or producing images of the occupants of a motor
vehicle.” Missouri bans integration of facial recognition into ALPR systems.
New Hampshire and Montana limit ALPRs to “the sole purpose of recording and
checking license plates.” Minnesota provides that “data collected by an [ALPR]
must be limited to” plate numbers; date, time, and location; and pictures of
plate, vehicle, and surrounding area.
Search limits
First, laws should ban “geofence” searches, which are searches in which police
use ALPRs to identify all of the drivers present at a particular time and place,
based on police claims that one of them might be the suspect they are looking
for. Even a warrant cannot justify such general searches, because the Fourth
Amendment requires warrants to “particularly” describe the person or car
being investigated. These dragnet searches of stored ALPR data are the
modern-day equivalent of the colonial-era “writs of assistance,” against which
our founders fought a revolution to end.

--- page 8 ---
Second, laws should forbid officers from searching stored ALPR data, unless
they first acquire a warrant from a judge based on probable cause and
particularity. The constant revelations of bad police searches of ALPR data
demonstrate the need for—at the very least—judicial control over this
dangerous surveillance technology. A federal judge in Oklahoma recently held
that the constitution requires this judicial control, because ALPR mass
surveillance invades reasonable expectations of privacy. EFF has filed lawsuits
making this demand in California and Wisconsin, along with local ACLU
affiliates: SIREN and CAIR-CA v. San Jose, and Wisconsin Council of Churches v.
Milwaukee, Racine, Kenosha, and Green Bay.
Three states (Minnesota, Vermont, and Virginia) require reasonable suspicion
of a crime before searching ALPR data, and a fourth state (California) requires
this from its highway patrol. This is a step in the right direction, but not
enough: the decider must be a judge, and the test must be probable cause.
Third, officers should be prohibited from searching stored ALPR data, unless
they are investigating a serious violent crime. On the flip side, ban searches for
immigrants, protesters, or traffic scofflaws.
Some states have moved in this direction. Missouri limits use of ALPR data
“criminal justice” and “lifesaving efforts.” North Carolina bans its use for
traffic enforcement. Connecticut bans its use based on race and other protected
classes, to identify First Amendment activity, for immigration enforcement, or
because of access to reproductive or gender-affirming health care. Idaho bans
its use for political activity.
Fourth, police officers must be required to log their searches of stored ALPR
data concerning a particular car. This must include the officer’s name and
agency, the date and time of search, a narrative description of the reasons for
search, any case numbers, and an upload of the warrant authorizing the search.
The officer must write the narrative in their own words. A drop-down menu of
reasons is no substitute, because it is too easy for officers to dodge an
explanation of the actual reasons by clicking a pre-written “reason.”
Many states require officers to log their searches of ALPR data, including
California, Connecticut, Idaho, Maryland, Missouri, Montana, New Hampshire,
Oregon, and Virginia. In Virginia, this must include an officer’s documentation
of both their “specific purpose” to search, and the “associated call for service
or case number.”

Disclosure limits
The “disclosure” limits below must broadly address sharing from an agency
that collected ALPR data to another entity. This includes an agency opening its
database to access and search by another entity. It also includes an agency
transferring data from its database to another entity’s database.
First, ban disclosure of ALPR data to any federal or out-of-state agency.
Federal and out-of-state agencies often lack the necessary protections of the
driving public. Connecticut forbids disclosures to the federal government or
agencies in 46 states, unless they have a warrant. California forbids disclosures,
unless otherwise permitted by law.
Second, ban disclosure of ALPR data to another agency, unless it follows all of
the rules above for an agency’s search of its own data (that is, the ban on
geofence searches, the requirement of a warrant, the limitation to investigation
of serious violent crimes, and the requirement of an audit trail).
Some states have taken steps in this direction. Utah requires, as a condition of
disclosure to a government entity or a defendant in a criminal case, a court
order finding reasonable suspicion of crime. Washington forbids disclosure
“except as required in a judicial proceeding.” Minnesota and Virginia require
the disclosing agency to find reasonable suspicion. Minnesota and Connecticut
forbid disclosure unless the receiving agency follows the rules applicable to the
disclosing agency.
Third, place strict limits on participation in a multi-agency disclosure network,
such as Flock or Vigilant. Connecticut forbids participation “in a system or
network that shares” ALPR data, unless it requires all participants to affirm in
writing that they will not use or re-disclose that state’s data except in
compliance with that state’s laws. Connecticut also forbids “bulk” data
disclosure, and Maryland forbids data “upload” to another agency.
Fourth, ban disclosure of ALPR data for purposes of investigating acts and
statuses that are lawful in-state. Three states (Illinois, New Jersey, and New
Mexico) forbid disclosure to investigate immigration status. Two states (Illinois
and New Mexico) forbid disclosure in investigate healthcare that is lawful in-
state. One state (New Mexico) forbids disclosure to investigate protected
assembly, petition, and speech.
Fifth, ban any sale of ALPR data. Six states do this: Arkansas, Maryland,
Oklahoma, Utah, Virginia, and Washington. California forbids its highway

--- page 10 ---
patrol from doing so. New Mexico forbids any disclosure to a private entity (or
indeed to any non-law enforcement agency). Data brokers are a menace to
privacy and other human rights.
Transparency
State lawmakers must ensure that the public has access to ample information
about the ALPR mass surveillance in their communities. Many people will use
this information to resist ALPRs.
First, state laws should require local governments to provide at least 30 days of
public notice before spending funds, accepting grants or private donations, or
entering into any contract or pilot program for ALPR technology. All proposed
vendor contracts and funding sources must be publicly disclosed prior to
approval. This helps local officials to exercise their procurement power to reject
ALPRs.
Second, laws should mandate the creation of a centralized, public-facing state
registry of all police agencies using ALPRs, and the specific locations of all
police ALPRs. Sources like DeFlock and EFF’s Atlas of Surveillance include
crowd-sourced maps of ALPR technologies, but they are not exhaustive. This
was one of the provisions in Minnesota Minn. Stat. § 13.824, which in part,
requires police agencies to notify the state within 10 days of installing any
ALPR, and requires a state agency to aggregate this information and publish a
master list of the locations of all ALPRs (unless a judge determines that
disclosure of a particular ALPR’s location poses a genuine security risk).
Third, mandate regular, independent, public audits of agency ALPR use, access
logs, and query justifications to ensure compliance with all applicable laws and
policies. Five states (Idaho, Minnesota, Montana, Virginia, and Washington)
require regular audits, ranging in frequency from monthly to biennial. Three
states (Idaho, Maryland, and Washington) require audits of access logs.
Minnesota requires an independent auditor and public access to the results.
Fourth, laws must require each agency operating an ALPR system to publish
regular reports on its website detailing key operational metrics. Six states
require such reports annually: Connecticut, Maryland, New Hampshire, New
Mexico, and Vermont. Arkansas requires them twice per year, and Oregon
requires them monthly. These states variously require reporting of: the number
of ALPRs, and a list of the locations of fixed ALPRs; the number of hotlists used,
and a list of them; the number of plates scanned; the number of apparent

--- page 11 ---
matches to hotlists, and how many were actual matches; the number plate
reads stored; and the number of searches of stored data, broken down by the
purpose and agency of search.
Finally, any new ALPR legislation must maintain a proper balance between
transparency and privacy in the use of public record acts (PRAs) to obtain ALPR
information. Public records and public scrutiny of ALPR programs have shown
that people are harmed by these systems. So lawmakers should not cut off
access to public records that document these abuses. For example, the public
should have access to operational policies, audit logs, vendor communications,
and aggregate statistics. EFF opposes blanket exemptions from PRAs that block
public oversight of APRs—such as recent bills in states like Arizona. The better
approach to protecting individual location privacy is a flexible, case-by-case
balancing test already provided by many PRAs. Also, when a document contains
both exempt and non-exempt information—like an audit log that contains both
motorists’ plate numbers and officers’ search reasons—open records laws
should require disclosure of the latter while withholding of the former.
Additional Restrictions
State lawmakers should adopt three additional bans to curtail the footprint of
police ALPR surveillance.
First, ban the placement of ALPRs or the collection of ALPR data near
healthcare facilities, immigration services, schools, places of worship,
courthouses, food banks, and other sensitive locations. Washington and
Connecticut do this.
Second, ban police from purchasing ALPR data. Utah and Washington do so.
Also, prohibit police from acquiring ALPR data from a private entity, except by
means of a warrant. Washington does so. Utah took a step in this direction,
requiring a court order based on reasonable suspicion. Absent this type of rule,
police evade limits on seizing data from third parties by simply purchasing it
from brokers.
Third, ban police and their vendors from using ALPRs or stored ALPR data to
power predictive policing technology. EFF opposes such technology and urges
state legislatures to ban it. ALPR companies have been providing law
enforcement clients with an “analytics” feature, which ingests data from across
police agencies, and produces a report of alleged crime trends, hotspots, and

--- page 12 ---
corridors. State legislators should prohibit this dangerous use of ALPR systems
and data.
Enforcement
A law without strong enforcement is just a piece of paper. EFF recommends
three kinds of enforcement measures, all of which appear in existing ALPR
statutes.
First, all privacy laws need a “private right of action,” meaning ordinary people
can sue a police department that breaks the law. An excellent model appears in
the federal Wiretap Act. It expressly authorizes a lawsuit by “any person”
whose personal information is processed “in violation of” this law. Privacy
harm is harm, and so a person should have their day in court, even if they
cannot prove a collateral harm, such as physical injury or economic loss.
Moreover, the Wiretap Act provides a full arsenal of remedies to a successful
plaintiff, including injunctive and declaratory relief, liquidated and actual
damages, and attorney fees.
At least three states enforce their ALPR statute with some version of a private
right of action. These are good first steps but need improvement. Washington
comes closest: it allows suit to enforce all ALPR rules and provides all remedies.
Unfortunately, the plaintiff must prove “injury,” which may shut the
courthouse doors to some people whose data is unlawfully processed.
Connecticut allows suit by a person “aggrieved” by violation of some ALPR
rules. But a person should be able to enforce all rules, and should not have to
prove they were aggrieved. Oregon allows suit against a police vendor that
“intentionally or with gross negligence” uses or discloses ALPR data in an
unlawful manner. But this does not allow any suit against police, or even
against a vendor that systematically violates the rules through ordinary
negligence.
Second, privacy laws applicable to the police commonly ensure enforcement
through the “exclusionary rule.” This means that if evidence is gathered in
violation a privacy law, it must be excluded from court proceedings. This is
sometimes called “suppression” or “inadmissibility.” The federal Wiretap Act
has a strong version of this rule, clearly excluding evidence derived from
violations of all rules. The so-called “good faith” exception has weakened many
exclusionary rules, so ALPR statutes should expressly reject this exception
(though some courts have interpreted some statutory exclusionary rules to have
no good faith exception).

--- page 13 ---
At least three states enforce their ALPR statute with some version of the
exclusionary rule. Again, these are good first steps but need improvement:
Virginia comes closest: “Any evidence obtained as a result of a violation” of
some of the ALPR rules “is not admissible.” A better rule would apply to all
ALPR rules.
Connecticut provides that ALPR data “may be admissible … provided such
data was collected or otherwise acquired or held or stored in accordance
with the provisions of this section” (or obtained pursuant to warrant). A
more clear exclusionary rule would “exclude evidence unlawfully
processed,” as opposed to “admit evidence lawfully processed.” The
exclusionary rule should also extend to data unlawfully used or disclosed
(and not just to data unlawfully collected or stored).
Washington provides that information obtained from “a knowing violation”
of some of the statute’s rules is “inadmissible.” But the exclusionary rule
should apply to all substantive limits (not just some), and to all violations
(not just knowing ones).
Third, police agencies and their private contractors that recurringly violate
ALPR rules should be forbidden from using ALPRs at all. In New Hampshire, if
an agency “repeated[ly]” violates the ALPR statute, the state department of
justice may “seek an injunction banning [its] use” of ALPRs. In New Mexico, if
a vendor “fails to comply with” that state’s ALPR law, it “may be subject to …
suspension of authorization or termination of access.”
Some states impose workplace discipline or criminal penalties on officers who
violate ALPR laws, including Connecticut, Idaho, and Missouri. This is no
substitute for the three types of strong enforcement measures above. ALPRs are
a systemic problem requiring institution-wide enforcement of legal
constraints, and not just episodic sanctions against individual officers.
Conclusion
Mass surveillance ALPRs fundamentally threaten digital rights, making an
outright statewide ban on police ALPRs the only effective way to fully protect
privacy and safety.  The next best thing a legislature can do is limit ALPRs to
real-time comparison of passing cars to hotlists, by banning storage of ALPR
data beyond a few minutes. If a legislature allows storage, it must impose a
tight deletion deadline (days and not months), and strict limits on search and
disclosure. A legislature must also ensure transparency and enforcement.
