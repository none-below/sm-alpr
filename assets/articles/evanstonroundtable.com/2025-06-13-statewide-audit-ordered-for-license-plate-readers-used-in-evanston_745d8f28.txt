Daily Brief Evanston’s latest news and features Raising Evanston 1X week: everything E-town for families Sign up A nationwide network of automatic license plate readers that includes the Evanston Police Department and hundreds of other Illinois agencies will be audited in Illinois by Secretary of State Alexi Giannoulias, after reporting revealed that out-of-state police searched data from at least two Illinois cities in abortion and immigration cases in potential violation of state law. Independent technology outlet 404 Media published two stories in May highlighting the searches, which were discovered in search audits obtained via Freedom of Information Act requests. These audits revealed that the Mount Prospect Police Department was included in a May 9 search by the Johnson County (Texas) Sheriff’s Office looking for a woman who it said had a self-administered abortion and concerned family members, and that the Danville Police Department received numerous out-of-state vehicle searches that directly cited “immigration” or “ICE” (U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement) as their reason, among others related to immigration enforcement. Both cities use license plate cameras and software provided by Flock Safety , a surveillance technology company that says it has placed such cameras in over 5,000 U.S. communities, including Evanston, and describes the technology as a “force multiplier” for law enforcement. Agencies that use Flock are able to search data from cameras well beyond their own borders, allowing for broad searches of tens of thousands of cameras across the country at one time. A screenshot from the homepage of Flock Safety markets the company’s license plate readers for use by law enforcement, businesses, property managers, K-12 schools, higher education and homeowners associations. Credit: Flock Safety In a Thursday news release , Giannoulias’ office asserts the out-of-state searches violate a state law adopted in 2023 that prohibits users of license plate cameras from sharing data with agencies investigating people for seeking reproductive health care or because of their immigration status. “License plate readers can serve as an important tool for law enforcement, but these cameras must be regulated so they aren’t abused for surveillance, tracking the data of innocent people or criminalizing lawful behavior,” Giannoulias said in the release. “No one seeking legal healthcare services in Illinois should face harassment or jail – period.” Accordingly, Giannoulias has ordered Flock to “immediately shut off access for the out-of-state authorities illegally using the system” and requested the Illinois attorney general’s office “investigate the matter,” per the release. His office said it will create an “audit system with additional safeguards” to ensure compliance with state law. In a Thursday blog post responding to Giannoulias’ actions, Flock stated it removed access to Illinois data for 17 agencies found to have “conducted multiple searches using reasons impermissible under Illinois law,” and later revoked access for another 29 at Giannoulias’ request. The company also touted a “proactive search term tool” it launched Thursday, which it claimed automatically excludes Illinois data if the “search reason” field includes words that “indicate an impermissible purpose under Illinois law.” Additional “AI-Powered Auditing Alerts” and an option for agencies to require case numbers be included to access data are promised by year-end. “We have been collaborating with the Sec. of State’s office to ensure Illinois can determine how and when to use LPR [License Plate Recognition] data that is derived from Illinois, as guided by state law,” the statement reads. “Flock takes compliance seriously, and we welcome the opportunity to continue working with Secretary Giannoulias’ office on promoting best practices around LPR use.” Mapping Evanston’s Flock In Evanston there are 18 Flock cameras affixed to poles at intersections around the city, the locations of which were disclosed in a prior FOIA request from August 2023 documented on public records database MuckRock . These locations are marked on the interactive map below. Zoom in on the map above to view where Evanston 18 stationary Flock cameras are located, as indicated by blue camera icons. Click on an icon to read which direction it faces, and to see a photo of the camera taken on June 12, 2025. Created by Alex Harrison using Google My Maps. These cameras are concentrated along the city’s border at major streets for entering and exiting. Eight cameras cover traffic coming to and from Howard Street, Evanston’s primary border with Chicago, and a ninth faces north on Sheridan Road next to Calvary Cemetery. Another six are placed at streets which cross the North Shore Channel, where the city borders Skokie. No cameras are placed along the city’s northern border with Wilmette, but this doesn’t mean a lack of coverage. Open source database DeFlock , which maps license plate readers operated by Flock and other vendors, shows at least seven cameras are stationed in Wilmette at intersections near the village’s border with Evanston. Another four are located in Skokie at various points west of Evanston. A Flock license plate camera, on the black pole, pointed east along Oakton Street just east of the North Shore Channel. Credit: Alex Harrison Evanston first contracted with Flock for 10 devices in December 2022 , which was invoiced for $24,999.90 in January 2023. Two months later, in March the city paid an additional $24,996 for the remaining eight license plate readers, a mobile “Flex” unit and implementation fees. Those two invoices, which were approved administratively, were respectively 10 cents and $4 short of $25,000, the figure above which city code says contracts must be approved by City Council. Just over a year later, EPD came to City Council in January 2024 with a five-year contract renewal with Flock at $48,500 per year, or $242,500 through 2028, which councilmembers approved 7-0. In the staff memo for the renewal, EPD Cmdr. Ryan Glew wrote that Flock “has been of tremendous value to the department,” specifically naming hit-and-run crashes, vehicle thefts and the April 2023 homicide of Jacquis Irby as incidents where the cameras assisted department investigations. When the RoundTable emailed city officials and EPD leadership Thursday to request comment on Giannoulias’ announcement, Glew wrote back that the department’s license plate camera uses “align with local ordinances and state law,” and referred to EPD’s standing policies on the Flock system’s use. These include prohibiting its use in determining immigration status or in immigration enforcement, prohibiting its use in interfering with reproductive health care and requiring system audits be performed on a quarterly basis. In January, ahead of President Donald Trump’s inauguration, City Council adopted a strengthened Welcoming City Ordinance , which specifically prohibits sharing data with entities requesting it for immigration enforcement purposes. A proposed ordinance that would protect data concerning abortion issues, dubbed the Health Data Protection Ordinance , will be on City Council’s agenda for adoption on June 23. The RoundTable requested EPD’s Flock audit records from the prior year via a Freedom Of Information Act request on May 29. The city denied these records June 4, citing “ongoing investigations.” The RoundTable has appealed this denial with the city, but the appeal is still outstanding at time of publication. Δ Cindy M Reed says: August 12th, 2025 at 8:39 PM Why are the ALPR basically in the Black neighborhoods? Reply David Beazley says: June 15th, 2025 at 11:21 AM Thanks for your reporting on this. An under-reported aspect of Flock safety is their “privacy” pitch to local communities. I’ve seen many presentations pitching Flock through public records of nearby communities (Wilmette, Kenilworth, Skokie, etc.). In these presentations, an emphasis is made that “no personally identifiable information” is recorded by the cameras. However, this is usually described as being raw video footage–stating that the cameras only record the rear of the vehicle and not the driver. Also, that raw video footage is only retained for 30 days and belongs to the local police department. There’s no reason to doubt those specific claims, but video footage is probably the least interesting aspect of data collected by these cameras. Obviously, they record plate numbers, but the cameras are doing much more than just that. For example, AI is being used to create a vehicle profile including plate, make, model, and color. The cameras can also identify vehicle damage, accessories (roof-racks, etc.), bumper stickers, and other unique car features. Data is also timestamped. Ownership of this data is unclear. Not to mince words, if you’re driving by one of these cameras, you’re projecting a lot more information than just the plate. Flock might know your work schedule, your politics, your hobbies, and the fact that your kid is on the ETHS honor roll. It doesn’t take much imagination to think of possible avenues for abuse. Reply Ezra Shevick says: June 13th, 2025 at 11:25 PM Thank you to Alex and the Roundtable for this extremely important story. Alex references an article from 404 Media entitled “ICE Taps into Nationwide AI-Enabled Camera Network, Data Shows.” This article establishes a few things in regards to the use of Flock cameras. It explains that municipalities have to opt in to “Statewide Lookup” and “Nationwide Lookup.” By opting in, a municipality shares its license plate data with all other municipalities who are opted in to this feature. By opting in, Evanston would gain the ability to search other municipalities’ license plate data. It also explains that Evanston was listed as having performed searches in Danville, IL according to a data file from Danville. I was able to download the file and found hundreds of searches which apparently originate from Evanston PD. It also explains how ICE and other federal agencies, while not having direct access to the Flock database, often can perform searches through friendly local police departments. They refer to this as “side-door access.” 404 Media claims that over 4,000 of the searches in the Danville data file were for immigration related purposes. Putting these all together, I think it is fairly reasonable to assume that Evanston has enabled the “Statewide Lookup” option. If that is in fact the case, you can assume that ICE and other federal agencies have indirect access to Evanston’s license plate data. Moving back to Alex’s article, the solutions Flock claims are clearly inadequate. Crucially, this system relies on the searching party to comply with state and local laws and provide an accurate reason for the search. The party who has shared the data can see who searched the data, and what reason they provided, but the vast amounts of searches performed ensure this exercise will be impractical. And obviously this is only retroactive. A magic AI bot that can flag potentially problematic searches hardly feels like a realistic solution to this problem.
