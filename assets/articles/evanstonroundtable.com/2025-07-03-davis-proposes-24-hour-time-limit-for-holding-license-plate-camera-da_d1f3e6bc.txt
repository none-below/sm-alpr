Daily Brief Evanston’s latest news and features Raising Evanston 1X week: everything E-town for families Sign up Logs of vehicles detected by the city’s network of automated license plate readers would be significantly shortened under a legislative referral filed Tuesday by Councilmember Parielle Davis (7th Ward), which proposes limiting the data retention period to 24 hours. Flock Safety , the private company contracted with the Evanston Police Department to provide the cameras and accompanying software, states in its transparency portal that camera data on detected vehicles and plate numbers is owned by EPD and currently retained for 30 days. System access records listing who searched the camera data and when are retained indefinitely. Davis was one of several elected officials who voiced wariness about the Flock system’s security in a City Council discussion last week, which followed the RoundTable’s reporting that Evanston’s network was shared with at least seven out-of-state police agencies that had also searched cameras in Mount Prospect and Danville, Illinois, for immigration-related cases. previous coverage FOIA records reveal Evanston data accessible to out-of-state police making ICE searches by Alex Harrison June 16th, 2025 August 28th, 2025 Council Bytes: EPD bars out-of-state agencies from plate reader network by Alex Harrison and Emma Richman June 24th, 2025 July 13th, 2025 Illinois Secretary of State Alexi Giannoulias ordered Flock to cut off several dozen non-Illinois agencies from accessing the state’s cameras after those searches were reported by national tech outlet 404 Media. His office is also developing a new audit system to ensure compliance with a state law that prohibits sharing data with agencies investigating people for accessing reproductive health or over their immigration status. “Overly broad data retention policies pose a security risk, open up data to abuse by internal actors, violate privacy principles, and increase legal and compliance exposure,” Davis wrote in the referral, which is co-sponsored by Councilmembers Clare Kelly (1st Ward) and Juan Geracaris (9th Ward). Purging data In a phone call Wednesday, she elaborated that besides it generally being “poor practice” to store data longer than necessary, she believes purging the data altogether is the only way to totally ensure it can’t be obtained by unauthorized actors, in particular the federal government. Davis works as a senior software engineer for Google. “We’re not a city of a bunch of people that are ready to lose our livelihoods, and to push back so significantly as to defy federal government orders, because it would have very significant consequences,” Davis said. “With that in mind, the only way we can avoid having to turn over data that perhaps would endanger people in our communities who are in precarious situations, is to not have it in the first place.” Zoom in on the map above to view where Evanston’s 18 stationary Flock cameras are located, as indicated by blue camera icons. Click on an icon to read which direction it faces, and to see a photo of the camera taken on June 12, 2025. Created by Alex Harrison using Google My Maps. EPD Cmdr. Scott Sophier told the RoundTable via email Wednesday that the camera network is currently in use under the new sharing restrictions described by Chief Schenita Stewart at the June 23 council meeting. This includes disabling all out-of-state sharing, including the “national lookup” tool, and requiring written authorization from her and City Manager Luke Stowe for any Illinois agencies requesting new access to Evanston’s network. Researching the impact Sophier wrote that the department and other city leaders have also “consulted with the Mayor and all Councilmembers on this topic.” Answering a question on how Davis’ data retention referral would impact the Flock network’s use in local and regional investigations, he wrote that the department “will continue to conduct robust research on ALPR data retention and its potential impact for the community.” Davis said she’s exchanged emails with Stewart about limiting the data retention period, including having no retention at all and only using the data for “real-time checks” for specific marked vehicles. “If we’re checking every car that comes in, and we’re saying, ‘Hey, this car on the AMBER list [for child abduction alerts], or this car on the stolen car list,'” Davis said, “we can do the check and then just never store the information, or at least put it into any type of long-term storage.” She said Stewart provided her with some use cases where shorter retention periods “made sense,” but she doesn’t believe there are any uses that justify holding the data for the current 30-day period. The proposal will be on the July 14 agenda of the Referrals Committee, which will assign it to a committee for discussion and set a recommended timeline. Davis is requesting it be sent to the Administration and Public Works Committee, of which she is a member, and said she expects a “back-and-forth dialogue” that’s more focused on policy concerns than the technology or the company providing it. “Whether or not we want to retain data for 30 days or 24 hours has nothing to do with Flock technology,” Davis said, “and it has more so to do with what we decide as a community, what values we’re going to pick.” Support the RoundTable!
