Concern about an employee’s work hours is a management problem. There are established
ways to address it: documented conversations, formal audits, or a request for a council
review. Milner Mayor Craig Timmons chose a GPS tracker instead. Timmons acknowledged
placing the device on Police Chief Lamar Bunn’s vehicle, according to WSB-TV, which
reported his confirmation. He also arranged for a surveillance camera pointed toward City
Hall, according to local reporting, though the camera’s full authorization and purpose remain
less firmly documented.

Chief, Officers, City Manager: All Gone by October
28
Bunn said he had done nothing wrong, and a resignation letter pointed to concerns about
leadership and judgment that extended well beyond one device.
Bunn said he had done nothing wrong and consulted an outside agency about whether the
tracking violated the law or his rights. No criminal charge or definitive legal finding has been
established in the available reporting. A Georgia Bureau of Investigation inquiry was
mentioned in local reporting; its status and scope remain unclear.
Chief Bunn, all five full-time officers, and most reserve personnel resigned, with departures
effective October 28, 2026. City Manager Tausha Grose also resigned shortly after.
A resignation letter obtained by WSB-TV cited concerns about the “judgment and decision-
making” of the mayor and certain council members regarding the police department. Bunn
said he did not ask the officers to leave.

Small Towns Have Oversight Tools. Timmons Didn’t
Use Them.
The governance failure here is straightforward to diagnose, even if the consequences for
residents have already arrived.
Municipal governments have recognized mechanisms for addressing personnel concerns:
documented performance reviews, timesheet audits initiated through proper administrative
channels, or a council-authorized inquiry. Those processes exist to protect employees from
abuse and to protect elected officials from exactly the outcome Timmons is now facing.
Timmons bought a tracker with personal funds, installed it without council knowledge, and
aimed a camera at City Hall. He acted outside the institutional channels that exist precisely
to make oversight accountable and transparent.
The result is not merely an HR dispute. A town of 772 people lost its entire full-time police
force, and the accounts of those who left cite broader concerns about leadership, judgment,
and trust in addition to the tracker itself. Resignation letters pointed to a failure of
governance, not just a single questionable decision.
The tracker did not create that failure. It made it visible.
