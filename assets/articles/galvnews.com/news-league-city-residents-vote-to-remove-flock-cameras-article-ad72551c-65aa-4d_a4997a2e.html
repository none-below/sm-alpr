League City residents vote to remove Flock cameras

LEAGUE CITY

League City voters rejected the continued use of the city’s 55 Flock Safety license plate readers Tuesday, triggering a council-approved plan to remove or disable the cameras after election results are officially canvassed.

A total of 25,021 voters, nearly 54%, voted against Proposition A, compared with 21,412 voters, about 46%, who supported keeping the cameras, according to unofficial election results.

Tuesday’s results remain unofficial until the state completes its canvass and certifies the vote.

The months leading up to Election Day were marked by extensive social media debate over whether the cameras should continue operating in the city, with residents divided over the technology’s law enforcement benefits and potential privacy implications.

“There’s no disagreement that they work,” Mayor Nick Long said Tuesday. “However, there’s also no disagreement that there’s serious privacy concerns.”

The vote came amid nationwide debate over Flock’s technology. Some municipalities have canceled contracts over privacy concerns, while others defend the cameras as crime-solving tools.

Flock’s database allows law enforcement to search vehicle records by license plate, vehicle type, color, distinguishing marks, location and time.

Since approving the referendum on Tuesday’s ballot Aug. 11, city council adopted contingency plans addressing both possible outcomes.

Under the removal plan approved Oct. 13, officials will have 72 hours after the official canvass of election results to remove the city’s cameras or cover those remaining to prevent them from collecting license plate data.

The resolution also directs City Manager John Baumgartner to review the city’s agreements with Flock Safety and pursue changes necessary to carry out the removal.

The plan could bring an end to the city’s roughly $120,000 annual contract with Flock Safety, although officials must first account for any remaining contractual obligations.

“We have a plan to get out of the contract and we will move on that as soon as we know the outcome of the vote,” Long said.

While the removal plan calls for identifying alternative public safety uses for money made available by ending the program, Police Chief Cliff Woitena said in October the money would first return to the city’s overall budget and no alternative use had been planned.

“This is $120,000 for police,” Long said Tuesday. “We’ll definitely keep it in the budget for the police.”

Had voters approved the continued use of Flock cameras, the city’s Citizen Data Privacy Committee would have remained in place to provide oversight of the system, review its policies and monitor compliance through audits.

With residents voting against the cameras, however, the committee will instead oversee one final audit of the city’s use of the system and submit its findings to city council before dissolving.
