Dane County Board votes to cut funding for Flock license-plate reader cameras

Chair says Board open to considering alternative ALPR systems

  • CHRIS LEE chris.lee@apg-sw.com

    Chris Lee

The Dane County Board has voted to remove funding from the Sheriff’s Office for use of its Flock Safety automated license plate reader system (ALPR) and prohibit further spending on the system.

According to a Dane County Board news release, on April 16, the Board voted to amend the 2026 county budget — which had included $80,000 for the annual Flock subscription — to remove that funding. The current subscription runs through May 31.

The vote was 32-1 in favor of the resolution, with Supervisor Michael Engelberger voting against it and one Board member absent.


“Nothing about this action suggests that our deputies have misused this system,” said Supervisor Chad Kemp, District 32, who authored the resolution.

“But the sheriff’s office has not been able to affirmatively confirm that the agencies it shares data with, or Flock itself, are not misusing the information collected by cameras in Dane County. Given what we know about how this technology has been abused elsewhere, the responsible course of action is to end funding for this contract.”

The resolution cited growing concerns raised by residents, policymakers, and privacy experts about reported and verified abuses of data collected through Flock’s ALPR network, including informal data-sharing with federal agencies that bypasses oversight and accountability measures, according to the news release.

The Dane County Sheriff’s Office had granted access to information gathered by its 24 Flock cameras to more than 140 law enforcement agencies, including some in Iowa, Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Missouri, New York, and Tennessee, the news release said.

“There are well-documented concerns about how this company operates and uses its technology to violate people’s 4th Amendment rights,” said County Board Chair Patrick Miles.

“The board’s action supports protecting our community from a proven bad actor. We have full confidence in our Sheriff and deputies, and we are open to considering other companies that have stronger safeguards in place. At a time when Flock, some of its law enforcement partners, and federal immigration enforcement agencies have been found to collect and misuse personal information for tracking and surveillance in ways that violate constitutional rights, the board has a responsibility to act.”

Miles said that board members have expressed openness to considering alternative ALPR technology not operated by Flock.

“This is about one company and its practices, not about whether law enforcement should have access to modern tools,” he said. “Supervisors remain open to solutions that support public safety while protecting the privacy and civil liberties of everyone in Dane County.”

In a response, Sheriff Kalvin Barrett said that while he respects the Board’s decision to terminate its contract with a provider it deemed untrustworthy and unreliable, the Board’s actions and statements during the discussion suggest that an additional motive was to reduce funding for the Sheriff’s Office.

“Despite multiple opportunities to support the office through amendments that would have preserved this critical funding, the Board majority voted against each proposal,” Barrett said. “These funds could have been reallocated to partner with a more reliable vendor or, alternatively, invested in recruitment and retention efforts to address staffing shortages and retain experienced deputies. Instead, the Board elected, once again, to reduce funding for the sheriff’s office.”

In the past few years, many south-central Wisconsin counties, cities, and villages have installed ALPR systems, which use cameras and software to automatically capture, analyze, and store license plate numbers, and often, additional identifying information from the backs of vehicles.

That information is processed, and when used as part of a network of data, can alert law enforcement to stolen or wanted vehicles, help them solve other crimes, or locate missing persons.

Atlanta-based Flock Safety calls itself the “largest public-private safety network” and says it is used by more than 5,000 law enforcement agencies in 49 states.

In addition to the Dane, Columbia, and Jefferson County Sheriff’s offices, the Cottage Grove, Marshall, Monona, Sun Prairie, and Waunakee police departments all use Flock cameras, as do Jefferson, Lodi, Poynette, Watertown, and Whitewater. Lake Mills and McFarland police are getting them this year.
