Sun Prairie Police propose ALPR switch, resident
opposition remains
Ethan Ferrell eferrell@hngnews.com
Oct 16, 2026
Residents across the region have been voicing opposition to the use of automated
license plate reader (ALPR) technology by their local police departments  over the
past few months.
Some have picked out one top vendor, Flock Safety, in particular due to privacy
concerns and other instances across the country where it has refused to remove
cameras after being instructed to do so.

Over the past year, a slew of area municipalities like Fitchburg, Verona, Monona,
and Cottage Grove have decided to end or not renew their ALPR contracts with
Flock.
In some cases, it was convenient operationally. The Waunakee Police Department
removed its five Flock cameras after they were vandalized in August.
For other communities, the decision was more policy based. The Cottage Grove
Village Board unanimously voted to discontinue the use of its Flock cameras
immediately on Sept. 21, with further discussion about a complete ban of ALPRs to
potentially come in the future.

The City of Sun Prairie announced that it would not be renewing its contract with
Flock for the coming year on Sept. 16.
The Sun Prairie Police Department (SPPD) currently has six cameras that will
expire on Dec. 31, 2026, and three others that will expire on March 31, 2027.
SPPD Chief Kevin Warych has stated that the decision is in response to resident
feedback, but that he believes ALPR systems are still valuable tools for law
enforcement.
As somewhat of a compromise, Warych proposed to the Sun Prairie Public Safety
Committee on Wednesday, Oct. 7 that the city switch to Axon for its ALPR services
going forward.
“I have subscribed to taking the open book approach on as many as issues that I’m
allowed to in policing, and license plate reader technology is very much that,” he
said on Oct. 7. ”The reason we bring this forth to the public safety committee is to
be open and transparent.”
The value Sun Prairie police see in ALPR
Warych stated that Flock hasn’t done anything specific in Sun Prairie to
“jeopardize” its trust, but that he also wasn’t “naive” to the discussions that have
been taking place across the state.
“About a year ago today, we had a very similar conversation… I summed it up (as)
people did not like the current vendor,” he said.
Warych noted that SPPD is already switching to Axon for its body-worn cameras
in 2027, and that having all of its software working on the same system would
already be a benefit.

He explained that four “buckets” make up his professional stance on ALPR
technology:
Public mistrust towards certain vendors, like Flock.
The standing legal precedents that do not see the technology as a violation of the 4th amendment,
and that they’ve ruled there is no reasonable expectation of privacy for publicly displayed plates.
How police departments structure their data retention policies for the information that ALPR
cameras collect.
And how police departments go about sharing, or not sharing, the data they collect with outside
organizations and other authorities.
In regard to the last two points, Warych proposed reducing SPPD’s data retention
window of license plate captures to a 14-day trial period and promised it will never
share data with federal authorities.

He added that if the city were to enter a contract with Axon, it would be for one
year at a time. SPPD would also require a Memorandum of Understanding with the
company to govern how its data is accessed and shared.
In a one-pager provided by Axon in the Oct. 7 online meeting agenda, the
company stated that law enforcement agencies own their own ALPR data, and that
it only has access for support requests. Additionally, partner agencies own the
technology’s hardware such as poles and cameras.
Part of Warych’s support for ALPR technology in Sun Prairie rests on the records
the department has kept on “success stories.”
SPPD has tracked ALPR assistance dating back to May 2023. Since that time and
up until now, the technology has provided 83 license plate “hits” that have aided in
investigations ranging from property and vehicle theft to hit-and-runs and
homicides.
“When we’re standing in front of that person regarding (a) crime, the police officer
should ask themselves, ‘Have we done everything we possibly can to help that
victim?’ I believe license plate reading technology gives us another tool to help
that victim,” Warych said.
Many of the Sun Prairie residents who came to speak in opposition of the
proposed contract with Axon on Oct. 7 stressed that they do support SPPD. The
companies who make ALPR technology are a different story, however.
SPPD is not the issue
Eight individuals registered in opposition to the Axon contact on Oct. 7.
Additionally, 20 residents submitted forms in opposition to be read in their
absence as well.

One primary theme was a growing unease with the amount their day-to-day lives
are being recorded in society but not with SPPD’s operations.
Anthony Olsen was one such Sun Prairie resident who registered in opposition
that evening. He stated having worked in public safety for over 20 years and as a
dispatcher for the past decade.
“I want to reinforce that the public correctly fears mass surveillance… These are
not simply license plate scanners. Please call them what they are. They are
surveillance scanners capable of facial recognition, body motion style, and gait
analysis,” he said.
Olsen continued by saying that he saw more merit in ALPRs mounted on squad
cars than at fixed locations, and that collecting this information on residents
creates a database to be used by bad actors even if SPPD doesn’t intend to share
it.
Hannah Thomas was another Sun Prairie resident holding similar law enforcement
dispatching experience to Olsen's, as well as a similar support for police but not
ALPR technology.
“I also believe that just because something has been ruled legal doesn’t make it
morally correct. It doesn’t matter whose logo is on an ALPR pole… switching
lenders is not a fix for the community’s concerns,” Thomas said on Oct. 7.
Roland Ryan, another Sun Prairie resident in opposition, told the public safety
committee, “Americans disagree on a lot of things. But, this is one of the few
issues in which the vast majority of us agree.”

District 2 Alder and commissioner Alder Santiago Rosas also noted that the chief’s
commitments to not share ALPR data with federal authorities, like Immigration
and Customs Enforcement (ICE), has led to his “confidence” in the proposal.
It should be noted that four residents registered in favor of the switch to Axon on
Oct. 7, with the two supportive speakers being Sun Prairie Police & Fire
Commission members Terry McIlroy, a former alder, and Lorie Candelmo.
“I fully support the use of license plate readers within the city limits of Sun Prairie.
They have proven to be a great tool in getting evidence to solve some crimes
sooner than later,” McIlroy said.
A situation where ‘everyone is right’
At the end of the Oct. 7 meeting, Warych reiterated that SPPD has residents’
privacy as one of its utmost priorities along with protecting its residents.
He noted that officers already conduct audits on who is accessing ALPR records to
ensure officers within the city aren’t abusing the technology.
His perceived value of the technology and his officers' integrity led to his
recommendation for Axon cameras. He also recognized where the public is
coming from, however.
“I understand people's perspectives, I understand the lens that they look at things
and their viewpoints. Everybody is right in this situation,” he said. “I'm making the
recommendation to keep license plate reader technology. I believe that the city
has listened to residents.”

In an interview with The Star after the Oct. 7 meeting, Warych said his experience
with how Axon handles the city’s body-warn camera footage over the years also
has contributed to his confidence in their claims about privacy.
Even if his opinion on the topic differs from the majority of residents who have
spoken up on the issue, Warych appreciated the number of people it has engaged.
“If I got this level of engagement in all our community meetings, we would have
excellent communication in the community,” he said. “We would be better off
because we're working with them. We would develop more relationships. I think
we would solve more problems.”
