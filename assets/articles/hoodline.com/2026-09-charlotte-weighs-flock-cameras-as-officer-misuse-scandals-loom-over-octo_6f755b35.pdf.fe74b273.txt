By Liam Patterson
Published on September 30, 2026
Charlotte residents packed a meeting at Harding
University High School this week to confront Flock
Safety representatives directly over the company's
license plate reader technology, just as the Charlotte-
Mecklenburg Police Department works to select a
vendor to replace its aging camera system. The
gathering brought together Flock officials, elected
leaders, and community members at a moment when
trust in automated license plate readers has been
rattled by a string of local officer misconduct cases.
The meeting was part of a series of discussions
between Flock Safety and Charlotte officials as the city
considers a new police camera contract, according to
WSOC-TV. CMPD is searching for an automatic license

Flock Touts Missing Persons Cases While
Residents Push Back
At the meeting, Flock public affairs representative
Jalen Johnson presented the company's technology
and its track record, crediting the system with helping
bring home more than 10,000 missing people in 2025
and assisting more than one million investigations
across the country, the station reports. Johnson
described the cameras as an objective investigatory
lead that aids ongoing police work daily, and said Flock
stores license plate reader data for seven days before
deleting it.
Not everyone in the room was persuaded. Daniel Oria,
an activist who co-founded a local group warning
residents about privacy risks, argued that license plate
readers collect data on citizens regardless of whether
they are suspected of wrongdoing, and that the
systems operate at a national level, tracking people
without their consent. Flock representatives did not
confirm whether the company is submitting a proposal
for the local contract, and the number and identity of
other bidders remains unknown, per the same report.
Advocates for the technology pointed to a recent case
in Catawba County, where Flock camera data was used
to arrest Leroy Loper in connection with the death of
Rob Doyle, who had been reported missing days before
the arrest. But that success story sat awkwardly

alongside two local scandals that have defined the

Officer Misuse Cases Cast a Shadow
Over Procurement
A CMPD officer was arrested this year for allegedly
misusing a license plate reader system, and the case
has become central to the local conversation. The
North Carolina State Bureau of Investigation arrested
Officer Seth Daniel Elliott on July 29 and charged him
with misdemeanor illegal access of a computer after
he allegedly queried Flock Safety and state CJLEADS
databases to leak an undercover officer's vehicle
details to a suspected drug target in Watauga County,
according to WSOC TV.
A neighboring department saw a similar breach.
Mooresville Police Officer Elizabeth Snowman was
arrested on August 5 and charged with accessing
computers after an internal audit revealed she
allegedly performed 31 unauthorized Flock database
searches to track her boyfriend's ex-wife and logged
fraudulent case justifications, per FOX 29. The two
cases, arising within days of each other, gave
Charlotte-area residents concrete examples of the
exact abuse that critics like Oria warned against.
Notably, CMPD does not currently own its own
stationary Flock cameras, but that hasn't stopped its
officers from tapping into the broader network. The
department has maintained inter-agency data-sharing
agreements that enabled officers to conduct more
than 4,600 queries into Flock camera networks owned
by other North Carolina municipalities and law
enforcement agencies, according to the Cape Fear
Beacon. That volume of cross-agency access is part of
why auditor controls and data retention rules have

State Law Expands Access Even as Some
Cities Pull Back
The local fight is unfolding against a backdrop of state
action that cuts the opposite direction. North
Carolina's budget, enacted July 7, permanently
authorized automated license plate readers across
state highway rights-of-way, covering roughly 78% of
state roadways, and allows local police departments to
access the statewide network through State Bureau of
Investigation agreements, according to DeFlock Apex.
Separately, state law requires agencies operating ALPR
systems to adopt a written governing policy before
operation, and explicitly bars using the data to enforce
traffic infractions.
CMPD's use of license plate technology is not new. The
department first deployed license plate reader
systems in 2006 and expanded its network using
federal grants tied to the 2012 Democratic National
Convention in Charlotte, eventually operating roughly
100 fixed and mobile cameras capable of scanning up
to 3,000 plates per hour per device, per WCNC.
Even so, other North Carolina cities have moved to cut
ties with Flock entirely. The Asheville City Council voted
5 to 2 on August 25 to terminate its Flock Safety
contract and remove its 11 municipal license-plate
cameras following extensive public opposition,
according to Carolina Public Press. Hoodline has also
reported on Catawba rejecting cameras, and on a
nearby coastal department that fired an officer over
Flock misuse.

Mooresville also reflect a pattern seen nationwide. An
