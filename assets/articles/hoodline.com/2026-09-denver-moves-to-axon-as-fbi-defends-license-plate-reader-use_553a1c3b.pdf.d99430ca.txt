DENVER MOVES TO AXON
AS FBI DEFENDS LICENSE-
PLATE READER USE
By Priya Choudhury
Published on September 16, 2026
Denver is ending its relationship with Flock Safety and
moving its automated license plate reader contract to
Axon, Mayor Mike Johnston announced after months of
disputes with the City Council over privacy and federal
access to local data. City officials said the replacement
system would include safeguards for Denver residents,
including protections related to immigration
enforcement and other uses of the information,
according to Colorado Politics.
What prompted Denver’s change
The dispute centered on whether information
collected by Flock could enter a broader network
accessible to agencies outside Denver. Critics warned
that such access could expose residents to
immigration enforcement. Flock has said its customers
control the data and that the company does not work

What Patel told senators
At a Sept. 15 Senate Judiciary Committee hearing, FBI
Director Kash Patel defended automated license plate
readers as an investigative resource. He said the FBI
uses the technology in cases involving child predators
and abducted children, asking local officials or private
companies for help when investigators need to trace
an unidentified vehicle, according to The Denver
Gazette.
What research shows
Independent research offers a mixed assessment of
automated license-plate readers. According to the
National Institute of Justice, license plate readers have
been shown to increase arrests and recoveries of
stolen vehicles, but their broader effects on crime
prevention and investigations remain unclear because
rigorous evaluations have been limited. An Office of
Justice Programs study found that auto-theft and
robbery clearances improved after a large fixed
license-plate-reader network was installed, particularly
where cameras were concentrated. However, those
changes were not statistically significant in
multivariate analyses, according to the Office of
Justice Programs.
Patel characterized the FBI's role as limited to
particular investigations rather than continuous
monitoring. He said private companies and
municipalities operate the cameras and collect the
records, while federal investigators request footage or

assistance in specific cases. He also said the
technology must be used lawfully, the Gazette
reported. President Donald Trump had expressed
support for Flock cameras days earlier while
acknowledging concerns about their effect on rights,
according to the same report.
The privacy dispute remains
The American Civil Liberties Union says Flock operates
more than 120,000 automated license plate readers
nationwide and argues that the system creates
detailed records of vehicle movements. The group also
criticized Flock’s announced changes, which include
shortening standard retention from 30 days to seven,
requiring a case code for searches and expanding audit
assistance. The ACLU said the effectiveness of those
controls remains uncertain and that agencies can still
establish longer retention periods: ACLU.
The Institute for Justice’s Plate Privacy Project
separately lists more than 140 reported examples of
ALPR misuse, including alleged stalking, wrongful stops
and non-law-enforcement use, according to the
organization: Institute for Justice. Those allegations,
along with Denver’s decision to change vendors,
illustrate the unresolved divide between law-
enforcement agencies that view the cameras as
investigative tools and civil-liberties groups that want
stronger limits on collection and access.
