CHICAGO COPS RAN
23,000 FLOCK CAMERA
SEARCHES THROUGH A
CITY AGREEMENT
By Jo Marquez
Published on October 10, 2026
Chicago police officers ran more than 23,000 searches
through Flock Safety's license plate camera database
between May and July 2026, accessing the privately
owned network through a city agreement rather than a
direct contract with Flock Safety.
The arrangement, reported by FOX 32 Chicago, has
raised fresh questions about how the department
accesses and oversees Flock's surveillance data. Audit
logs obtained by the station and detailed by Wirepoints
show Chicago police searches of the camera network.

That gap sits inside a broader set of state rules written
specifically to constrain how license plate data gets
used. Under a 2023 Illinois law, automated license plate
reader operators are barred from sharing vehicle
location data with out-of-state or federal agencies
seeking to enforce immigration law or investigate
reproductive healthcare access, according to the
Illinois General Assembly. The restrictions address
concerns about immigration enforcement and
reproductive healthcare access.
Enforcement of that law, though, has leaned on audits
rather than built-in penalties. Illinois Secretary of State
Alexi Giannoulias began an investigation into Flock
Safety in August 2025 amid allegations that the
company had allowed U.S. Customs and Border
Protection and out-of-state police to access Illinois
plate data in violation of state law, NPR Illinois
reported. The outlet noted the 2023 privacy statute
carries no explicit financial or criminal penalties for
vendor violations, leaving enforcement reliant on
agency review and contract terms.
Flock Tightens Its Own Rules Nationwide
Flock Safety itself announced platform-wide changes
on August 13, cutting its default data retention window
from 30 days down to seven and requiring search case
codes from every law enforcement user by the end of
the year.
Momentum for tighter rules is building in Congress,
too. U.S. Representatives Raja Krishnamoorthi of Illinois
and Michael Cloud of Texas introduced the bipartisan
No FLOCK Act on September 15, which would withhold

states that fail to restrict automated plate reader use
to five specific public safety purposes: toll

searches, and felony warrant or crime investigations.
At the state level, the ACLU of Illinois backed House Bill
5151, the Auto License Plate Recognition Act, in March
2026 to set statewide standards for retention and
transparency. The organization has argued existing
Illinois law lacks comprehensive rules governing how
local departments query and store daily motorist
location data.
Existing State Camera Network Predates
the Controversy
None of this is happening on a blank surveillance slate.
Governor J.B. Pritzker signed a $20 million expansion of
the Tamara Clayton Expressway Camera Act in June
2022, deploying state-funded license plate readers
across Cook County expressways and Chicago's
DuSable Lake Shore Drive, according to the Illinois
State Police. The law, named for a postal worker
murdered on Interstate 57 in 2019, authorized those
cameras to investigate expressway shootings,
vehicular hijackings, and violent felonies.
Misuse cases elsewhere in the region have sharpened
the stakes. Prosecutors charged two former suburban
Chicago police officers with official misconduct in
October for allegedly misusing Flock camera networks
and law enforcement databases to conduct
unauthorized personal searches, according to reporting
tied to the same FOX 32 Chicago investigation. A
separate West Chicago case also involved alleged
misuse of a Flock network.

Trending in Chicago
The broader network helps explain why advocates keep
pushing back. The American Civil Liberties Union says
AI-powered cameras catalog vehicle make, model,
color, bumper stickers, and body damage, building
detailed movement profiles of ordinary drivers.
Suburbs Pull Back While Chicago
Expands Access
Some Chicago-area municipalities have reconsidered
their Flock arrangements. Evanston deactivated its
cameras in August 2025 and had begun the process of
canceling its contract, while Bartlett planned to let its
contract expire over a lack of support and concerns
about Flock's safeguards. Hoodline reported on
Bartlett's plan to let its contract expire.
The contrast is stark: Evanston deactivated its
cameras and began canceling its contract, while
Bartlett planned to let its contract expire; meanwhile,
Chicago police have run tens of thousands of searches
through a system accessed under a city agreement
rather than a direct contract with Flock. That contrast
between local camera pullbacks and Chicago police
access is likely to remain a central question as state
lawmakers, members of Congress and local officials
continue weighing how much oversight automated
surveillance networks need.
