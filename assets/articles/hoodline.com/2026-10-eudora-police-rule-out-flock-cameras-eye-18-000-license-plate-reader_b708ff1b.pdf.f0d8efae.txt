EUDORA POLICE RULE OUT
FLOCK CAMERAS, EYE
$18,000 LICENSE PLATE
READER
By Marcus Vance
Published on October 11, 2026
Eudora Police Chief Wes Lovett wants an automated
license plate reader posted at the city's only stoplight,
but he has already crossed one major vendor off the
list entirely: Flock Safety. The roughly $18,000 request is
working its way through city hall discussions as
officials also ask voters to approve a new public safety
sales tax on the November ballot.
Lovett told KSHB 41 Kansas City that the department is
researching an ALPR system with short-term data
storage and limited data access, and that Flock-
branded cameras have been eliminated from
consideration. He said Eudora can learn from the
mistakes other departments have made with license
plate readers, framing the search as a deliberate

That backlash has been hard to miss in eastern
Kansas. In August, the neighboring cities of Gardner
and Wellsville both terminated their Flock Safety
contracts after residents pushed back over mass
surveillance concerns and third-party vendor access to
camera data, according to the Johnson County Post.
Gardner had reportedly spent $33,000 on setup and
$13,000 annually before pulling the plug, a detail
Hoodline also reported in its own coverage of
Gardner's Flock cancellation.
A Statewide Legal Fight Over Flock
The scrutiny extends beyond small towns. In July, the
Kansas Justice Institute filed suit in Sedgwick County
District Court challenging Wichita's network of roughly
200 Flock cameras, arguing in the case Grimmett v.
City of Wichita that warrantless automated vehicle
fingerprinting functions as a de facto GPS tracker, as
reported by Reason Magazine. Civil liberties advocates
have also pointed to a February case the ACLU
documented in Lenexa, Kansas, where police
reportedly used license plate readers to monitor a
resident's vehicle movements and issue a be-on-the-
lookout alert after he published a newspaper op-ed
critical of local police.
Flock itself announced nationwide changes in August,
cutting its default data retention period from 30 days
down to seven calendar days and mandating internal
auditing systems for police agencies that use its
technology. The company's systems reportedly scan an
estimated 20 billion plates per month nationwide, per a
report from St. Louis Public Radio covered by
Hoodline's earlier reporting on license-plate-camera

privacy fears. Lovett has said the goal in Eudora is real-
time crime-solving, not tracking residents, and he
believes an ALPR could have helped solve several
crimes in the city over the past year, including locating
stolen vehicles and responding when an Amber Alert
passes through town.
Where The Camera Would Go
If approved, the reader would likely be installed at the
intersection of East 15th Street and Church Street,
home to Eudora's only stoplight and a frequently used
entrance and exit point for the city. Eudora Police are
considering the system's cost alongside its data
practices, and the roughly $18,000 purchase request is
part of the city's 2026 capital improvement plan. City
Manager Zack Daniel said the request is still being
discussed among city officials, and the Eudora City
Commission has not yet approved the purchase.
The request would not exist in a vacuum. The Douglas
County Sheriff's Office already operates its own
automated license plate readers across county
roadways to help deputies identify stolen vehicles,
missing persons, and wanted suspects, storing
encrypted data in a CJIS-compliant cloud platform.
Eudora's roughly 6,100 residents average two cars per
household with a commute time of about 23 minutes,
according to Data USA, underscoring how central car
travel is to a city sitting between Lawrence and the
Kansas City metro.
A Sales Tax Tied To Public Safety
Money is the other half of this story. Eudora's general
fund, supported by property taxes, would cover an
individually approved ALPR purchase, but the city is
also asking residents to consider shifting public safety
equipment costs onto a new 0.5% sales tax appearing

on the November ballot. That tax would replace an
expiring half-cent levy that has funded Community
Center debt, which ends December 31, so the city's
overall sales tax rate would not increase if voters
approve the measure.
The new tax is projected to generate about $350,000
annually. If it passes, city officials say it would free up
general fund budget space to hire three full-time
firefighters at a cost of about $327,000 a year, allowing
Eudora to provide around-the-clock fire protection for
the first time in the department's 100-year history.
Daniel said the sales tax strategy is meant to let public
safety operations grow alongside the community.
Lovett has also noted that the department's weapons,
tasers and other equipment are aging, adding another
layer to the city's broader public safety funding needs.
The debate over how departments should be allowed
to use license plate data has also become a statewide
political flashpoint. During an October debate,
candidates for Kansas Attorney General found common
ground on consequences for ALPR misuse: Kobach
supported job loss, fines and possible jail time for
officers, and both candidates supported holding
private companies accountable, as discussed in
Hoodline's coverage. Under current Kansas law,
captured license plate data and the specific physical
locations of ALPR systems are exempt from public
disclosure, while questions about data-retention limits
and audit policies remain.
