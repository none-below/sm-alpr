KEARNS LOSES LICENSE
PLATE CAMERAS AFTER
PUBLIC BACKLASH
By Owen Matthews
Published on October 02, 2026
Unified Police has pulled its automated license plate
readers out of Kearns after public outcry prompted
people to damage the equipment. The move has
already cost officers a tool they say they leaned on
nearly every day to track down stolen vehicles and help
with missing-person cases.
According to FOX 13 News Utah, the Unified Police
Department removed the readers from Kearns after
the backlash reached a point where people began
damaging the equipment. Kearns precinct chief Levi
Hughes pointed to that public outcry directly, saying it
was what prompted people to go and damage the
cameras in the first place. Hughes called the loss
significant, noting the system is heavily relied upon by
law enforcement across the country.

“This is a huge loss for us,” Hughes said, according to
the station's report. Before the cameras came down,
the department said officers were receiving alerts on
vehicles being sought almost daily. Now, Hughes said,
“we don't get them.”
What the Cameras Could and Couldn't
Do
The department said the readers had helped officers
find stolen cars and respond to missing-person cases
before they were taken down, the station's report
notes. The cameras photograph a vehicle's license
plate and the car itself but, as Hughes emphasized,
they don't have zoom-in technology that would let
police identify who was actually driving. Hughes also
acknowledged that privacy is a major concern for
people, a worry that has echoed well beyond Kearns.
That tension is playing out statewide. Automatic
license plate reader cameras generally capture images
of vehicles, their license plates and direction of travel,
according to KSL.com. The same report notes Utah law
enforcement officials have credited the cameras with
helping solve major cases, including a triple homicide in
Wayne County, the killing of a Santaquin police
sergeant in 2024, a threat against Salt Lake County
government buildings that same year, and a 2022
human trafficking case involving two young girls in
Beaver County.
Residents Torn Between Safety and
Surveillance
Kearns resident Maria Hernandez, a mother of seven,
told FOX 13 News Utah she worries about what could
be lost without the cameras in place. She said every
tool helps when it comes to protecting and finding
people, calling the license plate reader system an
important tool overall. Hernandez specifically pointed

to situations involving kidnapping or an injured child as
moments when the cameras become critical.
Her concerns reflect a broader statewide debate that
has intensified in recent weeks. Utah's Law
Enforcement and Criminal Justice Interim Committee
met on September 16 to discuss what privacy
protections might be needed around the technology,
according to KUTV. That outlet reported the discussion
wasn't about banning the cameras outright but rather
about building in safeguards. At that same meeting,
Cache County Sheriff Chad Jensen described how Salt
Lake City police used license plate reader photographs
to identify a vehicle involved in a fatal hit-and-run
crash.
A Nationwide Pattern of Pushback
License plate readers capture the locations of
thousands of Utah vehicles every day, and the cameras
have faced growing backlash nationwide over privacy
and data sharing, as reported by UPR. That same
report found dozens of communities across the
country canceled contracts or deactivated cameras
this year alone. In Idaho, the Ada County Sheriff's Office
removed seven Axon outpost cameras from Kuna on
Sept. 8, just two months after they were installed, after
concerns the devices were creating division among
residents, according to the Idaho Statesman. That
agency said it had no plans to reinstall the Kuna
cameras but would keep readers running in Eagle, Star
and unincorporated Ada County.
No Timeline for Kearns Cameras to
Return
For now, Unified Police will keep the Kearns cameras
down while waiting to see whether state lawmakers
make changes to the rules governing the technology,
