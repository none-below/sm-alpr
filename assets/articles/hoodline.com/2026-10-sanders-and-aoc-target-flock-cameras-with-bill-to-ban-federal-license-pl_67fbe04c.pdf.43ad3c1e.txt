SANDERS AND AOC
TARGET FLOCK CAMERAS
WITH BILL TO BAN
FEDERAL LICENSE PLATE
TRACKING
By Priya Choudhury
Published on October 02, 2026
Sens. Bernie Sanders and Jeff Merkley and Rep.
Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez unveiled legislation Friday
that would ban federal agencies from using automatic
license plate readers, pause federal grant funding to
state and local governments with contracts for the
technology, and let Americans sue the federal
government over its use. The bill, called the Ban Flock
Act, is the latest effort in Washington to rein in the
company behind a camera network that has sparked
privacy complaints and documented cases of police
misconduct.
Sanders, Merkley and Ocasio-Cortez unveiled the bill on
Friday, according to FOX40. Sanders, the independent
senator from Vermont, said the country cannot

become a surveillance state where, as he put it, AI
oligarchs profit from government tracking of
Americans' movements. Flock's cameras use artificial
intelligence to scan license plates and other vehicle
details, according to NPR, which reported the company
holds contracts with more than 5,000 law enforcement
agencies nationwide.
Beyond recording plate numbers, Flock's readers build
what the company calls a vehicle signature, according
to WNCT. That profile captures a car's make, model
and color along with distinguishing features like
bumper stickers, decals and accessories, the outlet
reported. The scale of that data collection is a central
reason the technology has drawn bipartisan scrutiny
this year.
Competing Estimates on Camera Count
Just how big Flock's footprint has become depends on
who's counting. WNCT reported the company's
network spans more than 120,000 cameras across the
country, a figure also cited by CBS News, which put the
network at 120,000 cameras in 49 states. ABC News,
however, reported that more than 135,000 automated
license-plate-recognition cameras have been
identified nationwide overall, with roughly four out of
five made by Flock, citing data from DeFlock.org. A
separate report from The Hill, also citing DeFlock, put
the total mapped cameras across the continental U.S.
at about 138,000, with roughly 82 percent made by
Flock.
Whatever the precise count, the backlash has been
substantial. The American Civil Liberties Union's Jay
Stanley told ABC News this is the biggest, most
grassroots privacy backlash he's seen in more than 20
years working on the issue. At least 30 localities had

deactivated their Flock cameras or canceled contracts
since the start of 2025, per NPR, while ABC News
separately reported more than 50 cities and counties
have canceled or deactivated ALPR cameras made by
Flock and other manufacturers.
Misuse Allegations Fuel the Pressure
Congress has already begun digging into specific abuse
cases. A congressional hearing this year, part of a
broader investigation into Flock, heard testimony from
lawyers, a sheriff, a cybersecurity engineer and a driver
who says she was wrongfully accused of a crime
because of the technology, according to WNCT. Sen.
Josh Hawley led what the kcur.org report described as
the first congressional hearing on AI-powered
surveillance camera manufacturers, asking the CEOs of
Flock, Axon Enterprise, Motorola and Verkada to testify.
Among the witnesses was Lindsey Isaacs, a Florida
woman who, according to CBS News, was wrongfully
accused of fleeing a fatal accident and jailed in solitary
confinement after her Dodge Durango was
misidentified on a Flock camera. Separately, KCUR
reported that five Indianapolis police officers were
charged this week with abusing Flock data to track ex-
partners and stalk strangers. ABC News also reported
that a Kentucky police officer was arrested after
authorities alleged he used Flock cameras to track his
ex-girlfriend more than 2,000 times.
A Crowded Field of Competing Bills
The Ban Flock Act isn't the only legislative effort aimed
at the industry. Rep. Thomas Massie has introduced
the Flock-Off Act, which would block municipalities and
localities from purchasing, installing, maintaining,
operating or upgrading automatic license plate readers
and biometric surveillance cameras, according to

--- page 4 ---
WNCT; that bill would affect companies beyond Flock
as well.
Hawley has introduced his own measure, the Stop
Flock Abuse Act, which CBS News reported would
establish safeguards for Flock cameras and similar AI-
powered surveillance technology. The bill would require
a documented paper trail whenever police search
license plate reader data and mandate that driver data
be deleted after 10 days unless it's tied to an active
investigation, per the same KCUR report. Sens. Raphael
Warnock and Katie Britt have also sent a letter this
month to Attorney General Todd Blanche urging the
Department of Justice to issue more guidance to
prevent misuse of Flock cameras and automatic
license plate readers, CBS News reported.
States and Cities Are Already Pulling
Back
Some of the strongest action has come outside
Congress. In Texas, Gov. Greg Abbott ordered a halt to
all state funding for Flock cameras in September,
according to the Houston Chronicle. Dallas responded
by planning to shut down 321 state-funded automated
license plate readers by the end of September while
keeping roughly 300 city-funded cameras running, the
paper reported. Two dozen Texas cities and counties
overall have taken steps to remove the readers or end
their Flock contracts, including Austin, which
discontinued its ALPR policy in July 2025 after ending
its pilot program with the company, and Bastrop,
whose city council voted unanimously on Sept. 8 to
decommission its Flock system.
Nationally, some cities have simply abandoned Flock's
solar-powered readers altogether, while others are

Flock's Response
Flock has pointed to its own policy changes amid the
pressure. The company touted a series of policy
changes in a letter to Hawley, and Dan Haley, Flock's
chief legal officer, said Americans' privacy should not
depend on one vendor's good judgment, leadership or
commercial incentives, according to FOX40. Flock did
not immediately respond to The Hill's request for
comment on the broader debate. Neither Flock's CEO
nor the heads of Axon Enterprise, Motorola or Verkada
attended the congressional hearing despite Hawley's
request that they testify.
