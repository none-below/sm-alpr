The Trust Project What is this? It is not a secret that we are already living in a surveillance state. Our small town, Moorhead, didn’t become part of this surveillance state on Monday, July 14, 2025, after the Moorhead Police Department announced the installation of eight Flock cameras using $29,300 from the city budget; however, it certainly sealed the deal. Moorhead put the cameras up between March 21 and May 29, joining Fargo and West Fargo. Use of these Flock cameras in Moorhead was unprecedented and was something that most residents didn’t even dream of. According to the Minnesota Department of Public Safety, cameras were installed at the following locations in Moorhead: 15th Avenue N. and Sixth Street N. 2900 block of 20th Street S. 700 block of 20th Street S. 2800 block of 34th Street S. 400 block of 34th Street S. 30th Avenue S. and Ninth Street S. 400 block of Main Avenue 18th Avenue S. and Eighth Street S. Letters Letter: Fargo, Flock safety and ICE Fargo police say they understand why people may have questions about the use of the Flock cameras. However, they made it clear they see the cameras as a crime-solving tool, not a spying tool. Feb 11 · By Grace Brennan Flock Safety markets its cameras as a tool for public safety. Faster investigations, stolen vehicles recovered, crimes solved, but license-plate readers don’t just capture suspected criminals. They collect location data on everyone who drives through town. That includes residents commuting to work, students heading to class, people visiting clinics, places of worship, or political events such as local protests . There are multiple media reports of this data being used for other purposes: " Police surveilled a woman who had an abortion for her 'safety' to charge her with a crime ; ICE, Secret Service, Navy all had access to Flock's nationwide network of cameras ; and Feds used local cop's password to do immigration surveillance with Flock cameras , Furthermore, there is a casual surveillance alliance between ICE and local police with CBP having access to more than 80,000 Flock AI cameras nationwide too. This is mass data collection by design, not a targeted investigative tool. License-plate readers, data brokers and information-sharing agreements quietly shape how power is exercised at the local level. The fact that Flock surveillance images are public records that can be requested by anyone is a huge safety risk and invasion of privacy of Moorhead residents. Both the Moorhead and Fargo Police Departments’ contract with Flock Safety has a provision called 5.3 Disclosure of Footage. "Subject to and during the retention period, Flock may access, use, preserve and/or disclose the footage to law enforcement authorities, government officials, and/or their third parties, if legally required to do so or if Flock has a good faith belief that such access, use, preservation or disclosure is reasonably necessary or comply with a legal process, enforce this agreement, or detect, prevent or otherwise address security, privacy, fraud or technical issue emergency situations." This basically means regardless of whether or not a local agency like Moorhead PD decides to share data, Flock reserves the right to share data at their "discretion." Chief Helmick's answer to this was, "I understand why that clause raises questions; it sounds broad, and it’s important to clarify what it means in practice. Flock includes that language to comply with legal obligations and handle emergencies, but Moorhead PD controls how data is shared. Even if Flock must act under a legal requirement, those situations are rare and typically involve court orders or urgent safety issues.” However, this is not how legal contracts work; it doesn't matter what the police department believes or Helmick thinks. This legal language in the contracts clearly gives Flock Safety the right to share data at its discretion. License-plate data can be misinterpreted or misused. Cars are borrowed, sold or shared. Plates are misread. Databases are breached. Oversight is often thin, retention periods are long, and audit mechanisms are weak or opaque. Once data exists, the temptation to use it grows, regardless of the original intent. None of this means Moorhead must choose between safety and civil liberties. Communities can invest in safety without building surveillance infrastructure that treats everyone as a potential suspect. Moorhead shouldn’t use technologies like Flock, which lack strict limits: no federal access, minimal retention, transparent reporting, independent audits, and clear public consent. Feb 12 · By Angela Randall For the safety of our proud Minnesotans , protesters, our LGBTQIA+ community, immigrants, and people of color, stop city contracts with Flock Safety. In the same way other Minnesota cities have done, the Moorhead City Council should also stop all contracts with Flock Safely. Moorhead residents should not be forced to live under a surveillance state. Sumudu V. Nagodavithana, Moorhead, is a member of The Forum's Readers Board.
