ITHACA, N.Y.—Acting Police Chief Ted Schwartz introduced new automated cameras with license plate recognition technology the Ithaca Police Department (IPD) and the Tompkins County Sheriff’s office intend to start using at a City Administrative Committee meeting last week . The technology is from a company called Flock Safety , a company based out of Atlanta, Georgia. The company provides automated license plate readers using AI-powered learning software that collects pictures of passing license plates and stores them for a set amount of time in case they are needed for criminal investigations. Hector Soliman-Valdez, Flock Safety’s director of local government affairs, guided the committee through a presentation about the technology’s capabilities and uses. He said the cameras are fixed at chosen locations around the department’s jurisdiction. Soliman-Valdez said cameras would be stationed either at existing electric poles or free-standing ones provided by the company, and connect to the internet using a cellular network, like a smartphone does. Once the cameras detect an image they send it to “the cloud,” he said, using encryption technology for added privacy. The information can then be searched for and retrieved by the police department to use in its investigations. The system’s machine learning enables it to identify a car’s manufacturer, model, color and license plate number from its image. Essentially, the cameras take pictures of every license plate that passes by and then store information collected from those pictures for 30 days. Schwartz said the new program is a reaction to years of hearing the community push for local police to be more efficient and use more technology during investigations. Schwartz said there would be 20 cameras installed around the city when asked by Alderperson George McGonigal. While the locations haven’t been selected, he said main roads and thoroughfares will be prioritized and that the department is “not looking to put these in neighborhoods.” He said the department intends to work with the company to “essentially hit major areas where we’re most likely to get the bang for our buck. […] Based on past investigations and traffic patterns, we’re just putting them where we think they’ll be most effective.” Schwartz cited the killing of Alan Godfrey , the abduction and subsequent killing of Tatiana David , a shooting on the Commons in July , a commercial burglary of motorcycles that took place in Ithaca but the suspects were caught in a different county, and the killing of Thomas Rath . While the technology wouldn’t have prevented these crimes from happening, Schwartz said the technology could have quickened the investigations into them. All the incidents involved fleeing vehicles. “These are, potentially, either generating a lead or allowing us to follow up on a lead faster,” Schwartz said. “Before we’d have to look for footage and pull it and review it. Now we’d have a searchable database.” Schwartz said the data will not be used for immigration enforcement and that the cameras are not designed to be used as speed traps. They also only take photos of the backs of cars, Schwartz said, which he and Soliman-Valdez argued mitigates the chance for bias in arrests. Flock keeps the data for 30 days, after which it is automatically deleted. To keep the data for later use via download, it must undergo the normal procedure for evidence storage in IPD, Schwartz said. The data is not allowed to be sold, Schwartz said. “Thirty days provides your police department with enough time to investigate a crime, even if it’s someone who’s out of town, [someone] doesn’t notice that the crime has occurred, or there’s no leads right away and a detective needs to be assigned,” Soliman-Valdez said. “But it protects privacy by automatically deleting the data at the 30-day mark.” Flock’s technology is unique from other systems that use similar technology according to Soliman-Valdez, as it proactively notifies law enforcement of a stolen vehicle sighting or of a vehicle involved in a crime in their jurisdiction. Data is stored securely on the cloud and a case number is required to perform a search for footage, Soliman-Valdez explained while outlining privacy protections the technology has. This means that administrators can regularly audit the system to ensure it’s not being misused, Soliman-Valdez said. The company is already in use by 51 other police departments statewide. Schwartz said the new program would be paid for by the Gun Involved Violence Elimination (GIVE) grant that was recentl y awarded joi ntly to the Tompkins County Sheriff’s Office and IPD , which contained a significant amount of funding for “hotspot policing tactics,” as characterized by the state’s Department of Criminal Justice Services. The county sheriff’s office will be employing the technology as well, Schwartz said, although to what extent is unclear. The company is already in use by 51 other police departments statewide. “They are mounted on poles, sometimes city poles or Flock poles, depending on the locations that are needed, and they are connected to the internet via the cellular network,” Soliman-Valdez said. “Once they see an image, they send it via encryption to the cloud, and then it’s retrievable by the police department if and when there’s an investigation.” “We can’t have them everywhere, we can’t have a city network of cameras on every corner, that’s not reasonable,” Schwartz said. “We’re going to work with Flock and essentially hit major areas where we’re most likely to get the bang for our buck. […] Based on past investigations and traffic patterns, we’re just putting them where we think they’ll be most effective.” Alderperson Jorge DeFendini asked Schwartz for more information on the camera locations, as he said he was still hesitant about the program due to concerns about “more mass surveillance throughout the city.” He said he appreciated the extent to which Flock went to prevent abuse of their technology, but also noted that there has been significant opposition to Flock. For example, the American Civil Liberties Union has stated that Flock’s system oversteps the bounds of acceptable license plate surveillance, arguing in a February article that “every new customer that buys and installs the company’s cameras extends Flock’s network, contributing to the creation of a centralized mass surveillance system of Orwellian scope.” DeFendini asked how the company can ensure that the license plate numbers that it pictures are accurate, since they will then be used as evidence for arrest or searches. Soliman-Valdez said the pictures are graded by the company’s program. If a picture falls below a certain threshold of clarity or accuracy, the machine learning places a “caution” tag on the file. All images are able to be reviewed and verified by a human before any action is taken, which Schwartz said will be codified in the department’s policy when Flock is introduced.
