ITHACA, N.Y. — As the City of Ithaca plans to install an AI-powered surveillance camera system, the company that makes them recently made national headlines for a series of failures to comply with other states’ permitting laws. A Feb. 27 Forbes article details numerous instances in which Flock Safety, the start-up that produces the cameras, failed to acquire the proper permits before installing its cameras in communities across the U.S. Forbes reported that the surveillance company, which is valued at over $4 billion, violated laws in at least five states. South Carolina and North Carolina have banned Flock employees from installing the camera system, citing the company’s permitting transgressions. In North Carolina, the technology itself is not subject to the ban, but it must be installed by a third party contractor. In December, the City of Ithaca inked a $69,300 one-year contract with Flock to install 22 cameras on city streets, with annual recurring payments of $66,000 upon renewal. Funding for Flock cameras was included in the city’s 2023 budget, despite some apprehension among the city’s Common Council. The cameras have not yet been installed in the city, according to Flock. Ithaca Police Department (IPD) officials, who spearheaded the initiative, said they are not deterred by the company’s permitting woes in other communities. Ithaca Police Chief Thomas Kelly said he stands by what he sees as a powerful crime fighting tool, provided installation complies with local laws “It is important that when installing [Flock] hardware in Ithaca that the devices are in compliance with our local regulatory requirements,” Kelly said in a statement. “We believe this will be an efficient technology used to improve community safety.” While cameras are not yet installed in Ithaca, Kelly told the Ithaca Community Police Board Wednesday that the police department is already using the company’s software, which allows it to access license plate data captured by municipalities already using Flock. Flock cameras create “vehicle fingerprints,” allowing police departments to search footage for specific cars based on color, type, make, state registration, plates, or even features like a roof rack or bumper stickers. Under the contract, Flock deletes all the footage it collects on a rolling 30 day basis. Authorized IPD officers are able to select, download, and archive footage at their discretion. Kelly raised the example of the kidnapping and death of Tatiana David while discussing instances when the high powered surveillance system could have made a difference in a criminal case. David was abducted from her home in Ithaca in April 2023. Her kidnapper, Michael Davis, drove David to Virginia, where she was later killed during a gunfight between Davis and Virginia State Police. Ithaca Mayor Robert Cantelmo said in a statement that the city has been monitoring the permitting process of Flock cameras. “Chief Kelly and the department have confidence this technology will promote greater community safety and reduce unnecessary police interactions,” Cantelmo said. Cantelmo said that the police department will conduct regular audits to review whether the usage of Flock remains compliant with the city’s policies. “Information gathered with this technology is overwritten after 30 days and users will need to cite specific reasons for these data to be queried,” Cantelmo said. “IPD will ensure there are regular audits to guarantee compliance with policy.” In response to a detailed list of questions from The Ithaca Voice , a spokesperson for Flock safety said the company employs nearly 50 people to handle permitting and public approvals of Flock cameras. He added that in 2023, Flock submitted over 25,000 permits for its cameras. “[S]tate, county, and city jurisdictional lines are not always as clear as we would like, and with the scale at which Flock operates we are going to make mistakes,” the spokesperson said. “Each time we have been notified of those mistakes, we try to respond and act quickly to fix them.” The spokesperson stated the installation timeline for installing the cameras “depends on how quickly the permits are approved.” Support Independent Journalism Independent local journalism is one of the few public goods that belongs to all of us, but it only survives when the community that relies on it steps up to sustain it.
