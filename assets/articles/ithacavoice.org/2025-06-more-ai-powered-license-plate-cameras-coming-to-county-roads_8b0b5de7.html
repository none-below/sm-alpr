More AI-powered license plate cameras coming to county roads

by Megan Zerez June 13, 2025June 13, 2025

ITHACA, N.Y. — Tompkins County began its rollout of AI-powered license plate reading cameras last month. The move comes 18 months after Ithaca officials, in conjunction with Tompkins County, approved a deal with anti-crime technology company Flock to install a network of devices.

So far, the county has activated about 20 of the 50 total devices under its contract. The devices are in addition to a separate network of 21 other cameras that are under the oversight of the Ithaca Police Department. Elsewhere in the county, village officials in Trumansburg and Cayuga Heights have also contracted Flock to install the devices on their roads.

For each vehicle that comes within range of a Flock camera, the company’s software records its license plate number, color, make, model and bumper stickers or decals. The software also records the date and time the car passes through a particular intersection, which allows police to track a vehicle’s route through the city or county. Some of the devices also have the ability to detect gunshots and other loud noises.

That data is then transmitted via wireless cell connection to a cloud-based database operated by Flock. Law enforcement officials can search the database to identify vehicles they suspect are tied to a crime. 

The county and city used funds from two different state grants to pay for the installation of the cameras and the annual $2,500-per-device fee that covers the use of the company’s software, data storage systems and support services.

While the Tompkins County Sheriff’s Office has ownership of the data, it is stored on Flock’s databases. Police can only search for data from the last 30 days — anything older is deleted.

Jurisdictions that install the cameras can also opt to share the data they collect with other law enforcement agencies. Tompkins County Sheriff Derek Osborne said Ithaca Police Department is among the agencies that have access to the county’s data.

Meanwhile, Ithaca officials have approved data access rights for some 106 other law enforcement agencies, including the Tompkins County Sheriff’s Office, according to a public-facing Flock data portal. License plate data collected by Ithaca’s cameras have been searched by various law enforcement agencies an average of 2.5 times a day since May 12. 

Most of the other law enforcement agencies with access to data from Ithaca’s streets are municipal police departments and sheriff’s offices in New York and Pennsylvania as well as a handful of state agencies, district attorney offices and college campus police departments. One federal agency can also access the city’s data — the United States Postal Inspection Service, the law enforcement arm of the U.S. Postal Service. No other federal agencies have been approved.

Some law enforcement agencies appear to have allowed federal immigration agents to use data from cameras in their jurisdiction. Both Tompkins County and the City of Ithaca have policies forbidding local law enforcement from aiding federal immigration enforcement efforts. Flock’s website states that the City of Ithaca prohibits the use of the data for such efforts.

During a presentation in 2023 before the Ithaca Common Council, representatives from Flock said the cameras are typically installed at busier intersections, not neighborhood streets.

Osborne declined to give locations for any of the county’s cameras, citing concerns that doing so would reduce the efficacy of the network and create a “road map” for criminals looking to avoid detection.

The Ithaca Voice has identified the location of at least one piece of Flock infrastructure. It consists of a roughly 12-foot-high black pole topped with a solar panel the size of a small laptop near the southwest corner of the intersection of Green and Cayuga Streets. A small label on the underside of the panel identified the device as part of the Flock system.

At a May 27 county legislature committee meeting, Osborne said law enforcement officials had successfully used data from the cameras to respond to an incident in Lansing after a business owner reported suspicious behavior.

“[The business owner saw] a male driver remove a female from his vehicle who appeared unconscious,” Osborne said. “We were able to use Flock to locate that vehicle and the registered owner and check on her. It ended up [that] it was just a medical emergency, but it could have certainly been different.”

Data obtained via a Freedom of Information Law request shows that Tompkins County sheriff’s deputies have been able to access Flock data from other municipalities as early as July 2024. Previous reporting by The Ithaca Voice shows that the Ithaca Police Department has had access to Flock software since at least early 2024.

When a law enforcement official searches for a license plate or vehicle on any of Flock’s databases — whether it’s their own camera network or one in another city or state — the software logs the user’s identity, the agency they represent, the number of cameras they chose to query and the time and the date they accessed any data. The user must also provide a brief description of why they needed to access the data, though the specificity of this response is left to the user’s discretion.

The company and its proponents have argued the devices help police more effectively address crime. The company has also touted certain safeguards meant to prevent targeting based on the race or appearance of the driver or passengers. Flock claims the cameras only capture photos of the rear of the car and do not employ facial recognition technology.

Critics, including the American Civil Liberties Union, have expressed concern that the cameras are contributing to the rapid expansion of police surveillance technology. An analyst with the group wrote that Flock has resisted calls for inspection and testing by an independent technology monitoring group.
