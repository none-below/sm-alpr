The announcement comes after at least four other Flock contract holders across Tompkins County have been ordered to end their relationship with the company earlier this year. In recent months, anti-surveillance activists have criticized the technology as an invasion of privacy; Flock’s nationwide network of cameras and AI software capture detailed data on every single vehicle passing through their range. Law enforcement officials can also search data collected by other police departments’ Flock cameras, provided they have opted into data sharing. The company has contracts with thousands of law enforcement agencies and security companies across the country. At one point, five law enforcement agencies in Tompkins County had contracts with Flock, with about 80 total cameras installed around the county between them. Cornell University Police Department has seven cameras positioned at intersections that represent major entry points to the campus. A growing number of university students have pushed for Flock cameras to be removed from campus. A member of Cornell’s Student Assembly introduced a resolution calling on the university to remove the cameras at that body’s Oct. 15 meeting. A Cornell University spokesperson told The Ithaca Voice that the university ended its contract with Flock on Oct. 14. David Honan, Cornell’s associate vice president for public safety, said in a statement that the university has “notified Flock Safety that it expects the cameras to be removed.” “The company is beginning that process, and Cornell expects the cameras to be removed as expeditiously as possible,” Honan said. Cornell’s campus police force is the last of the five Flock contract holders in Tompkins County to announce the end of its relationship with the company. After mounting public pressure, elected officials in the City of Ithaca , Tompkins County and the villages of Trumansburg and Cayuga Heights moved to end their law enforcement agencies’ contracts with Flock earlier this year. While all those municipalities have announced they would not renew their Flock contracts, not all removed or decommissioned the cameras immediately, prompting some activists to remove or vandalize several devices. It’s unclear whether the decision to remove the cameras is connected to the recent national outcry over a civil lawsuit filed by a former student alleging she had been raped by seven members of a former fraternity in late October 2024. “We remain committed to maintaining an open and accessible campus environment while balancing privacy with campus safety,” Honan wrote.
