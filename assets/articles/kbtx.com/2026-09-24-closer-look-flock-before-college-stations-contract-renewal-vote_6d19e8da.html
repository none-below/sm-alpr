A closer look at Flock before College Station’s contract renewal vote

DeFlock BCS spent months reviewing local Flock search records. Here’s what they found ahead of Thursday’s vote
By Lillian Kosh
Published: Sep. 24, 2026 at 4:12 AM UTC|Updated: 15 hours ago
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

BRAZOS COUNTY, Texas (KBTX) - Thursday night, College Station City Council will decide whether to renew the city’s contract with Flock Safety. Ahead of that vote, KBTX spent the past several days trying to get answers from every side of the debate over these cameras: Brazos County Sheriff’s Office, Bryan police, College Station police, and the local group leading the pushback.

That group is DeFlock BCS. Among that group are John Hamilton, Ivan Svrcek, and Kody Fox, who have spent months auditing Flock search logs, records, and contracts across Bryan and College Station.

Brazos County’s Sheriff sat down with KBTX one-on-one, on camera, last week. Bryan and College Station police have both declined repeated requests for an on-camera interview. Bryan agreed to speak with KBTX off camera and provided a written statement. College Station provided only a brief written statement and directed further questions to a public records request.

Scale of the local Flock network

Three local agencies operate Flock camera networks of very different sizes. Brazos County’s Sheriff’s Office runs 10 cameras. Bryan Police operates 17. College Station’s network is by far the largest, with 84 cameras.

Ivan Svrcek said he first learned about the security vulnerabilities in Flock’s cameras and became concerned that “not enough people were paying attention to how powerful these cameras are.” John Hamilton started tracking camera locations using the national DeFlock map before speaking at Bryan-College Station city council meetings in February. Kody Fox said he began digging in after Flock cameras started appearing outside his own workplace.

Do the cameras only capture vehicles?

Bryan and College Station police both describe their Flock systems as motion-activated, meaning the cameras are triggered by any movement, not just passing cars. Both departments say that despite this, officers can only search vehicle-related images.

College Station police told KBTX in a written statement:

“Our cameras are motion-activated, similar to a home security system or doorbell camera that uses motion sensing to capture images... The only still images that officers with the College Station Police Department are able to search for within the system are vehicle-related images.”

COLLEGE STATION FLOCK TRANSPARENCY PORTAL

However, DeFlock disputes that officers are limited to vehicle images alone.

“With these cameras, every single person is getting photographed by them,” Hamilton said. “It doesn’t matter what you’ve done. You drive by, that camera will take a photo of not only your vehicle, but of any person that walks by as well.”

DeFlock says its review of College Station’s audit logs found the department using a feature called “freeform search” — which allows officers to search using descriptive details, not just license plates.

“In July of this year, College Station was using the object class people search to search for things like black backpack, blue pants,” Hamilton explained.

According to DeFlock, that pattern of use appears throughout the entire window of records they reviewed, October 2025 through July 2026, not as an isolated instance.

Bryan Police told KBTX it briefly tested a similar freeform-search capability in 2025, described it as a short trial, and said the department no longer uses it.

The Bryan immigration search

Reviewing Bryan Police’s audit logs, DeFlock found a search from January 2026 logged as “immigration retention.”

All three local agencies — Brazos County, Bryan, and College Station publish Flock transparency portals that list immigration enforcement as a prohibited use of the technology.

BRYAN PD FLOCK TRANSPARENCY PORTAL

Bryan Police, in a written statement, said the search was connected to investigating a threat, not immigration enforcement:

“The department recently responded to questions from KBTX regarding an audit containing a reference to ‘Immigration Enforcement.’ The reference relates to the department’s investigative activity surrounding a January 27, 2026, protest at Villa Maria Road and South Texas Avenue. Prior to the protest, BPD identified and investigated a confirmed social media threat of violence connected to a known individual in Bryan. Department personnel utilized available investigative resources to assess the threat and help protect those attending the event.”

Thousands of searches from outside agencies

DeFlock’s review also found a large volume of searches of Brazos-area camera data from agencies outside the county.

“College Station has had Flock cameras since October of 2025. And in that time, [Bryan and] College Station Police have run about 30,000 to 40,000 searches in that timeframe,” Hamilton said. “However, our network has been searched close to 3 million times. Because we share our data with a majority of places in Texas, anyone can search our data at any time.”

Brazos County Sheriff Wayne Dicky, in his interview with KBTX last week, said similar numbers involving his department’s data can be misleading:

“If an organization searches a statewide search, every system that is connected counts that as a search, even though their data may not have been involved at all... it’s a little misleading that you see these big numbers of other agencies searching Brazos County data.”

The usage creep

DeFlock also raised a broader concern: that Flock’s technology and capabilities are expanding faster than local policy and oversight can keep up.

“The usage creep of the entire system has proven to be too fast for us to keep up with, too fast for policy to keep up with. Because most of the time, we’re doing the informing of what the new technology is because it just changes so fast. And that’s an issue because our policymakers cannot keep up with that,” Fox said.

BCS DeFlock pointed to Flock’s continued expansion into new products, including drones, vehicle-mounted readers, and the ability to route existing traffic cameras through Flock’s software, as evidence the technology keeps evolving beyond what was originally presented to city councils.

Oversight and accountability

DeFlock BCS also raised concerns about how thoroughly local audits actually catch misuse, given how much of the network’s data is shared statewide:

“When we’re getting our data searched by 14,000 times plus a day by outside entities, none of that data is readily auditable in any meaningful fashion,” Svrcek said.

KBTX’s reporting found that the Brazos County Sheriff’s Office audits its Flock searches monthly. Bryan Police told KBTX it began regularly auditing its searches only this year—starting quarterly and, more recently, monthly.

BRAZOS COUNTY SHERIFF’S OFFICE

Bryan first installed Flock cameras more than two years ago, in January 2024. Both departments say they have found no confirmed cases of misuse. College Station has not provided KBTX with details on its own audit process.

However, DeFlock BCS concerns extend beyond how local departments use the cameras to how secure the cameras themselves are. The group points to a November 2025 white paper by cybersecurity researcher Jon “GainSec” Gaines, which detailed several vulnerabilities in Flock’s hardware.

DeFlock also pointed to a separate incident in December, in which security researchers found more than 70 Flock cameras nationally streaming live video to the open internet due to a misconfiguration issue.

“Anyone in the world could go to a camera’s IP address and see that camera live streaming with no password authentication at all,” Hamilton added.

Flock has publicly disputed characterizations of these findings as overstated.

Whats next

With College Station’s vote looming, DeFlock says residents should understand what does — and doesn’t — happen if the city walks away from its Flock contract.

“Our contract very clearly states that the Flock cameras are property of Flock. So even if a contract is canceled, we don’t have the right to remove them. So they will stay up and stay collecting data and sending that data back to Flock.”— John Hamilton, DeFlock BCS [timecode ~34:50–35:04]

Hamilton claims other cities that have ended Flock contracts have reported similar experiences, with cameras remaining active for weeks or months until Flock physically removes them.

KBTX asked College Station police about this directly; the department did not address the question in its written statement. However, Brazos County Sheriff Wayne Dicky told KBTX that, in his department’s case, both the cameras and any collected data belong to the county and would be removed if its contract ended.

College Station City Council votes tomorrow night on whether to renew its Flock Safety contract. Residents who wish to speak at the meeting must register before 1 p.m. Thursday. The meeting begins at 6 p.m. at College Station City Hall.
