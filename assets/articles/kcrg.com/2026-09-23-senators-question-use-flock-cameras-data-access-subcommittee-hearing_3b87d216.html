Senators question use of Flock cameras and data access at subcommittee hearing

The CEOs of four surveillance companies didn’t appear at a hearing where senators questioned the technology.
By Tim Darnell and InvestigateTV Staff
Published: Sep. 23, 2026 at 10:10 PM UTC|Updated: 17 hours ago
Add Us On GoogleAdd as a preferred source on Google

WASHINGTON, DC. (InvestigateTV) - Facing mounting federal scrutiny following months of InvestigateTV and Atlanta News First reporting, the leaders of America’s major surveillance companies were no-shows Wednesday for a Senate hearing probing how police officers have abused automated license plate readers to stalk civilians and fellow officers.

The hearing before the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism, titled “Always Watching: Flock’s Nationwide AI Surveillance Network,” came directly in the wake of an extensive series by InvestigateTV.

“The reporting on what these cameras can do is really pretty wild,” said U.S. Sen. Josh Hawley, R-Missouri, who chairs the subcommittee. “The Constitution of the United States is still just as valid and applicable today as it was almost 250 years ago. We have to find a way to protect and enforce the Bill of Rights for every law-abiding citizen of the United States.”

Hawley noted the hearing’s purpose was to “figure out what is going on” with automated surveillance cameras, adding, “If you haven’t heard of Flock, Flock has probably heard of you.”

Hawley pointed out that none of the chief executives invited to testify appeared at the hearing: Garrett Langley, CEO of Flock Safety; Rick Smith, CEO of Axon Enterprise; Greg Brown, CEO of Motorola Solutions; and Filip Kaliszan, CEO of Verkada.

But Flock Safety would send an email response to Hawley ahead of the hearing on September 8, saying it agreed “that the rules that protect Americans should be set by law” and not by the “policies of any single company.”

“Flock does not believe that Americans’ privacy should depend on one vendor’s good judgment, its current leadership, or its commercial incentives,” said Dan Haley, Flock’s chief legal officer. “That is why Flock has consistently supported clear, sensible legislation governing how this technology is used.”

InvestigateTV and Atlanta News First investigator Brendan Keefe’s reporting uncovered at least 19 instances over a single year in Georgia alone where law enforcement officers were arrested or relieved of duty for misusing Atlanta-based Flock Safety’s surveillance database for unauthorized personal reasons, including tracking estranged romantic partners and stalking fellow officers.

Flock Safety operates more than 120,000 automated license plate reader (ALPR) cameras across the country, capturing millions of vehicle movements daily.

The data is retained and searchable by law enforcement agencies across jurisdictions for 30 days.

While Flock launched an AI-powered “audit assistance” tool earlier this year to flag suspicious activity for municipal supervisors, reporting showed that victims often only discovered improper searches through independent public records requests and third-party tools like haveibeenflocked.com.

Privacy advocates and public records watchdogs also raised alarms after Flock removed officer names from external network searches and stripped license plate numbers entirely from system audit logs, severely limiting the public’s ability to verify whether their vehicles had been searched.

“This is still the United States of America, and it shouldn’t be that when you walk out your door in the morning, you just have to agree, in a sense, to accept the fact that you’re gonna be on camera, morning, noon, and night,” Hawley added. “You’ll be tracked by anybody and everybody in the world. You’ll have no privacy whatsoever.”

U.S. Sen. Dick Durbin, D-Illinois, echoed calls for federal intervention, emphasizing the urgent need to address constitutional boundaries.

“We have got to find a way to balance in America privacy and security,” Durbin said. “There is a balance. Congress needs to lead the way.”

Bipartisan concern was further highlighted when U.S. Sen. Katie Britt, R-Alabama, entered into the official record a joint letter with Sen. Raphael Warnock, D-Georgia, expressing serious concerns over potential civil liberties violations stemming from the widespread deployment of license plate readers.

Innocent woman jailed for 13 days after errant camera match

Among the witnesses who testified was Lindsay Issacs, a Florida woman interviewed during Keefe’s reporting who recounted how an automated camera match upended her life.

Issacs was arrested in Florida on charges of causing a fatal vehicle accident that killed three people after Florida troopers built their case around an ALPR hit. She spent nearly two weeks behind bars before a subsequent investigation proved law enforcement had identified the wrong vehicle.

“A Flock camera captured my vehicle a few miles from a terrible crash I had nothing to do with that led to 13 days in jail,” Issacs testified.

Other witnesses testified modern, interconnected cloud networks and artificial intelligence make today’s camera systems fundamentally different from traditional license plate readers.

“We are a long way from a simple automated license plate reader,” Hawley noted, following testimony from Alasdair Whitney, legislative counsel for the Institute for Justice, who pointed out that cameras linked to cloud networks represent a drastic shift.

Whitney urged Congress to establish firm federal guardrails, including requiring law enforcement to obtain search warrants prior to scouring historical license plate databases.

“The Fourth Amendment is a floor, not a ceiling,” Whitney said.

Sheriff Ross Teeple of Pinal County, Arizona, testified the latest AI-powered cameras pose a threat to constitutional rights. While Teeple noted the technology’s utility in criminal investigations — citing a local case where ALPRs successfully tracked an aggressive driver who pushed a woman’s car into oncoming traffic — he voiced alarm over transparency.

Teeple said he grew concerned after asking Flock Safety about the use of AI in its equipment. Though initially told no AI was utilized, he later found company statements contradicting that claim.

“The response I received was chilling,” Teeple testified. “I was told, ‘That wasn’t the AI you were asking about.’”

Benn Jordan, an independent security researcher who discovered dozens of Flock cameras streaming openly online, warned lawmakers about systemic digital vulnerabilities.

“Why create this much data when we can’t secure it?” Jordan asked, urging Congress to ensure any regulations placed on Flock apply universally across the surveillance industry, including competitors like Axon.

Civil liberties advocates call for federal guidelines

Chad Marlow, senior policy counsel for the American Civil Liberties Union, told the committee venture capital investment has transformed public-safety tools into an unchecked surveillance industry.

“The explosion of ALPR use and its tracking of innocent people reflects a view that if ALPR use is scaled up, it could produce the massive profits venture capitalists seek,” Marlow said. “Welcome to the surveillance-industrial complex, America.”

Marlow testified that Flock’s existing safeguards fail to address the core problem of systemic misuse and urged Congress to use its spending powers to halt the technology entirely.

“The time has come for Congress to ban any law enforcement entity who receives federal funding from using ALPRs,” Marlow said. “This prohibition should be without exceptions... when it comes to protecting core American civil rights and civil liberties, if you make one exception, you invite others, and that is a slippery slope we must avoid at all costs.”
