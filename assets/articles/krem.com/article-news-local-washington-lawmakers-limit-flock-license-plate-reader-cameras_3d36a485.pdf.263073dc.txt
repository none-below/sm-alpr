Lawmakers in Olympia push for tighter controls on automatic license
plate readers, citing privacy concerns and bipartisan support for new
regulations.
OLYMPIA, Wash. — For the first time, lawmakers in Olympia say automatic license plate
reader cameras, including the popular Flock system, are out of control and that they will try
to put new guardrails on how the data is collected and used.
Public hearings were held Tuesday for two bills aimed at regulating the technology, which has
rapidly expanded across Washington over the past few years.

Now, more than 80 jurisdictions statewide use the technology, including the Centralia Police

“It is simply data points in time,” said Centralia Police Chief Andy Caldwell. “We don’t know
where you’re going, who is in the car, we have no idea where you’re coming from — it’s just a
data point we use for investigations.”
But last October, the University of Washington Center for Human Rights reported federal
agents accessed Flock data to pull over immigrants. Since then, immigrant advocacy groups
have pushed to take the cameras offline.
“Members we work with directly are telling us we don’t know if it’s safe to go to work that
day, safe to go to school,” said Guillermo Zazueta with OneAmerica. “And we have to do
something about it.”
Bills would limit federal access and shorten data retention
Washington currently does not have rules regulating Flock cameras, while states like Utah,
Colorado, and at least 14 other states do.
The proposed bills would limit federal agencies from accessing Flock data and require
agencies to delete raw data, including pictures of vehicles and their license plates, within 72
hours of collection.
Opponents say that window is too short to investigate crimes.
“The 72-hour retention limit is too short for real-world investigations,” said Pasco Police Chief
Ken Roske. “A modestly longer window closer to 30 days with strict access controls and audit
requirements would protect privacy and enable investigators to identify suspect vehicles.”
Lawmakers say concern over surveillance is bipartisan
Senators who co-sponsored the bill said concerns about how the data can be used extend
across party lines.
“We’ve seen reports that have shown how the technology has been wielded against
community members without their knowledge,” said Sen. Yasmin Trudeau, a state senator
from Tacoma.
Senator Jeff Holy, a Republican representing Spokane County, also warned that the cameras
can reveal intimate details of a person’s daily life.
“What time you drop your kids off at school, when and where you attend religious services,
how often, which kind of doctor you visit,” Holy said. “It struck me these cameras vacuum up
all data regardless of if there is criminal wrongdoing.”
Nearly everyone who testified during the hearing agreed Washington needs more guidelines
— with debate centered on how strict those rules should be.
