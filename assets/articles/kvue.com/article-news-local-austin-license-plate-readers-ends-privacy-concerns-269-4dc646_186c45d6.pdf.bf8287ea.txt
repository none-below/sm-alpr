AUSTIN, Texas — Austin is doing away with its license plate reader program at the end of the
month after mounting concerns over privacy and data collection. 
Automatic License Plate Readers, or ALPRs, are devices that rapidly snap photos of license
plates, then store the images in a database that can link them to potential crimes or missing
persons. On Wednesday, some council members and local coalitions addressed how they
believe APLRs are being weaponized as mass surveillance tools. 
"There is not enough companies out there that can guarantee that our data will be protected,"
said Annette Price, the executive director of Grassroots Leadership. "It is best to not collect it
in the first place."

The city has contracts with Flock Safety and Axon to run the program. The city has 40
cameras installed and roughly 500 mounted on police vehicles. 
The arguments made by city leaders is that data collected could be used to track down people
seeking abortions and gender-affirming care. 
"That's the concern here: the dramatic negative impact on civil liberties and privacy for our
community with programs like this," District 7 Councilmember Mike Seigel said.
KVUE reached out to Flock Safety on Wednesday about the concerns and the city's decision
to end its contract. In a statement, the company said its technology has provided a public
safety benefit to Austin that has resulted in 165 arrests, about 134 prosecutions and one
missing/endangered person found. 
"Flock has designed our technology with accountability mechanisms built into the system.
Every search that is conducted on an agency's cameras is preserved permanently in an audit
trail - enabling oversight and transparency for every search. We are committed to ensuring
every jurisdiction can use Flock products in a way that reflects their values. Community safety
does not need to come at the expense of community values," said Holly Beilin, Flock's director
of Communications.
Beilin also noted that Flock doesn't have contracts with Immigration and Customs
Enforcement (ICE) or the Department of Homeland Security. She said how the technology is
used and which federal agencies law enforcement collaborates with is entirely up to each
police department. 
During a city work session Tuesday, City Manager T.C. Broadnax decided to axe the program
and withdraw it from Thursday's agenda, when a vote on extending the program was to be
considered.
Austin Police Chief Lisa Davis said she understood the concerns voiced by community
members, but credited the technology as one that filled the gap with the department's staffing
shortage and crime reduction. 
"As your Austin Police Department, we can be good stewards of this and do good with it and
make some exceptional, exceptional dents in our violent crime and in what it truly takes to
make this, Austin, the safest city in America," Davis said on Tuesday. 
KVUE reached out to APD on Wednesday for a comment regarding the decision. They
provided the following statement on Thursday.
"The Austin Police Department fully supports the City Manager’s decision to withdraw the
item related to Automatic License Plate Readers (ALPRs). Listening to the concerns raised by
our community members and elected officials has given us an important opportunity to
pause and reassess the path forward.
This includes increased focus on education, community engagement, active listening, and
further research to ensure we are addressing all perspectives. The City of Austin and Austin
Police Department deserves access to the most effective technology, tools, and resources to
keep our community, and our officers safe.

The use of technology plays a critical role in how cases are solved and removing dangerous
criminals from our streets. It is essential for us to responsibly look at ways to expand access
to investigative technologies.
I look forward to the continued conversations that will be taking place as we all work
together advancing our technology use in a way that aligns with all our values."
It's not clear what will happen to the cameras – whether they will stay installed or be
disconnected. Councilmember Vanessa Fuentes said it's a discussion that will need to take
place with Broadnax. 
Whether the ALPRs will be substituted is another factor Fuentes added that the city would
need to look into. The council member pointed to when the council shelved LPR technology
back in 2020.
"We'll continue to ensure that we are pursuing justice for, for all victims in our community,"
Fuentes said.
District 9 Councilmember Zo Qadri noted while ALPRs are axed for now, there is a possibility
that the item could be brought back to the council in the next five to six months. However,
Qadri believes the city will be able to push back against it when the time comes.
