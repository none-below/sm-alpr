Opinion: Los Gatos should keep its ALPR
cameras
ADAM MAYER October 2, 2026
T
he recent debate over automated license plate reader technology, including Flock cameras, has
generated significant opposition at Los Gatos Town Council meetings. As a longtime resident who
grew up in Los Gatos, I would like to offer a different perspective.
Los Gatos is consistently rated one of the safer communities in our region. I would like to keep it that way.
Maintaining public safety requires giving our Police Department effective tools to prevent and investigate
crime. I believe the Town should retain its existing ALPR network and consider expanding it where there
is a demonstrated public safety need.
This discussion is particularly important in the context of the potential expansion of police services into
neighboring Saratoga. If the Los Gatos-Monte Sereno Police Department ultimately takes on
responsibility for serving a larger geographic area and population, it will be more important than ever that
officers have the technology and resources necessary to investigate crime effectively and maintain public
safety across the communities they serve.
We can look to San Francisco for evidence of how this technology can help. In a recent San Francisco
Examiner commentary, Supervisor Matt Dorsey highlighted the dramatic decline in vehicle break-ins
following San Francisco’s deployment of a citywide ALPR network. Reported theft-from-vehicle incidents

fell from 19,841 in 2023 to fewer than 5,400 in 2025, a 73 percent reduction. While ALPRs may not
account for the entirety of that decline, the strong correlation between their deployment and the
reduction in smash-and-grab thefts provides compelling evidence that this technology can be an
effective tool for law enforcement.
Los Gatos has fortunately never experienced property crime on the scale San Francisco has. But that is
precisely why we should be careful about taking our community’s safety for granted.
I was particularly struck by some of the recent testimony from younger residents opposing the cameras.
Having grown up in Los Gatos myself, I understand how easy it is to grow up in a safe community and
assume that level of security is simply the natural state of things. I probably lacked some of that broader
perspective at the same age. Public safety does not perpetuate itself. It requires continued investment,
effective policing and appropriate tools.
There are legitimate concerns about privacy and misuse, and those concerns deserve safeguards. The
recent case involving a San Jose police officer who improperly accessed Flock data and provided
information to a relative involved in a domestic violence case is a disturbing example. But the officer was
terminated, and San Jose subsequently tightened access to sensitive law enforcement systems and
strengthened auditing procedures.
That incident demonstrates why strong access controls, auditing, transparency and meaningful
consequences for misuse are essential. It does not demonstrate that the technology itself should be
abandoned.
This does not need to be a choice between privacy and public safety. Los Gatos can establish strong
safeguards for ALPR technology while allowing our Police Department to use a valuable tool to protect
the community. And if our police department is preparing to serve an even larger area, now is not the time
to unnecessarily limit the tools available to its officers.
*Adam Mayer is a Los Gatos Planning Commissioner but writes here as a private individual. The piece has
