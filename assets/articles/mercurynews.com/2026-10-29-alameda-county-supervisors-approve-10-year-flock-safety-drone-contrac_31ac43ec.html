    Alameda County supervisors approve 10-year Flock Safety drone contract

    Docking stations aim to speed up emergency responses, but privacy advocates warn of potential surveillance overreach

    By Chase Hunter | chunter@bayareanewsgroup.com | Bay Area News Group
    PUBLISHED: October 29, 2026 at 4:30 AM PDT | UPDATED: October 29, 2026 at 6:16 AM PDT
    Getting your Trinity Audio player ready...

    The latest investment in surveillance technology in Alameda County was approved Tuesday, despite privacy concerns with the company involved, Flock Safety, which has come under fire throughout the Bay Area for its automated license plate reader camera system.

    The Alameda County Board of Supervisors on Tuesday approved a 10-year contract for two drone docking stations for Flock Safety’s Drone as First Responder program for the Alameda County Sheriff’s Office, an effort meant to quicken response times and feed real-time information to law enforcement officers.

    “The drones are giving you that extra set of eyes and ears, it reduces the number of officer-involved shootings,” said Brian Hofer, executive director of Secure Justice, a privacy and civil rights nonprofit. “They can go into backyards, fly inside of buildings looking for suspects, and be used in high-risk search warrants like raiding a drug house.”

    The Sheriff’s Office will install two drone docking stations at the Alameda County Fire Department’s Station 23 in Cherryland that will house, launch and deploy drones for ongoing crimes and emergencies to enhance tools for the Sheriff’s Office’s Real Time Information Center.

    Drones are of particular importance to the county’s vast unincorporated area where a limited number of law enforcement officers are available and hilly terrain can slow responses to emergencies, said Alameda County Sheriff’s Sgt. Justin Brannon.

    “We’ve provided real-time information during active incidents that helped keep both deputies and civilians safe,” Brannon told Bay Area News Group last year. “In many cases, we’ve de-escalated situations because of what the drone allowed us to see from above without rushing into unknown danger.”

    Drones have increasingly been adopted as a key public safety tool in the Bay Area over the past five years. In February 2024, Fremont’s police and fire departments launched the nation’s first joint Drone as First Responder program after pilot testing during the previous two years.

    San Francisco followed soon after, launching its own Drone as First Responder program in May 2024 after residents voted to streamline surveillance rules through Proposition E. The use of drones by the San Francisco Police Department has skyrocketed since then, with its fleet growing from six to 63, and monthly deployments rising from 35 to more than 600, according to SFPD.

    That growth could be cause for concern if it spreads to Alameda County, Hofer said.

    “Where I could see it being a problem is if the volume gets that high. I don’t think the county is that operational yet, but you could look at San Francisco or San Diego where these programs got started early, and they went from 50 flights a year to 100 flights a day,” Hofer said. “So in that sense, there’s a lot more people voicing opposition because of how ubiquitous DFR programs are.”

    With privacy concerns related to Flock Safety’s automated license plate reader camera system still a topic of debate, Hofer said aerial surveillance drones are materially different because of the scope of their use – ALPRs are fixed cameras that constantly feed data, whereas drones only capture data during deployment.

    Still, aerial surveillance drones have received pushback for privacy violations. Sonoma County was sued by the American Civil Liberties Union in June 2025 after drones were used for code enforcement of residents’ homes without a warrant.

    Permit Sonoma, the county’s planning agency, said drones would be used to address unpermitted cannabis grows in hard-to-access rural areas, but the program has since become a “runaway spying operation with no accountability, trampling residents’ right to privacy in and around their homes and targeting people for a wide array of unrelated code violations that have resulted in millions of dollars in excessive fines,” according to the ACLU of Northern California.

    The lawsuit was settled in September with Sonoma County agreeing to stop using drones to surveil residents’ homes without a warrant or consent from the homeowner, in addition to new regulations regarding their use.

    In Alameda County, data captured from drones must be deleted within 60 days from county servers, unless that data is involved in a criminal investigation or an internal affairs investigation, according to the Alameda County Sheriff’s Office.

    “I’ve seen firsthand how drones can save lives and reduce risk to both deputies and the public. However, none of that is worth it if we lose public trust,” Brannon said. “The policy is not just a checklist, it is a commitment to using this technology responsibly and with respect for everyone’s rights. Privacy is something we protect, right alongside public safety.”

    The evolution of surveillance technology has reignited debates across Bay Area cities on the line between privacy and public safety, especially with Flock’s ALPRs. More information will need to be gathered on law enforcement’s use of surveillance drones to determine if the technology has found a balance between safety and privacy, Hofer said.

    “I don’t think we know whether drones are the middle road on surveillance tech. If our conversation was limited to checking out a safe house, it’s good,” Hofer said. “But if we look at all the authorized uses, it’s unclear. We don’t have sufficient data to show all the pros and cons.”
