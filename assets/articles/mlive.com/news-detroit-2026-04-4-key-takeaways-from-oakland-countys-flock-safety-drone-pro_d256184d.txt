(Cory Morse | MLive.com) Cory Morse | MLive.com By Advance Local Express Desk OAKLAND COUNTY -- The Oakland County commission’s decision on April 8 to implement a police-drone surveillance pilot program for the sheriff’s office has sparked intense debate over the balance between public safety and constitutional privacy. These are the four takeaways: 1. Tactical safeguards and contract restrictions on the pilot program Oakland County approved a nine-month pilot program to deploy seven Flock Safety drones, a decision that includes an optional transition into a $2.5 million, two-year contract. To ease concerns about privacy, the county commissioners voted to ensure that all data collected during the program belongs exclusively to the county and that the drones are only deployed for specific scenarios: search-and-rescue operations, deputy requests, or direct calls for service. The commission also agreed to separately consider the two-year, $2.5 million contract later this year. Despite these amendments, the 13-4 vote included four Democratic commissioners voted against the measure, highlighting a persistent partisan divide over the necessity and safety of automated police surveillance. 2. Public outcry and fears of federal immigration enforcement involvement The drone deployment has faced vocal opposition from residents who fear this technology will lead to mass surveillance of the county’s 1.3 million residents or be used by federal authorities for immigration enforcement. While Flock Safety has publicly stated it does not hold contracts with ICE or the Department of Homeland Security, investigations by 404 Media and Ars Technica suggest that thousands of searches have been performed on behalf of federal agencies. Critics like Oxford resident Michelle Coyle argue that using tax dollars for such technology is a violation of community trust. She was among nearly 4,000 people who signed an online petition arguing the program oversteps constitutional boundaries. 3. Law enforcement success stories versus investigative data risks The debate over the Flock drones is characterized by a tension between proven tactical utility and the risks of broad data collection. Oakland County law enforcement proponents cite recent successes, such as using drones to locate a murder suspect and find a missing autistic boy, as evidence that the technology saves lives. However, investigative reporting has revealed that Flock’s partnership with Amazon’s Ring cameras and third-party data brokers creates a massive tracking apparatus. The ACLU of Michigan has warned that this pervasive surveillance creates a chilling effect, particularly in minority communities, where the feeling of being watched can dissuade individuals from exercising their rights and participating freely in society. 4. Lessons from Ferndale’s rejection of subsidized outside surveillance The county’s decision follows a divergent path taken by local municipalities like Ferndale, where the city council recently ended its partnership with Flock due to transparency issues. Ferndale Councilwoman Laura Mikulski revealed that her city’s own police accounted for less than 1% of the queries into their license-plate reading system, while outside agencies from as far away as Texas accessed the data for reasons labeled in the log as “thirsty” or “hamburger.” This specific pattern of usage led officials to conclude that local taxpayers were essentially subsidizing a shared database for external agencies with little local benefit. Mikulski’s findings suggest that without strict oversight and clear answers on who controls the data, such programs risk becoming tools for what Mikulski called “plain bizarre” non-police activity rather than focused public-safety measures.
