Gov. Newsom touts hundreds of license plate reader cameras in Oakland

480 surveillance cameras will be installed around East Bay freeways and run by the highway patrol.
by Eli Wolfe March 29, 2024, 10:57 a.m.March 29, 2024, 3:26 p.m.

State police will install a network of 480 automated license plate readers around Oakland and East Bay freeways, and the highway patrol will control them. 

Gov. Gavin Newsom announced Friday that CHP has entered into an agreement with the surveillance company Flock Safety to install 290 cameras around surface streets in Oakland and another 190 along state highways throughout the East Bay. Flock’s license plate readers, also called ALPRs, are powerful surveillance cameras that can also capture the make and model of vehicles, as well as other identifying features, to generate real-time alerts for law enforcement. A press release issued by Newsom’s office indicates that CHP, Oakland police, and other “allied agencies” will be able to search for wanted vehicles using the camera system and receive real-time alerts.

Newsom made the announcement in a video posted to X in which he stood on Oakland’s busy Hegenberger Road, an area that has been under intense media scrutiny because of its very high rate of vehicle burglaries, thefts, and armed robberies, and the closure of an In-N-Out fast food restaurant.

Last October, the Oakland City Council struck a deal with Flock Safety to pay for 300 license plate reader cameras around the city, which would have been operated by Oakland police but paid for with a state loan. In February, sources revealed that the state wanted to give direct control of the cameras to CHP. That same month, Newsom announced CHP “surge” operations in Oakland and deployed state prosecutors to support the Alameda County District Attorney with charging serious crimes. 

“This investment marks another step forward in our commitment to bolstering public safety and tackling organized crime and roadway violence in Oakland and across California,” Newsom said in a statement. Newsom has recently pushed for more aggressive measures on public safety, including calling for legislation to expand criminal penalties to combat retail theft and auto burglaries. 

“This new camera network will help us stop crimes and hold more suspects accountable,” Oakland Mayor Sheng Thao said in a statement thanking Newsom and CHP. Oakland experienced significant spikes in several crime categories last year. This trend has improved in the first months of 2024, although robberies are still high. 

Other cities like San Francisco used grant money awarded by the Board of State and Community Corrections to pay directly for license plate reader systems. Oakland failed to apply for this grant money on time due to poor organization and coordination between multiple officials and departments. 

Flock Safety, a Georgia-based company, has been expanding throughout the Bay Area in recent years. Piedmont, San Leandro, Hercules, Vallejo, and Oakley have Flock systems, and Berkeley struck a deal for cameras last year. The company has also sold license plate readers to private homeowner groups in Oakland. 

When a Flock camera detects a license plate belonging to a car that is wanted in connection with a crime or a missing person, it produces a hit on a so-called “hot list,” which alerts law enforcement. Flock also allows customers to easily share data with law enforcement agencies, who can perform searches on the cameras. 

Privacy groups such as the ACLU have expressed concern that Flock is making it easier to perform mass surveillance and that the data could be misused or improperly shared. California law makes it illegal for public agencies to share data with out-of-state police and other agencies, but the ACLU and Electronic Frontier Foundation have discovered that this is being done by some law enforcement departments. Cat Brooks, co-founder and executive director of the Anti-Police Terror Project, criticized the state for spending money on surveillance cameras.

“We are concerned by both a state and city in massive budget deficits and the largest homeless population in our city and across the country,” Brooks said in a statement. “When we decide to deploy 480 new cameras we should be asking how many people could be housed with the money we spend on this, how many people could be trained to do living wage jobs.”

The governor’s announcement doesn’t offer specifics on when or where the cameras will be installed. A public record from February reviewed by The Oaklandside suggests that city officials wanted many of the cameras to be placed along commercial corridors, downtown, West Oakland, and East Oakland.
