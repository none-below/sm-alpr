Police can track the movements of vehicles with these powerful license plate reader surveillance cameras. But critics say they’re ripe for abuse. by Roselyn Romero and Darwin BondGraham Nov. 20, 2025, 3:11 p.m. Updated Dec. 19, 2025, 10:55 a.m. Share this: Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook Email a link to a friend (Opens in new window) Email Print (Opens in new window) Print Share on Reddit (Opens in new window) Reddit Share on Bluesky (Opens in new window) Bluesky A Flock Safety automated license plate reader camera near 51st Street and Broadway in Oakland. Credit: Darwin BondGraham/The Oaklandside If you’ve driven in Oakland recently, your car’s license plate number has almost certainly been recorded and stored in a privately owned database used by the police to investigate crimes. That database is owned and operated by Flock Safety , a company that provides surveillance technologies — including automated license plate readers , drones , and gunshot detectors — to thousands of law enforcement agencies and communities across the U.S. Here in Oakland, Flock has installed nearly 300 license plate reader cameras. But what exactly are these cameras, how do they work, and why have they generated so much debate? We’ve sought to answer these and other frequently asked questions below. Have Flock cameras been used to enforce immigration laws? Do Flock cameras reduce crime? What is a license plate reader camera? License plate reader cameras are surveillance tools that automatically record the license plate number of every vehicle that passes within their sight. They can be fixed to a pole or another stationary location, or a vehicle. All these license plate scans become data that police use to investigate crimes. Officers can get real-time alerts when a camera spots a license plate that’s on a stolen or wanted car. Or, the police can search the database to trace a specific vehicle’s movements. What is Flock Safety? Flock Safety is a law enforcement technology company that operates what it calls a “nationwide public-private safety network” of surveillance cameras, drones, license plate readers, and other devices. It has contracts with over 5,000 communities, 4,500 law enforcement agencies, and 1,000 businesses nationwide. The company, based in Georgia, is valued at $7.5 billion and receives nearly $700 million in venture capital funding, according to its website. One of Flock’s products is a line of license plate reader cameras . How do Flock’s license plate reader cameras work? Flock cameras aren’t traffic cameras; they don’t measure a car’s speed or issue traffic tickets. They do, however, continuously monitor vehicles and store the data in the Flock operating system , a portal where users can look up a vehicle’s plate number and see where that vehicle was spotted in the last 30 days without obtaining a warrant. The cameras also record what Flock calls a “vehicle fingerprint,” which includes the make, model, and color of the car, the state the license plate is registered in, whether the license plate is covered or missing, and unique features of the car — such as roof racks and bumper stickers — that would help law enforcement identify it in an investigation. Concerns over the spike in violent and property crimes starting five years ago led many community members to demand action by the police. In response, the Oakland Police Department and state law enforcement leaders explored the idea of setting up hundreds of license plate reader cameras in Oakland. In 2023, the Oakland City Council approved OPD’s surveillance use policy for automated license plate readers , a necessary first step before the department could start using these kinds of devices. The following year, the council allowed OPD to enter into contracts with the California Highway Patrol and Flock Safety to set up a network of license plate reader cameras throughout Oakland. That same year, in response to multiple home burglaries, robberies, and shootings, some Oakland neighborhoods began installing their own Flock cameras. More recently, some council members and activists have urged Oakland to enter into a new $2.25 million contract with Flock to replace the existing agreement between OPD, CHP, and the company. They also support the department’s proposal to expand the surveillance network by incorporating privately owned cameras into the Flock database. How many Flock cameras are there in Oakland, and where are they? Oakland currently has 293 Flock cameras installed throughout the city, according to the city’s Flock transparency portal . However, OPD has acknowledged that some of the cameras have been damaged and need to be replaced. We obtained information about the locations of Oakland’s Flock cameras and mapped them here. Keep in mind that other Bay Area police agencies also have Flock cameras, or license plate readers provided by other vendors, so surveillance camera coverage of Bay Area roads and highways is much more widespread than this map depicts. Why does OPD want to keep Flock? OPD and supporters of Flock argue that the surveillance cameras provide important leads and evidence that can be used to solve crimes, particularly stolen vehicles or other crimes involving cars. This, they say, helps improve solve rates and aids in prosecutions. Some also believe the cameras help reduce crime by acting as a deterrent; they argue that when people who want to commit a crime know they’re being watched, they might choose not to commit the crime after all, out of fear of being caught. The Oakland Alliance for Public Safety , a pro-police and pro-Flock Safety advocacy group, says the surveillance cameras stop robberies, reduce high-speed chases, catch repeat offenders, and solve shootings and homicides. The alliance points to the decline in robberies and carjackings citywide as evidence of Flock’s effectiveness, though independent studies haven’t confirmed the correlation between the deployment of Flock cameras and the reduction in crime in Oakland. Still, support for surveillance technologies appears strong among some community members. An Oakland Metropolitan Chamber of Commerce survey this year found that 67% of respondents said they want the city to monitor privately-owned camera systems, such as business security cameras, with consent from the owners. Similarly, 66% support the use of city-owned automated surveillance cameras, and 59% support the use of drones to monitor public safety incidents. What do Flock cameras look like? Flock sells different surveillance products, but the ALPR cameras in Oakland are small black oval devices with a camera lens usually pointed directly at a busy lane of traffic. They have black square solar panels attached to power them. They’re typically mounted on poles near major intersections and freeway entrances and exits. A Flock Safety automated license plate reader camera attached to a light pole, between a traffic camera (top) and a traffic light array. Credit: Darwin BondGraham/The Oaklandside What are the arguments against Flock? Civil libertarians and privacy advocates say that Flock is building a mass surveillance network that can track the movements of all Oakland residents, not just those suspected of committing crimes. The roughly 290 cameras in Oakland currently record roughly 48 million vehicle plates each month. Many have called into question the constitutionality of Flock devices, with civil liberties groups like the ACLU and the Electronic Frontier Foundation arguing that the technology infringes on Americans’ right to privacy under the Fourth Amendment. “Automatic license plate readers have the potential to create permanent records of virtually everywhere any of us has driven, radically transforming the consequences of leaving home to pursue private life, and opening up many opportunities for abuse,” the ACLU has argued . Flock says it doesn’t sell or share its data with third parties. But concerns remain over how secure Flock’s data is and the loopholes users can take to access it. TechCrunch reports that some lawmakers have called on the Federal Trade Commission to investigate Flock for allegedly failing to protect its system from hackers and foreign spies. While Flock cameras don’t use facial recognition software or record people’s racial or ethnic identity, the Electronic Frontier Foundation, a nonprofit that advocates for digital privacy rights, found that over 80 law enforcement agencies nationwide have used racist language when searching Flock’s ALPR network. Multiple cities nationwide have terminated their contracts with Flock, limited data sharing and access, or imposed hefty fines for contract violations, due to privacy and security concerns. Those cities include Austin, Texas ; Eugene, Oregon ; Denver ; Sedona, Arizona ; and more. Have Flock cameras been used to prosecute people seeking reproductive health care? Yes, there is evidence of this. Earlier this year, 404 Media reported that Texas law enforcement agencies searched Flock’s license plate data to identify a woman seeking an abortion. Over a monthlong period, an officer with the Johnson County Sheriff’s Office accessed over 80,000 license plate records from Texas, Washington, and Illinois to try to locate a woman whom authorities said had a “self-administered abortion.” While the sheriff said the woman’s family was concerned about her health and safety, privacy experts expressed concerns that law enforcement in a state where abortion is a crime can access cameras in other states where it is legal. The Electronic Frontier Foundation later published court records that showed Texas deputies had used Flock surveillance data as part of an abortion investigation. Have Flock cameras been used to enforce immigration laws? A Flock Safety automated license plate reader camera that has been covered in paint. Credit: Darwin BondGraham/The Oaklandside Oakland is a “ sanctuary city ” in a sanctuary state , and city laws prohibit OPD from assisting federal agents in enforcing immigration laws. Opponents of Flock allege that cameras in California have been used by outside law enforcement agencies to monitor and arrest people suspected of being undocumented. For instance, the Associated Press reported that U.S. Border Patrol officials have used Flock data to surveil and detain individuals with “suspicious” driving histories. In Evanston, Illinois, Flock was found to have illegally reinstalled its ALPR cameras after the city had ordered them removed because the system violated Illinois’ privacy laws by allowing U.S. Customs and Border Protection agents to access the data. Do Flock cameras reduce crime? The research is mixed. Experts generally say it’s difficult to establish a causal relationship between Flock cameras and changing crime rates. When license plate reader cameras have been installed in cities, Flock and its supporters claim that crime clearance rates — the percentage of cases “solved” by the identification or arrest of a suspect — increase. They also claim that license plate reader cameras help deter crime, as shown by falling crime rates after cameras are put to use. The Oaklandside reviewed several recent academic studies that tried to measure the impact license plate reader cameras had on public safety in different cities. The results of these studies show mixed and inconclusive results. A 2019 study of Charlotte-Mecklenburg, North Carolina, where police deployed nearly 100 stationary cameras, showed “for the city overall, there were no indications of significant improvements in clearances for auto theft cases during the post-LPR period,” and that “at the city level, there was no change in robbery clearances overall.” The researchers did find “moderately significant improvement” in clearance rate for auto theft in areas of the city with the highest concentrations of cameras, and a roughly 2% increase in clearance rates for robbery cases that were not solved on the first day. But these weren’t conclusive findings, and the researchers wrote: “the most sophisticated tests did not show statistically significant evidence of greater improvements.” It was possible, they surmised, that “factors beyond [license plate reader] deployment contributed to improvements in case closures, particularly for auto theft investigations.” A 2016 study found “significantly increased follow-up arrests in the Cincinnati Police Department compared with traditional policing approaches” after license plate readers were put to use by local police. The researchers concluded that police were able to make “more follow-up arrests by using fewer police officers compared with traditional policing,” and that the technology quickly paid for itself by making officers more efficient. Research published earlier this year about the use of license plate readers in Atlantic City, New Jersey, showed mixed findings. The study’s authors wrote that the expansion of the police department’s license plate reader camera network didn’t reduce violent crime. “There is little evidence that the ALPR expansion was associated with reductions in violent crime in general,” they concluded. However, they did find evidence suggesting that the cameras “were associated with decreases in shootings, motor vehicle thefts, and property crime in general.” They ended their study by issuing a caveat noted in other research papers about drops in crime: “It is difficult to rule out other factors —both locally and nationally — that might be contributing to those reductions,” they said. Most of the studies we were able to review concluded that there needs to be much more research done to gain a firm understanding of how license plate readers may or may not contribute to public safety. And, there’s also a vast and growing body of literature about the potential harms of license plate readers when it comes to privacy and civil liberties. Correction and update: The original version of this story mischaracterized the findings of a 2011 research report about the impact surveillance cameras had on crime in three cities. We have updated this story by removing reference to that report because of the error, and another cited report, because neither study was specifically about license plate reader cameras. We have also updated this story by including recent research on the effects license plate readers have on public safety.
