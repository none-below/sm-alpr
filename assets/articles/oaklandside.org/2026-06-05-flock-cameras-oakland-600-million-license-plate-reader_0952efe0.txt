Credit: Darwin BondGraham/The Oaklandside There are 293 automated license plate readers spread around Oakland’s streets, feeding data around the clock to the police. And last year they gathered a staggering amount of data on motorists. In 2025, Oakland’s Flock Safety camera system recorded 638,747,333 license plates, according to a report compiled by OPD. You read that right — more than six hundred million. A car traveling through Oakland will likely be spotted by multiple cameras, so these are not unique plate reads. But this number still reflects the astonishing power of surveillance used to track vehicles on Oakland’s streets today. Only a fraction of the hundreds of millions of license plate reads generated leads for OPD. According to data shared by the department in an annual report , the Flock system generated a total of 1,099,837 “hotlist” alerts last year. A hotlist alert is a notice about a vehicle potentially connected to a crime. The vast majority of Oakland’s alerts — over 620,000 — were for stolen plates. These numbers might seem large, but they amount to a mere 0.17% of total scans for the hotlist alerts, and an even smaller fraction of a percent, just 0.09% for stolen plates. Never miss a story. Email Δ But even though the useful alerts are a tiny fraction of the total scans, they’re still overwhelming Oakland police. There were so many hits for stolen vehicles and plates last year that OPD kept these alerts deactivated, according to the report. The department said it doesn’t have the staffing or resources to respond to them. OPD still has alerts for vehicles wanted in connection with various serious crimes. Department officials still insist that the license plate readers are partially responsible for the decline in certain categories of violent crime, and the report cites a couple of examples of how Flock has helped OPD apprehend criminals. “We believe that this is just one element of a cohesive approach by the department and the city to address violent crime,” Lt. Gabriel Urquiza said during a Thursday meeting of the Privacy Advisory Commission. “I’m by no means saying that that entire drop-off is because of this technology, but this is an element of those efforts to address this type of crime, and to continue to keep that work going, moving forward.” When the City Council was considering whether or not to keep the Flock plate readers last year, one of the arguments OPD made was that the technology helps the understaffed police department more easily catch criminals. The Oakland NAACP made the same efficiency argument in endorsing Flock, calling the cameras a “force multiplier.” And so did the company’s CEO. In an October 2025 press release , Garrett Langley boasted that Flock’s results in Oakland “show how modern tools and technology can help hardworking investigators close more cases amid staffing challenges and improve safety in communities.” But some of the company’s biggest boosters are now arguing that without sufficient staffing, the technology can’t actually meet its full potential. Earlier this week, Garry Tan, a San Francisco tech investor who had poured money into San Francisco and state political causes, wrote a blog post about the high number of stolen-plate and stolen-vehicle alerts generated on Oakland’s Flock system. Citing data from a six-month period in 2024, Tan wrote that Oakland’s system generated over 210,000 alerts tied to stolen vehicles or plates. “Oakland’s Flock system surfaced a huge amount of potential enforcement work. OPD’s own report suggests the department did not have the staffing, configuration, or operational pipeline to absorb it,” Tan wrote. Flock’s chief strategy officer, Rahul Sidhu, reposted Tan’s article on social media. “210,000 stolen cars is… a lot,” Sidhu wrote . “That’s almost 50 stolen cars an hour. If every cop on duty tried to pull them over 24/7, there still wouldn’t be enough.” Other users pointed out that the 210,000 figure referred to alerts, not unique stolen cars. One car can and likely does trigger multiple alerts as it passes by multiple cameras over a period of days, weeks, or even months. On social media, Bryan Culbertson, a traffic safety advocate, wrote that only 3,000 cars were reported stolen during this period in 2024, but Flock flooded the department with alerts, creating “useless noise.” Sidhu doubled down, arguing , “That level of volume is really difficult to respond to with limited resources.” The Privacy Advisory Commission greenlit sharing Flock data with public works and the city attorney OPD wants to be able to share images from its Flock system with the Public Works Department and the City Attorney’s Office, Lt. Urquiza told the privacy commission Thursday. The goal is to help civilian officials catch and punish illegal dumpers, he said. But police officers would still be the only city employees with direct access to the Flock system, he said. In recent months, city officials have advanced legislation to create stiffer penalties for illegal dumping, but a report by the City Auditor found that the city is not making good use of its dedicated illegal dumping surveillance cameras. The Commission unanimously recommended that the City Council approve this rule change. The volunteer body also gave the greenlight to a list of approved searches that OPD can plug into the Flock system. This includes welfare checks, which Urquiza explained would involve trying to find a missing person or someone in need of medical attention. The list also includes “Pornography/Obscene Material” as an approved search term. It’s unclear what this would entail. The Oaklandside asked OPD about this search term but did not receive a response. The privacy commission appeared largely uninterested in the volume of Flock data. This marks a notable departure from the commission’s behavior even just a few months ago. Several former commissioners — notably Brian Hofer — would use PAC meetings to grill OPD officials about Oakland’s surveillance systems. Hofer and Sean Everhart resigned from the commission after expressing frustration with city leaders not doing enough to ensure privacy rights for residents. Hofer and Everhart were especially concerned with Flock. When the City Council voted to approve a $2 million contract with Flock last December, scores of residents appeared at City Hall to protest the surveillance system. Many cited a SF Standard report from July 2024 that revealed SFPD had requested data from Oakland’s system and passed it to federal authorities. Hofer later sued Oakland, alleging that OPD has repeatedly violated state law that forbids the sharing of data with out-of-state agencies. His lawsuit is pending. OPD has repeatedly stated that it doesn’t share data with federal or out-of-state agencies. During Thursday’s commission meeting, Urquiza said the department has audited search queries and found that officers are not abusing the system by entering inappropriate searches.
