Oakland’s future could look distinctly different depending on who’s elected mayor or city councilmember on Nov. 3. We sent all candidates detailed questionnaires about pressing issues like police staffing, homeless camps, government transparency, Flock, and AI. We’re printing their responses in full, below, so voters can compare and contrast their visions and qualifications. We made some minor formatting adjustments but otherwise did not edit what they wrote. Two incumbents, District 6 Councilmember Kevin Jenkins and District 4 Councilmember Janani Ramachandran, did not submit responses. We’ll update this page if they do. One note: When we sent out these questionnaires, the Oakland Police Department had not yet been released from federal oversight . We’re still publishing the candidates’ responses to a now-outdated question about this, because they reveal their ongoing stances on police accountability. I helped open the Maia Correia protected bike lane on Lakeshore to prevent another tragic death, launched a dedicated fund for sideshow-prevention infrastructure, and set parking limits at the Lakeshore cul-de-sac to curb street takeovers. I authorized the sale of Measure U bonds to fund a street paving blitz that is already being seen across the district and Oakland. Many roads still urgently need safety improvements. International and Foothill Blvds., 14th Ave, Lakeshore Ave, Park Blvd, the streets surrounding Oakland High and Lincoln Elementary, and E. 21st St. are some of our most dangerous. The 14th Ave redesign is well underway. We’re advocating for OakDOT to install traffic diverters at parts of Lakeshore to disrupt dangerous traffic. As part of the Oakland-Alameda Access Project, I negotiated community benefits, including angled parking to narrow roads and reduce speeds in Chinatown. This project has broken ground, and will reroute dangerous freeway ramps and tunnels away from Chinatown’s neighborhood streets where 60% of serious crashes occur. Where do you stand on the city charter amendments on the November ballot? Do you support the concept of a strong mayor system? Voters call mayoral and council offices when potholes grow, landlords mistreat tenants, abandoned boats leach chemicals into the estuary, and much more. Right now, unelected city administrators cannot receive direction from either the mayor or council. That leaves residents without a direct line to solutions. I support a strong mayor and strong council because clear authority lets residents hold city leadership accountable. This measure does not undermine council, it provides subpoena power to hold a mayor, their officials, and department leadership accountable. Mayor Lee and I partnered to write amendments vesting council with the power to publicly confirm or reject department head appointments. These mimic the US Senate’s appointment and confirmation process, which enhances transparency and supports department accountability to representatives. Since day-to-day operations occur at the department level, confirmation hearings will help Oakland avoid unqualified or unprofessional directors. Have you visited any of Oakland’s homeless shelters or homeless camps and spoken to people living there? If so, which, and what did you learn from the experience? I frequently visit the encampment community at the Peralta District parking lot under the I-880, between Laney and the Estuary, including individuals from the Wood Street Commons. We’re working to bring necessities like potable water over there and have reached out to Laney to discuss ways we can better support this community, and connect them with services. I have also visited the individuals at Webster and 6th, including Father DeMaio who advocates for that community. Speaking with unhoused people underscored how broken our current system is. People are trapped on housing wait lists for years. Since Oakland doesn’t have enough shelters, that means waiting in dangerous, traumatizing environments that make eventual recovery more difficult. Because of my advocacy, the council reversed cuts to shelters that could have dramatically worsened the situation earlier this year. I am actively working to convert the crime-ridden motels in my district into homeless shelters and have already secured commitments from two motel owners that they are willing to work with a shelter provider. Oakland has a new plan to cut unsheltered homelessness in half, but it’s billions of dollars short of achieving it. Do you have any out-of-the-box, shorter-term ideas for addressing the homelessness crisis? The ballot measure I authored, Measure FF, is one of them. The measure closes a corporate tax loophole to bring in city funding but it exempts developments that convert unused properties into homeless shelters – so private funding not just public to convert offices and warehouses into shelter solves this issue. We need to treat homelessness as a public health issue where early responses drastically improve outcomes. People get worse on the street while they wait, longer periods of unsheltered homelessness drastically reduce someone’s chances of getting better. I’m advocating that we focus on prevention and shelter, our fastest, most scalable solutions. By intervening early, we reduce the wait lists for permanent housing. I also support a pilot program to test whether paying encampment residents to maintain sites can reduce city costs, benefit unhoused people, and prepare them for long-term jobs. Testing this could inform a shelter workforce model where a range of roles are filled by currently or formerly unhoused people. This would be a double dividend for shelter economics, reducing operating costs while improving support to unhoused people. Affordable housing development has increased in Oakland lately, but the funding sources — especially Measure U — are running out. Meanwhile, market-rate housing construction has also slowed down significantly in recent years and building costs are rising. How can the city attract more development? Study after study shows the way to bring down rents is building more housing at all income levels. I am leading efforts to reduce housing costs in Oakland, authoring amendments to approve new housing on transit and removing fees on housing that drove up rents. I wrote and passed a resolution advancing a San Antonio BART station feasibility study. This is a first step towards more homes, shopping, transit, and community spaces in this area. We need to bring a unionized modular housing factory to Oakland. UC Berkeley’s Terner Center finds factory-built housing can cut construction costs 10 to 25% and build times 20 to 50%, while creating stable union jobs here. I am running on a comprehensive Oakland housing plan to reduce time-consuming discretionary review of housing, end parking minimums, issue pre-approved design plans for fourplexes and townhomes, launch a social housing pilot on public land, and much more. I will protect renters by keeping rent stabilization and Just Cause protections strong, continue to fund tenant legal aid, and letting tenants with disabilities move to accessible units without losing rent control. https://www.charleneforoakland.com/oakland-housing-plan The artificial intelligence industry has expanded rapidly and has begun to arrive in Oakland. Should Oakland embrace AI businesses? Should the city incorporate the tool into government work? I support the data center moratorium and authored amendments to strengthen it–requiring a study of impacts to Oakland and closing a loophole that lets council end a moratorium earlier. I’m very concerned by the lack of national regulation, safety, and job displacement. We need proactive, local regulations to AI’s risks. Oakland should only embrace AI businesses that align with our progressive values such as researching cures, optimizing clean energy, assisting people with disabilities. City government should responsibly use AI to improve services. Many city departments are at capacity, and the city’s outdated technology undermines delivery of the basics. Furthermore, regulators need to keep pace with the private sector profit-driven AI use, such as corporate landlords algorithmically increasing rents. We should use AI to search databases, identifying patterns from abusive landlords. There are other applications, such as processing housing permits faster, and triaging our 311 backlog. Any deployment needs guardrails, humans in the loop, and no displacement of city workers. Across the country, both liberal and conservative leaders are terminating contracts with Flock Safety, the license plate reader surveillance company. Last December, the City Council approved a two-year contract with Flock to pay for hundreds of cameras in Oakland. If elected, will you support or oppose renewing the contract with Flock? Why or why not? OPD is severely understaffed. We have the country’s highest auto theft rate. That expense hits poor Oaklanders hardest. ALPRs have recovered those vehicles in real time. They supply evidence against traffickers and underage sex buyers. I have concerns with Flock. I consulted with immigrant communities that have the most at stake. I authored what officials have called the strongest safeguards in the country: “hard coding off” the National Lookup feature that gave federal backdoor access; banning the use for enforcement related to immigration, abortions, or gender-affirming care; requiring security audits every 6 months to affirm that safeguards; setting the nation’s highest fines at $200k per violation. I am now working to ensure we share data with only Sanctuary cities. I authored Oakland’s new Sanctuary City law, clearing immigration data from our systems, establishing city property as ICE-free zones, and strengthening the ban of any city cooperation with immigration enforcement. Now, I am actively pushing the city for an open bid to replace Flock. I’m working with Mayor Lee to press OPD to start that process now, so we can assess other safety camera options. What’s one Oakland issue you personally care a lot about that isn’t discussed enough and isn’t represented in this questionnaire? To tackle the outrageous cost of living, we must create jobs, especially for those without college degrees. I launched Boarded 2 Bustling, getting 20 businesses fill storefront vacancies. We brought Grocery Outlet to the neighborhood, co-developing a safety plan with OPD, since that was a top concern. I fast-tracked a permit snag that almost stopped their launch. Now, they are looking to employ over 20 people. I’m supporting the Coliseum’s transformation into a music destination for tourism, affordable housing, and local jobs. I’ve invested in our LGBTQ+ cultural district, with the aim to build it into a hub for queer-owned small businesses. I’m working to expand re-entry training and second-chance jobs for those exiting prison. These second-chance jobs include doing much-needed cleanup work. By reducing recidivism rates, we reduce peoples’ likelihood of committing crimes or becoming homeless. We’ve seen the loss of unionized trades and manufacturing jobs, a critical path into the middle class for people without college degrees. Rebuilding that ladder will be my second term’s work: building a modular housing factory, creating maritime Port jobs, and jobs in clean energy. The city auditor recently conducted a survey about Oakland’s ethics climate.
