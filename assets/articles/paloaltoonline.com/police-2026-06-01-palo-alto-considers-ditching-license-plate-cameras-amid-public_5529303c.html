Palo Alto considers ditching license plate cameras amid public outcry

Dozens urge city to cancel contract with Flock after data was searched by outside agency
by Riley Cooke June 1, 2026 9:24 pmJune 1, 2026 9:24 pm
  • Share on X (Opens in new window) X
  • Share on Facebook (Opens in new window) Facebook
  • Print (Opens in new window) Print
  • Email a link to a friend (Opens in new window) Email

“GTFO: Get The Flock Out!” read a sign at City Hall on Monday evening.

It was held by one of dozens of people who turned out to the City Council meeting to protest Palo Alto’s contract with Flock Safety, a company that operates 30 automatic license plate recognition (ALPR) cameras in the city. Flock has come under increased scrutiny after it allowed out-of-state and federal access to neighboring jurisdictions in the Bay Area — and in March, this publication found that the same unauthorized access was granted in Palo Alto without the police department’s knowledge.

Police Chief James Reifschneider has maintained that no actual data was received by the thousands of out-of-state agencies that had access to Palo Alto’s ALPR system, but on Monday night, he conceded that the department was “not wedded to Flock” as a vendor for the technology given the data privacy concerns.

“We took for granted that we understood the universe of features that were available,” he said, referring to the fact that the police department’s internal auditing did not catch the out-of-state sharing for over a year.

He added that the main issue was “poor oversight as opposed to malice or any nefarious intent” by Flock.

The Palo Alto City Council appeared to be convinced of the opposite during its discussion of Flock. While the council didn’t take any formal actions, multiple council members expressed support and trust in the city’s own police force and said they were suspicious of Flock’s repeated breaches of local and state law, not just in Palo Alto, but across the country.

“Palo Alto is just a tiny little tip of the iceberg on this. Again, I’m not worried about any of y’all,” Council member Julie Lythcott-Haims told Reifschneider. “I’m worried about us being complicit in a nationwide system to systematically dismantle Fourth Amendment rights of Americans.”

Council member Ed Lauing similarly suggested that Flock “fell asleep at the switch” and broke the law. And Mayor Vicki Veenker proposed fining Flock for its breaches in Palo Alto and having the company pay for the additional auditing that the city is conducting into its practices.

“We’ve been looking at the damages to us, but I’m interested in the penalty for them,” Veenker said.

Palo Alto has had a contract with Flock since 2023 for 20 ALPRs, which was then expanded to 30 cameras in 2024. The existing contract, which is set to expire in December 2029, will cost Palo Alto about $93,500 annually, according to Reifschneider. About a third of the cost over the next two years will be covered by a state grant to combat organized retail theft, he added.

Reifschneider cited more than 130 cases since 2024 where Flock cameras have served as key evidence in an investigation, as well as a 13% reduction in residential burglaries and a 24% reduction in auto burglaries today compared to a year ago.

He called the ALPR technology a significant factor in the reduction of crime in Palo Alto.

Josh Waldorf, the vice president of the Palo Alto Police Officer’s Association, spoke highly of ALPRs during the public comment period.

“Flock helps us work smarter. Palo Alto does not have unlimited police staffing,” he said. “This technology does not replace officers, good police work or constitutional standards — it helps officers focus their efforts, respond faster and follow evidence based leads instead of relying on mere chance.”

Waldorf compared canceling Flock’s contract to preventing police officers from taking fingerprint or DNA samples.

But Waldorf was in the minority of speakers during the comment period, where more than two dozen people urged the city to reconsider or cancel its Flock contract.

Many were members of a newly formed coalition called Safety Over Surveillance Palo Alto, which includes 11 local organizations under its umbrella to advocate against surveillance technology like ALPRs. 

Locally and across the Peninsula, momentum against Flock has been growing. Earlier this year, Mountain View disabled all its ALPR cameras after an investigation by the Mountain View Voice found out-of-state access by hundreds of agencies without city officials’ knowledge or permission. The Santa Clara County Sheriff similarly terminated its contract with Flock this year, although Reifschneider noted that the cameras under the sheriff’s jurisdiction were not yet in operation.

Referring to the Fourth Amendment, Tim MacKenzie said it would be illegal for a police officer to search your home without a warrant, even if the officer did not seize any property. He compared this to Palo Alto’s ALPRs that were searched without a warrant by out-of-state agencies, and even if they did not retrieve any data, it is still cause for concern.

Austin Marshall, another speaker who is a member of the SOS coalition, also noted that the FBI is seeking proposals to buy a nationwide ALPR system. With Flock being the largest company with a preexisting network of ALPR cameras, Marshall and others warned the council about the potential for even more surveillance that could be carried out in pursuit of the Trump administration’s attacks on immigration and reproductive rights.

“I’m not suspected of a crime,” Marshall said. “Why does my trip to the supermarket earn me a spot in a government database controlled by a company that so recklessly disregards state law, and indeed your own robust policies?”

Members of the public and the council repeatedly questioned Flock’s ability to make good on its promises regarding data privacy and surveillance.

In that vein, the police department is working with its independent police auditor, the OIR Group, to review Flock’s practices in Palo Alto, as well as local police officers’ use of the technology. Previously, the city considered assigning the task to the consulting firm Baker Tilly, which functions as the city auditor, but the firm recused itself because it also provides services to Flock.

In the meantime, the police department has committed to proactively publishing both the network audits, which show searches by outside agencies, and organizational audits, which show searches conducted by PAPD officers, every month on its website.

Council member Pat Burt requested that City Manager Ed Shikada and the police department provide the council with more specifics on the scope of the audit before proceeding.

The audit results, and a potential vote on whether to cancel the contract with Flock, will return to the council after the summer recess, which runs from June 19 to Aug. 10.

“This set of elected officials ultimately has that oversight responsibility, and I don’t want to be hoping and guessing that the scope is the one that we would like to see,” Burt said.
