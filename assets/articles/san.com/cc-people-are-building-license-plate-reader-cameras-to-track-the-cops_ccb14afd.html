People are building license plate reader cameras to track the cops

Oct 14, 2026 at 12:00 PM CDT
Mikael Thalen

Full story

Last week I wrote about a Canadian YouTuber who was visited at his home by police after he built a license plate reader camera designed to only track law enforcement vehicles.

The YouTuber, Ontario resident Anthony Sistilli, caught the attention of the police after producing two short-form videos about the project that ended up earning more than a million views across social media.

The police said they were concerned that Sistilli’s setup — built with a cheap Amazon camera, a laptop, and some AI-generated software — could be used to create a map of their movements and thus allow criminals to get the upper hand or even target cops.

Sistilli had no intention of building such a map but found the visit rather ironic given that the public has many of the same concerns when it comes to the police tracking them with cameras made by Flock Safety and other companies. And Sistilli’s setup is far less advanced and capable than the license plate reader systems used by law enforcement. 

But the visit by the cops, as first reported by Straight Arrow, has brought much more attention to Sistilli’s project than it ever would have received had they not shown up at his door. A video Sistilli uploaded about the encounter has racked up more than 815,000 views on YouTube alone. 

Although Sistilli is not the first to create such a project, discussions across social media suggest that others are looking to replicate and improve upon his work, primarily in the U.S. where companies like Flock have spurred a bipartisan backlash against the technology.

So don’t be surprised if you see in the coming weeks and months other stories about people building license plate reader cameras of their own. And who knows, maybe they’ll be visited by law enforcement as well. We already know that Homeland Security is monitoring a project known as SparrowMap, which uses volunteer security cameras to track the movements of police. 

As for Sistilli, he told me that he has not been contacted again by police following the release of their now-viral meeting. He also isn’t lingering on the topic of license plate reader cameras and says he plans to focus on data centers next.

What I’m reading

  • Feds Got Warrants to Use Fake Cell Towers to Hunt Down Don Lemon’s Producer: WIRED reports that law enforcement agents obtained authorization to use a cell-site simulator, a device that mimics a cell phone tower and is commonly referred to as a Stingray, to locate a 21-year-old college student who helped journalist Don Lemon in his coverage of an anti-ICE protest at a Minnesota church earlier this year. 
  • FBI Arrests Executive at Ransomware Negotiation Firm: Brian Krebs has the story about a 54-year-old Canadian cybersecurity executive who was arrested in Pennsylvania and charged in connection with the recent devastating breach of the FBI by the ShinyHunters ransomware gang.
  • Amazon has over 1 million robot workers. What’s next for human employees?: Ars Technica is asking what will happen to Amazon’s human work force as the company, which already has 1 million robots deployed in its warehouses, continues to automate its operations. While Amazon says it is automating only the most dangerous jobs, critics argue that the company won’t stop replacing humans as its robots become more and more advanced.

What do you want to know?

In last week’s Confidential Informant newsletter, I asked you how you deal with and respond to data breaches when your personal information is affected. Many of you sent in some great responses, two of which are worth highlighting.

Nearly everyone who replied said that they utilize credit monitoring services —  which, ironically, many received for free in response to being in a data breach — to protect themselves from scammers who could, among other things, open credit cards in their name.

So if you haven’t frozen your credit, it’s not a bad idea.

On the other side of the spectrum, one reader instead replied: “I don’t care anymore. Fraud and corruption everywhere.”

Believe me, I get it. Having covered surveillance and data breaches for more than a decade, it’s easy to feel overwhelmed. If I can offer any advice, the best things you can do are use a password manager, which will allow you to create and store unique and complex passwords, and enable two-factor authentication for your online accounts. Just doing that will put you in a much better place in terms of digital security.
