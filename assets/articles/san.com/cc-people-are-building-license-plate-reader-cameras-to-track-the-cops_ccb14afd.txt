Image credit: Getty Images Summary Full story Why this story matters Get the big picture Media landscape Timeline Sources Related stories Read full story Full story Last week I wrote about a Canadian YouTuber who was visited at his home by police after he built a license plate reader camera designed to only track law enforcement vehicles. The YouTuber, Ontario resident Anthony Sistilli, caught the attention of the police after producing two short-form videos about the project that ended up earning more than a million views across social media. The police said they were concerned that Sistilli’s setup — built with a cheap Amazon camera, a laptop, and some AI-generated software — could be used to create a map of their movements and thus allow criminals to get the upper hand or even target cops. Sistilli had no intention of building such a map but found the visit rather ironic given that the public has many of the same concerns when it comes to the police tracking them with cameras made by Flock Safety and other companies. And Sistilli’s setup is far less advanced and capable than the license plate reader systems used by law enforcement. But the visit by the cops, as first reported by Straight Arrow, has brought much more attention to Sistilli’s project than it ever would have received had they not shown up at his door. A video Sistilli uploaded about the encounter has racked up more than 815,000 views on YouTube alone. Although Sistilli is not the first to create such a project, discussions across social media suggest that others are looking to replicate and improve upon his work, primarily in the U.S. where companies like Flock have spurred a bipartisan backlash against the technology. So don’t be surprised if you see in the coming weeks and months other stories about people building license plate reader cameras of their own. And who knows, maybe they’ll be visited by law enforcement as well. We already know that Homeland Security is monitoring a project known as SparrowMap , which uses volunteer security cameras to track the movements of police. As for Sistilli, he told me that he has not been contacted again by police following the release of their now-viral meeting. He also isn’t lingering on the topic of license plate reader cameras and says he plans to focus on data centers next. READ MORE: He built his own Flock-style camera to track police. In last week’s Confidential Informant newsletter, I asked you how you deal with and respond to data breaches when your personal information is affected. Many of you sent in some great responses, two of which are worth highlighting. Nearly everyone who replied said that they utilize credit monitoring services — which, ironically, many received for free in response to being in a data breach — to protect themselves from scammers who could, among other things, open credit cards in their name. So if you haven’t frozen your credit , it’s not a bad idea. On the other side of the spectrum, one reader instead replied: “I don’t care anymore. Fraud and corruption everywhere.” Believe me, I get it. Having covered surveillance and data breaches for more than a decade, it’s easy to feel overwhelmed. If I can offer any advice, the best things you can do are use a password manager, which will allow you to create and store unique and complex passwords, and enable two-factor authentication for your online accounts. Just doing that will put you in a much better place in terms of digital security. That’s all for this week.
