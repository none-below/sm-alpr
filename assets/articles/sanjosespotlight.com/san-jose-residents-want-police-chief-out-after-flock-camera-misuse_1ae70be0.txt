Community advocates are calling for San Jose’s police chief to step down, after revelations that a police officer used license plate tracking technology to help his cousin stalk a woman she alleges abused her. In addition, a coalition of groups — including Safety over Surveillance, Services, Immigrant Rights and Education Network (SIREN), Working Partnerships USA, Electronic Frontier Foundation, Amigos de Guadalupe Center for Justice and Empowerment and more — are demanding San Jose cancel its contract with surveillance company Flock Safety and conduct an audit on its system of automatic license plate readers. “Mass surveillance does not keep us safe, it keeps us afraid and endangers all of our visitors to our beloved San Jose,” Kimberly Woo, an organizer with SIREN, said at a Wednesday rally. “Real public safety comes from investing in people, strengthening communities, protecting the rights and dignity of all residents.” The SJPD officer was fired in April after a yearlong investigation that found he had illicitly accessed license plate reader data on his phone, according to the Mercury News . The police department was first informed of the incident in February 2025 after the woman reported that the man knew her whereabouts. City officials have repeatedly reassured residents that Flock cameras are safe and secure, despite incidents that point to the contrary. In March, while the investigation was ongoing, Police Chief Paul Joseph told the City Council that “our audits have shown that our license plate reader data has not gone anywhere it isn’t supposed to go.” Kimberly Woo, an organizer with SIREN, said the San Jose Police Department breached its trust with the public at a rally held on Sept. 30, 2026. Photo by Joyce Chu. Deputy Police Chief Macedonio Zuniga said he couldn’t address why Joseph was not forthcoming about the investigation. “I can’t speak to what was occurring at the time or where this investigation was at the time,” Zuniga told San José Spotlight. Advocates said these incidents breach public trust and spell a lack of transparency within SJPD. “The city’s reliance on these cameras creates new threats, and there’s no amount of safeguards that can truly eliminate the possibility of abuse,” Woo said. Two separate proposals — one put forth by Mayor Matt Mahan, Vice Mayor Pam Foley and Councilmembers Domingo Candelas and George Casey, and another written by Councilmembers Rosemary Kamei, Anthony Tordillos, David Cohen and Michael Mulcahy — aim to make it harder for city employees to use personal devices to access data from license plate readers. They would also expand audits designed to catch illicit searches. At the San Jose Rules and Open Government Committee meeting on Wednesday, the five councilmembers on the committee unanimously voted to have staff analyze the work needed to bring the proposals forward, and bring it back to the committee in a few weeks time. Then the policy will go before the full council to vote on its implementation. Foley said the memos do not address the demands advocates have to cover the cameras and end the Flock contract since city council makes the final decision, not the rules committee. “I can’t express enough how concerned we are about the breach, so I look forward to having this formal discussion with council and how we’re going to move forward, and with the police chief present because his input is important as well,” Foley said at the meeting. “We did listen to him in March, and we had established guardrails that we all thought would be successful. Turns out, they weren’t, and it’s very, very alarming.” In March, councilmembers adopted a range of new safeguards for San Jose’s network of 474 cameras, including limits on where the devices may be installed and how long license plate data may be stored. But the latest incident further exposes the gaps in accountability that exist with automated license plate readers, which have come under increasing scrutiny in recent months. In November, a coalition of local advocacy groups sued San Jose , alleging the city’s practice of allowing warrantless searches of its license plate data violates drivers’ privacy rights. Earlier this year, South Bay jurisdictions —including Santa Clara County, Los Altos Hills and Mountain View — terminated their contracts with Flock Safety , amid concerns the company has allowed transfers of license plate reader data to out-of-state law enforcement agencies, a practice barred by state law. Zuniga said SJPD will follow the city council’s lead on using surveillance cameras. “We’re going to look at the workload to see everything that we can do to ensure that we leverage our technology responsibly and ethically,” he said. Greg Ramos, an advocate with Safety over Surveillance, said the city needs to take decisive action in ending the Flock contracts and ensuring the safety of residents. “Everybody’s always playing hot potato with the responsibility of things, and then they always say, ‘We’re going to come back to it when the data is available,'” he told San José Spotlight. FACT Automated License Plate Readers (ALPRs) help law enforcement in Santa Clara County solve violent crimes, such as a July 2026 triple-shooting in San Jose where investigators used ALPR data from Flock Safety cameras to identify a suspect vehicle and arrest two primary suspects within days. How ALPRs Aid Murder and Violent Crime Investigations Vehicle Identification: Cameras capture a vehicle’s make, model, color, partial plates, and unique characteristics (like bumper stickers) even when a license plate is obscured. Tracking Travel Patterns: Investigators stitch together camera hits to map suspect movements across city and county lines following a violent incident. Apprehension: Real-time data alerts allow police to quickly locate fugitive vehicles and execute arrests, such as the apprehension of suspects in San Jose’s 16th homicide of 2026. Controversies and Policy Changes in Santa Clara County While police departments emphasize the utility of ALPR networks in solving serious crimes, the technology faces intense scrutiny regarding privacy and data misuse: Data Misuse: The San Jose Police Department fired an officer after an investigation revealed he improperly used ALPR data to track a citizen, prompting city leaders to push for stricter access controls and legal penalties. Vendor Pushback and Bans: The Santa Clara County Board of Supervisors voted against utilizing Flock Safety as a vendor for unincorporated areas and West Valley cities like Cupertino and Saratoga over data-sharing and privacy concerns. Legal Challenges: Civil rights groups, including the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) and the Council on American-Islamic Relations (CAIR), have filed lawsuits challenging local ALPR deployments as violations of California privacy statutes and constitutional rights.
