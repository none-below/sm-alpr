His Son Keeps Pressuring Him to Drop

During a recent interview with journalist Kara Swisher, local crypto
billionaire Chris Larsen revealed that his 19-year-old son keeps
pressuring him to stop funding SFPD’s Flock surveillance technology
amid growing backlash.
Larsen said he has defended the surveillance technology to his son by
pointing to its role in reducing crime, but the 19-year-old remains
unconvinced. As Business Insider reports, when Larsen told him that
surveillance had helped bring crime down in San Francisco, his son
responded that he would rather have the crime than the surveillance.
Larsen made the comments on a recent episode of the podcast, On with
Kara Swisher, filmed at San Francisco’s Pier 27, where he discussed the
growing backlash against Flock and other surveillance tools. He
acknowledged that there is now significant opposition to the technology,
which he attributed in part to what he called “bad press.”

Larsen donated $9.4 million to SFPD in 2025 to expand its Real-Time
Investigation Center, consisting of automated license-plate readers,
drones, surveillance cameras, and other tools, which also helped fund
the center’s move from the Hall of Justice to a larger facility downtown,
along with additional equipment and drone launch sites.
He told Swisher that support for surveillance technology was
considerably stronger one or two years ago, but that opposition has since
grown alongside the national “DeFlock” movement. Last month,
protestors organized a demonstration outside Larsen's home.
According to transcripts from the podcast, Larsen suggested restrictions
that could be implemented in San Francisco, including reducing the
amount of time the police department keeps the footage. SF currently
retains Flock data for a year, far longer than the roughly 30-day period
that had previously been Flock’s standard — before Flock reduced it to
seven days amid mounting criticism.

He also pointed to New Hampshire, where state law requires license-
plate data to be purged within three minutes unless it is connected to a
criminal investigation. 
Several jurisdictions have moved to restrict the technology, per Business
Insider. Florida ordered Flock license-plate readers removed from state
highways last month, while Denver and Tempe, Arizona, have deactivated
their cameras.
The conversation also touched on Larsen’s push for clearer
cryptocurrency regulations. He told Swisher that Congress’s Digital Asset
Market Clarity Act would help establish whether cryptocurrencies are
treated as securities or commodities, which he considers the biggest
unresolved issue facing the industry.
Pointing to his four-year legal battle with the Securities and Exchange
Commission (SEC), Larsen argued that determining whether
cryptocurrencies fall under SEC or Commodity Futures Trading
Commission (CFTC) oversight would help create clearer rules and give
companies and consumers more certainty.
The House passed the bill in 2025, and a revised version advanced
through the Senate Banking Committee in May 2026. The Senate is
expected to take a procedural vote on September 15, with some
Democrats and Republicans warning its lack of sufficient safeguards
could destabilize the banking system.
