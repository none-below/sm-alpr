    Even the billionaire funding Flock cameras in San Francisco has concerns

    Crypto founder Chris Larsen, who has put more than $12 million toward police surveillance technology, said the data gathered should be wiped more often.

    By Omar RashadPolitics Reporter
    Published Sep. 17, 2026at2:38pm

    Even Chris Larsen — who has put millions into San Francisco’s police surveillance technology — thinks it’s worth dialing back certain aspects of Flock.

    The tech billionaire’s hourlong appearance on journalist Kara Swisher’s podcast, taped live this month at a conference in San Francisco (opens in new tab), revealed that Larsen’s own son isn’t a fan of automated license plate readers. 

    But the crypto founder also shared privacy concerns about how the cameras are used and suggested some changes. 

    ADVERTISEMENT

    “I think San Francisco could do — needs to do a better job,” Larsen told Swisher. “San Francisco keeps Flock information for a year. That’s way more than the average. The average is about 30 days.”

    Larsen’s suggestion would still leave San Francisco well outside Flock’s new recommended standard (opens in new tab). The company last month cut its default retention period for new law enforcement customers from 30 days to seven, amid mounting privacy concerns.

    Larsen last year made a $9 million commitment to the city’s police surveillance technology and committed $3 million more this summer (opens in new tab). 

    His donation last year helped establish the San Francisco Police Department’s new Real-Time Investigation Center, moving it away from City Hall to a former Ripple Labs office in the Financial District. 

    Larsen said there will always be a trade-off between public safety and privacy. But even with evolving police technology, like police drones, he said some areas should be censored. 

    “I would like to see those technologies blacking out people’s backyards, blacking out people’s decks, places that are private,” he told Swisher. 

    Pressure has been mounting on Flock in recent months. A Washington Post investigation (opens in new tab) revealed misuse of the license plate readers, including police officers using them to track former romantic partners. 

    The SFPD disclosed in June that its Flock network had been improperly searched nearly 300 times on behalf of federal and out-of-state agencies over roughly a year, despite state law and department policy restricting access. That was after a Standard investigation found that outside agencies had gone through San Francisco’s Flock cameras to make searches on behalf of federal immigration authorities. 

    Additionally, a San Francisco police officer improperly used Flock cameras to track down his wife’s stolen vehicle. A handful of Bay Area cities have also canceled their contracts with Flock following reports of police misuse and access by federal immigration authorities.

    The debate over the cameras isn’t over in San Francisco.

    Two of Flock’s employees sit on the San Francisco Democratic Party’s board. Additionally, the company’s contract with the city is up for renewal in February 2027.
