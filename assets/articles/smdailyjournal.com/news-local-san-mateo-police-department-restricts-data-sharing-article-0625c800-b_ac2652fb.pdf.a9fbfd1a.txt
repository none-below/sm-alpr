San Mateo Police Department restricts data sharing
Officials limit sharing permissions and implement strict database audits to identify potential
misuse
By Cecilia Schutz Daily Journal staff
Oct 10, 2026
 
The San Mateo Police Department has tightened the reins on its real time
information center technology, which includes the use and control of automated
license plate readers, body cameras and drones, by limiting sharing permissions
to select jurisdictions and implementing strict database audits to identify
potential data misuse.

These changes, presented at the City Council special meeting Monday, mirror the
recommendations issued by the San Mateo County Civil Grand Jury report,
released Aug. 3, that reviewed San Mateo County’s police agencies’ use of ALPRs.
The report didn’t find any illegal, out-of-state data-sharing as of December 2025,
but did see sharing with private universities and noted that unauthorized data
access and insufficient oversight mechanisms for the cameras remains a concern.
To bolster their data protections, the police significantly reduced the number of
agencies it was sharing data with, from over 280 at its height, to now just eight. It
has removed sharing with all university police departments, and is now requiring
every agency to sign a sharing agreement. San Mateo police can deny a sharing
request or terminate an agreement if an agency was involved in a data breach.
Requests for sharing are handled by police Lt. Steve Casazza.
“We’ve really limited our sharing just to have more control over the data and who
it gets to,” Casazza said.
Flock Safety, a technology used within RTIC to respond to emergencies,
apprehend people suspected of crimes and identify stolen vehicles and missing
people, offers permanent audit trails and automatic data deletion — a tool San
Mateo police have continued to use, with some modifications.
The agency started requiring case numbers, along with a computer-aided
dispatch number for emergency calls, and a search reason to supervise how the
database is being used, so San Mateo police can have more oversight and ensure
searches are for legitimate and justifiable purposes, said Casazza. Every search is
tied to an authorized user, and while users document the offense type, search
details are recorded into the database and audited monthly.
RTIC integrates real-time information from law enforcement technology, such as
live camera feeds, drones, license plate reading and mapping, to assist San Mateo
police officers with quick responses to emergencies and crimes.
Between 2024, the year RTIC was first established, and 2025, San Mateo police
observed a 25% drop in property crime and a 20% decrease in violent crime,

Nash said she felt “very impressed” with the department’s work on RTIC, and
wants to “support its continuance” through consistent data auditing.
“I have seen the incredible benefit that the community has received from this
technology, and I would be very interested in keeping a close eye on what is going
forward, and having periodic reporting to the council,” she said.
Echoing Diaz Nash, Councilmember Rob Newsom expressed the need to continue
auditing and the importance of this technology for the well-being of the general
public.
“I do want to make sure we emphasize that this is not just being used for crime
enforcement,” Newsom said. “It’s being used for other public safety and it does
serve and really assist and save lives in that respect.”
Although San Mateo police are not aware of any breaches to its Flock cameras,
heightened surveillance and data exposure remains a concern among some San
Mateo residents. Multiple community members spoke out against the use of these
cameras during the public comment portion of the special meeting.
“Mass surveillance cameras violate our civil rights and make us all less safe,”
Emily Morris, San Mateo resident and member of Safety Over Surveillance in San
Mateo, said. “They track us everywhere we go, regardless of whether or not we’re
suspected of a crime.”
On the other hand, San Mateo resident Michael Ragan, supports the technology,
and said he is willing to give up some of his personal privacy for “the good of our
public safety.”
“I think the rest of the community should think about it before they turn their
backs on this type of technology,” he said.
