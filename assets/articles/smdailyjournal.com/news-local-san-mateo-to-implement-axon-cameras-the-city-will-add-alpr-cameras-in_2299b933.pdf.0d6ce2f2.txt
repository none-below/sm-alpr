San Mateo to implement Axon cameras: The city will add
ALPR cameras in new contract with Axon Enterprises
By Cecilia Schutz Daily Journal staff
Oct 10, 2026
 
San Mateo decided to expand its contract with Axon Enterprise, a major supplier
of law enforcement technology, to include additional automated license plate
readers that may replace its current cameras, despite facing significant backlash
from residents in attendance at the City Council meeting Oct. 5.

The city is signing on to an eight-year purchase agreement with the company,
which includes a one-year free trial period and payments beginning in June 2028.
During the trial, the San Mateo Police Department will deploy Axon ALPRs to the
same locations as the current cameras supplied by Flock Safety, another
prominent law enforcement vendor, and conduct a side-by-side comparison to
determine which system is preferred. With enough sample data, SMPD expects to
identify the most suitable provider in the next six to nine months.
The decision to test out new ALPR hardware comes after the civil grand jury
report, issued Aug. 3, found deficiencies in the city’s Flock system, and cautioned
that unauthorized data access and insufficient oversight mechanisms for the
cameras is still a concern. While it remains to be seen if the implementation of
Axon ALPRs will phase out the city’s use of Flock cameras, Police Chief Ed
Barberini said that this opportunity with Axon is “unique” and would allow SMPD
“to provide the city with all of the available products.”
“We have taken a very responsible approach to ALPR data in the city,” Barberini
said. “We have to be conscious and aware of other issues Flock has had in the
state and across the country.”
Many neighboring cities have revisited or discontinued its use of Flock cameras
following nationwide scrutiny over the company’s data-sharing practices.
Mountain View announced its decision to turn its ALPRs off after finding that
hundreds of law enforcement agencies, including several federal entities, had
accessed its license plate data without authorization, the Mountain View Voice
reported.
A comprehensive audit from April 2023 to May 2026 revealed that various out-of-
state and federal departments had access to Redwood City’s data, leading the city
to not renew its contract with Flock, and seek out other vendors through a
request for proposals. Half Moon Bay also let its contract with Flock expire,
noting a general lack of distrust with the product.
In a unanimous motion, the City Council accepted its response to the civil grand
jury report. Several community members and activists at the meeting called upon
the city to cease its use of ALPR technology altogether, and strongly objected to

bringing in another vendor. 
“Many cities have found ALPRs made mistakes, or their data wasn’t properly
shared to their surprise, with devastating consequences,” said Emily Morris, a San
Mateo resident and member of Safety Over Surveillance San Mateo County, a
privacy advocacy group. “We cannot rush into a new ALPR vendor when the city
has not established the safeguards necessary for the next surveillance system.”
Kimberly Woo, a San Mateo resident and member of SOS, remarked on the
similarities between Axon and Flock, and said that switching vendors would make
no difference.
“Data privacy concerns do not disappear because the name on the camera
changes,” she said. “Axon is Flock’s evil twin and has received millions of dollars
from ICE. We simply cannot trust Axon to keep our data secure”
Axon Enterprise supplies Immigration and Customs Enforcement with body-worn
cameras and electroshock weapons such as Tasers, and has entered into a handful
of million-dollar contracts with the federal agency since 2020. President Donald
Trump also bought between $1 million and $5 million in Axon stock back in
February, which was noted by community members during public comment as a
reason not to continue contracting with the company.
“You are contributing to the horrible corruption that we’re seeing and the self
enrichment and degradation of our democracy that is happening at the national
level,” said Tim Mackenzie, San Mateo resident and member of SOS SMC,
addressing the council.
San Mateo Mayor Adam Loraine weighed tabling the contract for at least another
month to make time for additional community outreach, “knowing that the
contract might not be the same, and knowing that we risk losing the exact terms,”
he said.

Chief Barberini and Councilmember Rob Newsom advised against waiting any
longer to accept the contract, as the longer they wait, the greater chance they
may lose the initial free trial with Axon.
“The contract is open ended, so we have a full year to decide whether we are
going to pursue it,” Newsom said. “It doesn’t preclude us from having a town hall
six weeks from now, and it doesn’t preclude us from saying that the city changed
its mind.”
Councilmember Danielle Cwirko-Godycki referred to the city’s current agreement
with Axon, stating that the company has been supplying San Mateo with body
cameras and ALPR for law enforcement vehicles over the last 10 years. She said
the short-staffed police department, coupled with the growing city population,
warrants the continued use of the technology.
“Are we not thinking about the big picture here?” Cwirko-Godycki said. “We need
some type of mechanism to help supplement our officers with enforcement.”
Councilmembers Nicole Fernandez and Lisa Diaz Nash agreed to move forward
with the contract, under the condition that the city schedules a town hall meeting
by the beginning of the new year, to garner more community feedback on the use
of ALPR cameras in the city.
The motion to accept the contract passed in a 4-1 vote — with Loraine opposed.
The purchase agreement for ALPR cameras provided by Axon will cost the city a
little over $1.4 million, with a cumulative total of more than $8.4 million,
including the current contract. If continued, the contract for the Axon ALPR
cameras will expire in June 2034.
