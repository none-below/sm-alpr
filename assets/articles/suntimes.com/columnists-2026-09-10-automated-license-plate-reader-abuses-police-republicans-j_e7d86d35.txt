Greg Abbott and Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, both Republicans, reflect a bipartisan backlash against warrantless surveillance. By Jacob Sullum A Flock license plate reader is shown in August on Touhy Avenue near McCormick Boulevard in Skokie. Ashlee Rezin/Sun-Times SHARE COPY LINK Share CLOSE Twitter Facebook Bluesky Email Reddit Flipboard Last month, Texas Gov. Greg Abbott froze state funding for automated license plate readers, acknowledging concerns about the warrantless surveillance enabled by such artificial intelligence-assisted cameras. Abbott took that step the day after Florida Gov. Ron DeSantis, a fellow Republican, declared that license plate readers are “out of control.” Both governors cited recent reports about police officers accused of using license plate reader data for personal purposes , which seem to be driving a shift in public opinion about the technology. Such incidents highlight the perils of giving cops across the country easy access to such data, which they can use to track your travels, identify nearby vehicles and even predict your itinerary, without judicial oversight or meaningful internal supervision. Abbott mentioned a Lufkin, Texas, police officer who was charged with using license plate reader information to stalk an ex-girlfriend, her relatives, and her acquaintances more than 10,000 times. DeSantis alluded to similar cases in Florida, where officers in Sarasota and Haines City had recently been arrested for misusing license plate reader data. As of Tuesday, the Institute for Justice counted 181 local governments that have canceled or declined to renew license plate reader contracts since the beginning of 2025. In Texas alone, the Dallas Express reports , at least 23 jurisdictions have dropped contracts, suspended service or reduced the size of their license plate reader networks. Some 7,000 police departments have installed those networks, which include around 140,000 cameras, without giving much thought to the privacy implications or the potential for abuse. But some of them are now having second thoughts , reflecting rising public skepticism of unconstrained license plate reader use. In a YouGov survey last month, 46% of respondents opposed police use of license plate readers, including 31% who were “strongly” opposed, while 38% favored the practice. A year ago, by contrast, YouGov found that 45% of Americans supported license plate readers, while 33% opposed them, including 20% who were “strongly” opposed. That reversal coincides with proliferating reports of blatant license plate reader abuses . The Institute for Justice has identified “well over 100" such incidents, which it notes “may be just the tip of the iceberg” given the reality of lax oversight and widespread data-sharing. Police officers are supposed to use that information, which they can access on computers or mobile phones, only for legitimate law enforcement purposes. But as a Wichita police supervisor conceded in 2024, it’s “not even possible” to enforce that policy by making sure that each search complies with it. Compounding that challenge, each agency typically shares license plate reader information with many others. Once that happens, The Wichita Eagle noted in 2022, local police officials have “no control over how those outside agencies use it and no way to flag unlawful searches.” In Norfolk, Virginia, a local radio station reported last April, police officers often give “incomplete and imprecise reasons” for license plate reader searches, including “suspect,” “suspicious” and “investigation” or “inv.” Such vague labels hardly seem consistent with a state law requiring that cops state a “specific purpose,” including “the offense type,” thereby providing enough information to determine whether a search is legitimate. “The police cannot police themselves,” says Institute for Justice attorney Robert Frommer. His organization argues that a warrant generally should be required to access license plate reader data — a policy it aims to establish by challenging license plate reader networks on Fourth Amendment grounds and urging states to enact protective legislation . The congressional response to license plate readers, including several bills and an investigation that Sen. Josh Hawley, R–Mo., announced last month, has been dominated by conservative Republicans, which confirms that the backlash is not limited to libertarian or left-leaning critics. Whether it will yield effective constraints is another question. License plate reader are “easily containable,” Abbott says , a bit overoptimistically . “They’re going to be required to be used responsibly if they’re used at all.” Jacob Sullum is a senior editor at Reason magazine.
