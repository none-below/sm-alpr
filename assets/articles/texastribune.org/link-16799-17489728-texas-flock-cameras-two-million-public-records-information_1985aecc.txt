Texas city charges $2 million for Flock records as agencies make it harder to see how cameras are used by Ayden Runnels, The Texas Tribune October 5, 2026 HTML Plain Text <h1>Texas city charges $2 million for Flock records as agencies make it harder to see how cameras are used</h1> <p class="byline">by Ayden Runnels, The Texas Tribune <br />October 5, 2026</p> <div class="wp-block-custom-everlit-iframe-embed"></div> </p> <p>In the Fort Worth-area suburb of North Richland Hills, getting answers about how police use their Flock Safety cameras could cost $2.3 million.</p> <p>Or at least, that’s how much the city is charging a Flock watchdog — requiring it all be paid up-front — after he filed a public records request for information about the city’s AI-powered cameras. The invoice said it was accounting for labor costs of $15 an hour — equivalent to more than 14 years of work — to search about a terabyte worth of communications about errors, misuse and effectiveness of the Flock system.</p> <p> The North Richland Hills price tag is just the latest example of how local and state governments have increasingly made it more difficult to access information about Flock cameras as scrutiny over the surveillance system has intensified in recent months. </p> <p>Phil Mynona filed the records request in late August on behalf of his anti-surveillance group called the Texas Privacy Coalition. He’s filed nearly identical public record requests to almost 200 law enforcement agencies seeking to understand how they use the devices.</p> <p>Mynona is a pseudonym he has often used to speak publicly about Flock, because he said his job in the technology industry would be put at risk because of his advocacy. He has received troves of information from agencies, some at no cost and others with varying price tags for labor costs. Recently he said the city of Sealy, west of Houston, handed over more than 400,000 pages of documents for free.</p> <p>The multimillion dollar invoice from North Richland Hills struck him as “ludicrous” and a way of stifling his search for records.</p> <p>“This is a crazy outlier, I think the highest that we received in some amount was [$5,000] from Victoria, Texas, and at the time, I thought that was ridiculous,” he said.</p> <p>A North Richland Hills city spokesperson confirmed the public records invoice to The Texas Tribune but referred questions about their Flock camera use to a <a href="https://www.nrhtx.com/1226/What-People-Are-Talking-About">city website</a> with information about the cameras. </p> <p>Mynona has since narrowed the request for information, but the city did not respond when asked whether that would lower the cost. A Monday night <a href="https://www.facebook.com/nrh.pd/posts/pfbid02xUxbjwm2xdDjTpAcZvwZ1LBKnAKTVdP1V774or1rqrn74ZaHinjotecK4mua4k49l?rdid=kJeriTfZG2brSyvd#">social media post</a> from the police department, however, said the modified request’s cost is “expected to be significantly less.”</p> <p>The city’s post said the $2.3 million cost estimate was “understandably shocking,” but was calculated based on the amount of communications legally required to be reviewed within the almost terabyte worth of data. North Richland Hills Police Chief Jeff Garner said in a statement included in the post that the city’s use of Flock cameras reflected the “community’s expectations.”</p> <p>“As far as this latest record request, we always strive for transparency,” Garner said. “Our use of [license plate readers] adheres to these community principles and honors our commitment to pursue justice for victims of crime.”</p> <p>The Tribune has a similar pending request for records to the city.</p> <p>Flock cameras collect license plate numbers as well as other identifying information, including make, model, bumper stickers and dents, and officers can search thousands of cameras owned by hundreds of police agencies in a single query.</p> <p>Flock Safety, the Atlanta-based company, releases few details about its cameras, leaving disclosure and transparency decisions to be decided by the patchwork of both state and local governments. </p> <p>Camera locations, the amount of data retained and how often police are searching for vehicles are unknown in most cities across Texas unless departments explicitly share them, placing even more importance on receiving public records. </p> <p>Anti-surveillance groups have underscored the need for transparency surrounding the use of the devices, as information unearthed in public records requests has spurred investigations, arrests and prompted departments to change policies or conduct their first audits.</p> <p>That includes the case involving Lufkin Police, where an officer on Wednesday was <a href="https://www.ktre.com/2026/09/30/lufkin-officer-pleads-5-years-100-counts-related-flock-camera-misuse/">sentenced to 5 years</a> in prison after pleading guilty to 100 felony counts of abusing official information after he used Flock cameras to stalk several people.</p> <p>It was a public records request from <a href="https://www.usatoday.com/story/news/nation/2026/08/28/flock-camera-investigation-police-arrested-fired-surveillance/91383586007/">USA Today</a> that prompted both the investigation and an audit of the entire Lufkin Police Department — which flagged a second officer for potential misuse, according to records from the Department of Public Safety.</p> <p>Gov. Greg Abbott has pointed to that case as part of the reason for a “crackdown” on Flock cameras, which he joined in by <a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/08/28/texas-greg-abbott-flock-cameras-order-state-money/">blocking state funding</a> and <a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/09/17/texas-flock-camera-pause-highways-txdot/">pausing certain installations</a> for the surveillance cameras in August and September. </p> <p>Meanwhile, information provided for free by some law enforcement agencies has been held behind invoices costing thousands by others, while some agencies have denied requests to review data on camera locations and searches on the camera network.</p> <p>Houston news station KPRC was <a href="https://www.click2houston.com/news/local/2026/09/24/harris-county-sheriffs-office-quotes-enough-dollar121000-for-flock-records-as-hidalgo-plans-oct-15-meeting/">quoted $121,000</a> for Flock records, despite the news agency receiving thousands of pages’ worth of similar records from smaller police departments at no cost, it reported. Conservative online Texas news site Current Revolt reported the city of Irving is charging <a href="https://x.com/CurrentRevolt/status/2106049650813747436">$70,000 for records</a> that the city of Carrollton provided for free.</p> <p>In some cases, records provided publicly earlier this year by agencies have become more elusive.</p> <p>In response to a public records request for Flock audit data, DPS in July gave the Tribune a complete list of the department’s searches and thousands of other agencies using the agency’s Flock network, with officer and license plate numbers redacted. </p> <p>But when the Tribune made an identical request for the next few weeks of data in early August, DPS only provided records of its own searches and not that of outside agencies. </p> <p>When asked for a third set of search data shortly after, DPS withheld the records completely and challenged the request, asking the Attorney General’s Office to allow it to withhold the entirety of the search data it previously provided.</p> <p>DPS did not respond to a request for comment about the change in their response.</p> <p>The agency operates more than 900 of the estimated 13,000 Flock cameras statewide across state roads and along the state’s border. </p> <p>DPS recently reined in how many agencies are allowed to access its extensive network after <a href="https://www.texastribune.org/2026/09/04/texas-flock-cameras-dps-network-search-abbott/">a Tribune review</a> of its audit data revealed that 2,800 agencies, most of them outside of Texas, were accessing its cameras every four seconds without required agreements with DPS. </p> <p>The Texas Department of Transportation, which is required to approve permits for license plate readers placed along state roads and highways, declined a request to share the number of permits it approved and the locations of approved cameras. In a challenge sent to the Attorney General’s Office, TxDOT cited public safety concerns.</p> <p>DPS and Texas’ Department of Criminal Justice have also challenged record requests that would identify camera locations across Texas.</p> <p>Some city departments, however, are more forthcoming about where their cameras are placed. Police in the city of Live Oak, northeast of San Antonio, provide an <a href="https://liveoaktx.maps.arcgis.com/apps/instant/basic/index.html?appid=1fe4ad954b3346b9a7e2a3a337c5cfbf">interactive map</a> of their Flock camera locations, and Corinth in North Texas lists the <a href="https://transparency.flocksafety.com/corinth-tx-pd">street locations</a> of all of their cameras online.</p> <p>Flock provides agencies with their own avenues to be more transparent with how they use the devices, but whether departments use the features and how is up to them.</p> <p>Agencies’ audit data of searches is exportable through the click of a few buttons on Flock’s platform. The company also provides agencies with the option to host transparency portals that show how many cameras departments have, how many searches they conduct, how many vehicles are scanned and even allow search data to be directly downloaded from the site.</p> <p>“The portals give agencies a public-facing way to publish information about their [license plate reader] programs, including policies, usage and access rules to help communities better understand how the technology is being used,” Flock spokesperson Andrew Smith said in a statement.</p> <p>Out of the hundreds of agencies that use Flock cameras in Texas, only 60 make use of the transparency portals, the Tribune found, representing more than 2,600 of the estimated 13,000 cameras operating in the state. Of those, only 16 share their search records.</p> </p></p> <p>This &lt;a target=&quot;_blank&quot; href=&quot;https://www.texastribune.org/2026/10/05/texas-flock-cameras-two-million-public-records-information/&quot;&gt;article&lt;/a&gt; first appeared on &lt;a target=&quot;_blank&quot; href=&quot;https://www.texastribune.org&quot;&gt;The Texas Tribune&lt;/a&gt;.&lt;img src=&quot;https://i0.wp.com/www.texastribune.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/cropped-cropped-texas-tribune-favicon.png?resize=150%2C150&amp;amp;ssl=1&quot; style=&quot;width:1em;height:1em;margin-left:10px;&quot;&gt;</p> &lt;img id=&quot;republication-tracker-tool-source&quot; src=&quot;https://www.texastribune.org/?republication-pixel=true&amp;post=245210&quot; style=&quot;width:1px;height:1px;&quot;&gt;&lt;script&gt; PARSELY = { autotrack: false, onload: function() { PARSELY.beacon.trackPageView({ url: &quot;https://www.texastribune.org/2026/10/05/texas-flock-cameras-two-million-public-records-information/&quot;, urlref: window.location.href }); } } &lt;/script&gt; &lt;script id=&quot;parsely-cfg&quot; src=&quot;//cdn.parsely.com/keys/texastribune.org/p.js&quot;&gt;&lt;/script&gt;&lt;script async src=&quot;https://ping.texastribune.org/ping.js&quot; data-source=&quot;repub&quot; data-canonical=&quot;https://www.texastribune.org/2026/10/05/texas-flock-cameras-two-million-public-records-information/&quot; crossorigin=&quot;anonymous&quot;&gt;&lt;/script&gt; Canonical Tag: Copy Tag Article Content: Texas city charges $2 million for Flock records as agencies make it harder to see how cameras are used Ayden Runnels, The Texas Tribune October 5, 2026 In the Fort Worth-area suburb of North Richland Hills, getting answers about how police use their Flock Safety cameras could cost $2.3 million.
