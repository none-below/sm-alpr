More than a million people have had their faces scanned in a trial of real-time facial
recognition technology in Western Australia, resulting in 122 arrests and nine
incorrect alerts.

--- page 2 ---
WA police said under the five-month trial, which concluded on Saturday, the
technology scanned 1,380,882 faces after 100 deployments across 43 regional and
metropolitan locations.
Alongside the 122 arrests, the alerts led officers to engage with 146 registered sex
offenders and locate 10 people for a welfare check.
The deployments included three operations at the Perth royal
show, where 223,749 faces were scanned, resulting in 15 alerts, three arrests and
seven “interactions” with registered sex offenders.
A police spokesperson confirmed the system generated nine incorrect alerts against
the total 1.38m scans, meaning there was an error rate of 0.00065%.
Addressing the false matches at a media conference on Sunday, the WA police
commissioner, Col Blanch, confirmed officers engaged with each individual.
“I want to be very clear on those false alerts,” he said. “No one was arrested. No one
made a complaint. Each one did result in an interaction with that person and asking
them relevant questions.
“It’s a very, very, very low number. It’s not zero. We’d love it to be zero. But I think
the important thing for us is to engage with our community just like we would if a
human thought someone was a wanted person.”
Blanch acknowledged the technology was “not perfect” and that authorities “knew
this going into this trial”, but maintained that officers followed up on alerts in the
same way they would if a member of the public raised suspicions.

However, civil liberties advocates have criticised how the WA police framed the
error rate.
The chief executive of Civil Liberties Australia, Bill Rowlings, said WA police were
“severely gilding the lily” by claiming the incorrect alert rate was 0.00065% and
suggested it should instead be calculated by comparing how many of the arrests
resulted in convictions.
“We will only know what the incorrect rate is when the 122 people arrested have
their day in court,” he said.
“If half the 122 arrested … are not convicted, the ‘incorrect’ rate will be 50%,” he said
in a statement, saying police had performed the almost 1.4m scans “with no
reasonable suspicion whatsoever”.

--- page 3 ---
Sarah Schwartz, legal director at the Human Rights Law Centre, said every false
match is “a real person who may be stopped, questioned or investigated because an
algorithm got it wrong”.
“The consequences of those mistakes have real consequences for the people of
Western Australia.”

She questioned if it was “proportionate in a democratic society” to conduct
“biometric surveillance on an extraordinary scale” of more than a million people –
and raised particular concern over the deployment of surveillance vans at public
demonstrations.
When the trial was announced, the state’s independent privacy watchdog, the office
of the information commissioner WA (OIC WA), said it was not involved in
implementing the trial, warning of “collective privacy harms” in a statement. The
OIC WA was contacted for comment.

The police surveillance vans have now been removed from public deployment while the trial is evaluated.

On Sunday, Blanch said the trial “focused on using modern technology responsibly
to identify people who presented a risk, while allowing the vast majority of people
to go about their day without any impact”.
Under the trial guidelines, cameras mounted on or positioned near marked police
vehicles scanned faces in designated public areas, matching them against an alert
list drawn from police databases. Police said images of people who weren’t on the
list were pixelated in real time and deleted immediately without being stored.
The trial previously triggered backlash over concerns about privacy and algorithmic
bias. Experts say the technology could disproportionately affect marginalised groups
who are already overrepresented on police watchlists, with facial recognition
algorithms historically having higher false-match rates for people with darker skin
tones.
Schwartz said the governance framework surrounding the technology remained
opaque.
“WA police have provided remarkably little detail about the safeguards governing
this technology,” she said. “Their publicly available policies on the use of this
