The city of Oshkosh has rescinded its contract with Flock Safety for automated license
plate readers.
The decision was made after the Oshkosh Police Department stated the company
misrepresented information about the cameras' capabilities.
Concerns from the public included privacy erosion, data sharing, and potential misuse by
law enforcement.
The reversal came less than 24 hours after the council had initially approved a one-year
renewal amid community pushback.

unanimously not to renew the contract less than 24 hours after approving an

What led to Smith's issues with Flock Safety?
Addressing Common Council at a special April 22 meeting, Smith said he was
immediately advised by officers that Flock cameras create a “heat map,”
directly refuting claims to the contrary from Flock Chief Information Security
Officer Chris Castaldo.

Castaldo told councilor Brad Spanbauer the images captured by Flock
cameras do not create a pattern of movement for vehicles while also claiming
federal agencies couldn’t access Oshkosh’s data without OPD’s knowledge.
Saying “integrity and trust is important to us all,” Smith said he met with City
Manager Rebecca Grill and advised city staff to immediately revoke the
contract.
Oshkosh now joins regional municipalities in Verona, Sturgeon Bay and Dane
County in moving on from the contentious license plate readers.
Are the Flock cameras still active?
Assistant Chief of Police Brian Schuldes advised the Northwestern that the
OPD will be powering off each of Oshkosh's 26 Flock cameras on April 23
while covering the license plate readers with a bag to ensure they cannot
collect data.
Schuldes also revealed that any access by outside agencies has been revoked
while all internal users have had their accounts deactivated with the
exception of the city's Flock Administrator.

What happened at the previous council meeting?
The decision to reverse course came as a shocking turn of events, happening
less than one full day after Oshkosh voted in favor of retaining the license
plate readers despite numerous public outbursts amid major community
pushback.
Common Council did initially opt to shorten the initial two-year, $163,500
contract proposal to a one-year deal, taking into account public comments on
the floor at the April 21 meeting by proposing the establishment of a
committee to review the Flock contract.
But that came after community members stormed out of the meeting
following no fewer than two public outbursts where one person screamed
“liar, liar” at Castaldo.
How did the public react to the city rescinding the Flock
contract?
The mood of Wednesday’s session was significantly more tranquil with 13
community members praising Smith for his humility, honesty and
transparency in revealing the discrepancy in public.
Deputy Mayor Karl Buelow even apologized to the community for “making the
wrong decision” before council voted in unison to end the one-year deal.
What are Flock cameras and what are they used for?
Solar-powered cameras, Flock Safety readers are essentially tools mounted
on poles used to capture the rear of vehicles and analyze license plates to
provide other vehicle data in a system.

The company’s client base is composed of more than 5,000 law enforcement
agencies, who can search the system for plate numbers, vehicle color, vehicle
type or a distinguishable feature to receive real-time alerts tracking stolen
automobiles or vehicles associated with warrants or a crime.
Oshkosh previously deployed 26 of those Flock cameras across city streets,
with council unanimously approving their implementation in 2022 through a
state grant covered by Gov. Tony Evers’ COVID relief funds.
Why did community members oppose Flock cameras?
The renewal would have come at a cost to the city while the public pointed to
concerns about possible infringement of Fourth Amendment rights, privacy
erosion, security vulnerabilities, the company’s data sharing and law
enforcement misuse.
In February, Mountain View City Council in California terminated its contract
with Flock after an investigation revealed more than 250 unapproved
agencies conducted around 600,000 searches of the city’s records over the
span of a year.
Both Milwaukee and Menasha have also seen police officers charged after
using the Flock system to personally track vehicles belonging to women.
