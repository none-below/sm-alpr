CHARLOTTESVILLE, Va. (WVIR) - As concerns continue to mount over automated license plate
readers (ALPRs) and how law enforcement can use them to track drivers, a bill has been
proposed in the General Assembly to rein in the technology.
“Automatic license plate readers are a tool of mass surveillance, so they capture not only the
license plate as the name suggests, but the make and model of the vehicle as well,” said Ruby
Cherian, an attorney with the Legal Aid Justice Center in Charlottesville.
Cherian says there are currently zero state laws regulating the use of ALPRs in Virginia. Some
have objected to these cameras due to privacy concerns.
“[The readers] can reveal personal information about someone,” said Cherian. “For instance, if
there’s ones placed outside of a doctor’s office, or a church, it’s really easy to tell the comings
and goings of people, especially if you have a lot of cameras that cover a city.”
Charlottesville Police Department has ten Flock cameras, which are scattered across the city on
different intersections. According to the city’s Flock transparency portal, these cameras have
detected 105,065 cars in the past 30 days and prompted police to conduct 133 searches in that
same time period.
Police Chief Michael Kochis says Charlottesville’s local Flock policy is one of the strictest in the
state, and that they are already in line with what HB2724 would require.
“I am not a big fan of technology,” said Chief Kochis. “I fear, at times, as a profession, we may be
moving faster than our communities understand it, and so why for us, it was so important to take
our time.”
Charlottesville only holds onto Flock data for seven days, which is well within the bill’s proposed
30-day retention period. They also only use the cameras for criminal justice purposes, such as
homicides, missing persons, or stolen vehicles.
“The amount of stolen vehicles we’ve recovered from the use of these cameras have been
staggering, and if you think about the importance of recovering a stolen vehicle, we’re talking
about someone who don’t have a vehicle to go to work anymore,” said Kochis. “Every day that
vehicle is gone, they’ve got to figure out another way to go to work or pick up their kids.”
Cherian says that CPD’s local policy entails a monthly public audit with information about how
they are using the cameras, but she has not seen any audit on the transparency portal yet.

In Albemarle County, police are relaunching plate reader software for the first time in about a
decade. Jason Marden, Special Operations Commander for ACPD, says these are different from
the Flock cameras because they are mobile and part of the cameras already installed in their
cars.
“The officer will then be able to look at that, find the vehicle, and then run that through the normal
procedure,” said Marden. “There’s still a lot of checks and balances along the way throughout the
system to make sure it’s used properly.”
ACPD will be activating this software, which detects license plates in real time as officers drive, in
five of their cars. Each one costs $500 per year.
“The system that we’re operating is really just getting extra eyes and getting more than what the
officer can watch,” said Marden.
