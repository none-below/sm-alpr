CHARLOTTE, N.C. (WBTV/Gray News) - Records obtained from the University of North Carolina
at Charlotte show that out-of-state police departments are searching the college’s network of
Automatic License Plate Recognition cameras far more than local law enforcement.
The agency that searched the UNC Charlotte network the most between March and May 2026
was the Houston Police Department. The law enforcement agency conducted searches that
included the UNC Charlotte network more than 183,000 times from March through May 2026.
Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police ranked 30th in total searches of the UNC Charlotte network. UNC
Charlotte Police Department ranked 1,491st, with 229 searches over the three-month period.
Cameras feed a nationwide database
As cars drive by the Flock cameras on the UNC Charlotte campus, the cameras capture license
plate numbers and add them to a nationwide database that law enforcement agencies across the
country can search. Searching the data and the license plates they capture is limited to law
enforcement, but there are also cameras owned by private companies and HOA’s that are
included in the network.
Records from UNC Charlotte show that when Houston officers searched a license plate in the
Flock system, they often searched more than 6,000 networks across the country, while many
other departments appeared to limit their searches to fewer networks.
Students approached on campus were unable to guess which agencies searched the network
most.
“Houston, Texas?!? Wow. OK. Interesting!!” said Verity Morgan, a student who recently hosted a
campus debate on Flock cameras.
“Houston??? Why is Houston looking at Charlotte?” said Radhika Chaudha, a student.
“I mean what rights do people have now? You’re losing your right to privacy and you don’t have a
choice,” Chaudha said.

“In my personal opinion, I don’t see why they’re necessary for our campus,” Morgan said.
“Money could definitely go to different things, I would say. And then it’s kind of creepy that people
in Houston are trying to figure out what we got going on over here,” said Peyton Marshall, a
student.
In an email statement sent to WBTV, the Houston Police Department said, “The use of automated
license plate recognition systems by Houston Police Department personnel is regularly reviewed.
The department has identified usage that is being investigated further.”
Attorney tracking flock camera use statewide
Mark Williams, an attorney in Wilmington, North Carolina, runs a website dedicated to opposing
Flock cameras and other license plate reading technology. His records requests have helped
reveal which police agencies are searching Flock networks on college campuses across the
state.
“This is a mass government surveillance system that has been built throughout the state of North
Carolina,” Williams said.
Williams warned that while there are rules in North Carolina limiting what law enforcement can
use the data for, other agencies in other states lack some of the same guidelines. Meaning UNC
Charlotte can’t control how the data their cameras capture is used.
“Under North Carolina law, you can’t sell the data. Texas has no guardrails. Most of those places
don’t have rules against selling the data, how it’s used, and who has access to it. That’s a
problem,” Williams said.
University responds
UNC Charlotte denied WBTV’s request for an interview with Police Chief Jeffrey Baker.
The university’s Director of Media Relations wrote in an email: “UNC Charlotte participates in a
shared law enforcement network that includes approximately 1,600 agencies. When a law

The university said the technology has helped University police in a wide variety of cases,
including the arrest of a suspect in a larceny investigation involving more than $100,000 in
equipment stolen from an on-campus construction site. The university has not provided the arrest
record nearly a month after it was first requested by WBTV.
UNC Charlotte said it does not share network access with non-law enforcement entities such as
homeowner associations and private businesses. Records show some other police agencies that
searched the network include CSX railroad police and the parks department in Toledo, Ohio.
UNC Charlotte is not required to share its network with other law enforcement agencies.
Appalachian State University turned off national sharing of its Flock network, and UNC Chapel
Hill made the same decision last week.
There have been no accusations of misuse of the Flock network at UNC Charlotte. The university
says it regularly reviews the system, and officers are required to use specific case numbers any
time they search the network.
North Carolina does not require reporting of misuse of the Flock system. Past cases of misuse
have come to light when officers were arrested for misusing the system, including at the
Charlotte-Mecklenburg Police Department and Mooresville Police Department, or when a
department voluntarily reported misuse.
