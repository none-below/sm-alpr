WASHINGTON, DC. (InvestigateTV) - Facing mounting federal scrutiny following months of
InvestigateTV and Atlanta News First reporting, the leaders of America’s major surveillance
companies were no-shows Wednesday for a Senate hearing probing how police officers have
abused automated license plate readers to stalk civilians and fellow officers.
The hearing before the Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism, titled
“Always Watching: Flock’s Nationwide AI Surveillance Network,” came directly in the wake of an
extensive series by InvestigateTV.
“The reporting on what these cameras can do is really pretty wild,” said U.S. Sen. Josh Hawley,
R-Missouri, who chairs the subcommittee. “The Constitution of the United States is still just as
valid and applicable today as it was almost 250 years ago. We have to find a way to protect and
enforce the Bill of Rights for every law-abiding citizen of the United States.”
Hawley noted the hearing’s purpose was to “figure out what is going on” with automated
surveillance cameras, adding, “If you haven’t heard of Flock, Flock has probably heard of you.”
Hawley pointed out that none of the chief executives invited to testify appeared at the hearing:
Garrett Langley, CEO of Flock Safety; Rick Smith, CEO of Axon Enterprise; Greg Brown, CEO of

Flock Safety operates more than 120,000 automated license plate reader (ALPR) cameras
across the country, capturing millions of vehicle movements daily.
The data is retained and searchable by law enforcement agencies across jurisdictions for 30
days.
While Flock launched an AI-powered “audit assistance” tool earlier this year to flag suspicious
activity for municipal supervisors, reporting showed that victims often only discovered improper
searches through independent public records requests and third-party tools like
haveibeenflocked.com.
Privacy advocates and public records watchdogs also raised alarms after Flock removed officer
names from external network searches and stripped license plate numbers entirely from system
audit logs, severely limiting the public’s ability to verify whether their vehicles had been searched.

behind bars before a subsequent investigation proved law enforcement had identified the wrong

Other witnesses testified modern, interconnected cloud networks and artificial intelligence make
today’s camera systems fundamentally different from traditional license plate readers.
“We are a long way from a simple automated license plate reader,” Hawley noted, following
testimony from Alasdair Whitney, legislative counsel for the Institute for Justice, who pointed out
that cameras linked to cloud networks represent a drastic shift.
Whitney urged Congress to establish firm federal guardrails, including requiring law enforcement
to obtain search warrants prior to scouring historical license plate databases.
“The Fourth Amendment is a floor, not a ceiling,” Whitney said.

Sheriff Ross Teeple of Pinal County, Arizona, testified the latest AI-powered cameras pose a
threat to constitutional rights. While Teeple noted the technology’s utility in criminal investigations
— citing a local case where ALPRs successfully tracked an aggressive driver who pushed a
woman’s car into oncoming traffic — he voiced alarm over transparency.
Teeple said he grew concerned after asking Flock Safety about the use of AI in its equipment.
Though initially told no AI was utilized, he later found company statements contradicting that
claim.
“The response I received was chilling,” Teeple testified. “I was told, ‘That wasn’t the AI you were
asking about.’”
Benn Jordan, an independent security researcher who discovered dozens of Flock cameras
streaming openly online, warned lawmakers about systemic digital vulnerabilities.
“Why create this much data when we can’t secure it?” Jordan asked, urging Congress to ensure
any regulations placed on Flock apply universally across the surveillance industry, including
competitors like Axon.
Civil liberties advocates call for federal guidelines
Chad Marlow, senior policy counsel for the American Civil Liberties Union, told the committee
venture capital investment has transformed public-safety tools into an unchecked surveillance
industry.
“The explosion of ALPR use and its tracking of innocent people reflects a view that if ALPR use is
scaled up, it could produce the massive profits venture capitalists seek,” Marlow said. “Welcome
to the surveillance-industrial complex, America.”
Marlow testified that Flock’s existing safeguards fail to address the core problem of systemic
misuse and urged Congress to use its spending powers to halt the technology entirely.
