BACKGROUND:

comment on the proposed operating budget and Capital Improvement Program (CIP) budget. This review of the budgets is 
the second of those two public hearings. 
Staff presented a preview of the 2026-27 General Fund budget and the 2026–2031 CIP on April 20, 2026, and provided the 
final proposed budget at the first public hearing on the FY 2026-27 proposed budget and five-year CIP on June 1, 2026. In 
addition, the Measure S Oversight Committee recommended that the City Council approve the Measure S-funded 
programs and capital improvement projects at its meeting on May 21, 2026.

Pension Contributions
A key component of the cost of employee compensation is the unfunded accrued liability (UAL) with the California Public
Employees’ Retirement System (CalPERS). The UAL is the amortized cost of CalPERS’ investment gains/losses as well as
changes in actuarial assumptions as they impact the funding available and needed for the City’s pension obligations. 
Citywide UAL pension costs are expected to continue to go up in FY 2026-27 as they did in FY 2025-26. For FY 2025-26, the 
Citywide pension total increased $1.0 million while an increase of $1.7 million is expected in FY 2026-27 to $25.5 million. 
Given the current state of the pension fund, the City’s overall UAL pension costs are estimated to gradually keep increasing 
through FY 2030-31, after which they are expected to gradually ramp down, provided CalPERS can achieve its 6.8% annual 
investment return target for future years and there are no major changes to actuarial assumptions.

GANN LIMIT
In addition to the adoption of the FY 2026-27 budget and capital improvement program, there is another action requested 
of Council as part of the overall budget adoption process on June 15th. This action is to establish the City’s appropriations 
limit for FY 2026-27. The appropriations limit, which was originally established in 1979 by Proposition 4, places a maximum 
limit of the appropriation of tax proceeds that can be made by the state, school districts, and local governments in 
California. California Government Code requires that the City annually adopt an appropriations limit for the upcoming 
fiscal year. The appropriations limit for the City for FY 2026-27 is $300,145,069, while the proceeds of taxes subject to the 
appropriations limit is $145,976,000. Therefore, the City is well below its appropriations limit for FY 2026-27.

City Manager’s Letter of Transmittal 
Honorable Mayor and Members of the City Council: 
I am pleased to present for your review and consideration the proposed Fiscal Year (FY) 2026-27 operating budget and the 
FY 2026-27 Capital Budget along with the City’s Capital Improvement Program (CIP) for the next five years. This budget 
reflects our commitment to maintaining core City services and making strategic investments in our community, while 
pursuing long-term fiscal health in a challenging and unpredictable environment.  
The proposed citywide budget, including both operating and capital, is $356.3 million for fiscal year 2026-27. This budget 
focuses on funding core services, Council priorities, and community needs. The five-year CIP reflects the City’s 
commitment to improve and maintain City facilities and infrastructure, by investing $350 million over the next five years in 
capital projects.  
Overall, the Citywide budget does increase in expenditures, which reflects growing operational pressure as inflationary 
impacts and rising labor costs increasingly affect the cost of delivering services. Costs tied to outside goods and services, 
utilities, insurance, and personnel continue to trend upward, reflecting broader economic conditions that have materially 
increased the City’s ongoing operating costs despite efforts to maintain a stable budget. Ongoing obligations such as 
payments toward unfunded pension liabilities also continue to place demands on the budget. In addition, funding 
challenges limit the City’s ability to continually maintain and modernize aging facilities and infrastructure, with over $348 
million in unfunded capital needs over the next five years. As the City continues to navigate this cost escalation, this budget 
proposes minimal programmatic expansion for FY 2026-27 given the estimated structural deficit tied to the provision of 
essential services.  
Simultaneously, over the past year, the City’s revenues have continued to face pressure, and the economic outlook has 
become increasingly uncertain. Factors such as global instability, persistently high inflation and interest rates, flattening 
sales tax revenues, and shortfalls in State Vehicle License Fee (VLF) funding are all affecting the City’s financial picture. A 
recent proposed ballot measure to eliminate the City’s real property transfer tax presents an added threat. In addition, there 
are broader risks to state and federal funding that could further impact local resources. 
Taken together, these factors present a risk to the City’s long-term financial stability. Owing to careful financial 
management by the City Council, both current and past, the City has built up reserves that can help bridge this gap in the 
short term. However, it is critical to balance this short-term solution with a proactive plan that ensures long-term 
sustainability. This includes maintaining core services, investing in critical infrastructure, managing costs responsibly, 
pursuing grant opportunities and increased economic development, and identifying reliable, ongoing sources of revenue – 
all facets of the City’s active Financial Sustainability Plan.  
Financial Sustainability Plan 
While we continue to closely monitor these fiscal challenges, we are taking proactive steps through our Financial 
Sustainability Plan. The plan is already underway, with several actions in progress this past year and additional measures 
planned to help strengthen the City’s financial future. Our first step has been to implement expenditure controls through 
freezing ten vacant positions, assessing and shifting appropriate expenses to Non-General Fund sources, generating 
benefit savings by reducing Worker’s Compensation allocations given a robust existing balance, and implementing 
various operating budget savings through contract renegotiations. These measures are estimated to save us 
approximately $3.2 million. The FY 2026-27 budget also proposes $2 million less in General Fund funding for capital 
projects to help offset some of the anticipated operating deficit.

Tax Revenue Uncertainties 
San Mateo relies on tax revenues to fund our core services. Although secured property tax, the City’s largest tax revenue, 
remains a stable revenue source, this revenue base is projecting a moderate 4.0% annual growth for the next few years. 
Sales tax, as the City’s second largest tax revenue, is projected to stay flat with a moderate 1-2% annual growth. The 
City’s third largest tax revenue, property transfer tax, fluctuates with economic conditions and the housing market. Due to 
high interest rates and a stable, but slow housing market, property transfer tax is anticipated to grow moderately with a 
$9.0 million revenue projection for the upcoming year. However, recent potential efforts to eliminate Real Property 
Transfer Tax at the state level pose a threat to this essential revenue source.  
In addition, the City continues to face major financial uncertainty due to the State’s property tax in-lieu of vehicle license 
fee (VLF). VLF is a key component of the City’s property tax revenues, making up roughly 18% of our total property tax 
revenues, and about 8% of the total General Fund revenues. The absence of reliable funding sources for VLF from the 
State of California has become a major threat to the City’s fiscal sustainability. 
The City’s FY 2026-27 proposed budget assumes that the State will not honor its VLF obligations to the City. Our share of 
FY 2023-24 county-wide VLF shortfalls is $6.6 million. Of this total, only $4.4 million has been backfilled to date, leaving 
an unrestored backfill of $2.2 million. In addition, the City does not anticipate receiving any portion of the $6.9 million FY 
2024-25 shortfall repayment during FY 2026-27. These unrecovered funds have already exacerbated the City’s budget 
deficit and will continue to do so should VLF shortfalls remain unreimbursed.  
Pension Costs 
Pension obligations continue to place a significant strain on our budget. CalPERS pension contribution requirements have 
increased significantly over the years due to a number of factors, including CalPERS lower-than-expected investment 
returns in prior years and changes in actuarial assumptions. The City’s unfunded pension liability (UAL) is currently about 
$290 million. Citywide UAL pension costs are expected to continue to go up in FY 2026-27 as they did in FY 2025-26. For 
FY 2025-26, the General Fund pension total increased $1.1 million while an increase of $1.3 million is expected in FY 
2026-27. Some of the increase is due to historic changes to the unfunded pension liability resulting from the CalPERS’s 
investment loss of -6.2% in FY 2021-22 and the 5.8% investment gain in FY 2022-23, which did not meet the CalPERS 
investment projections. In the last two complete fiscal years, CalPERS met its investment objectives with a 9.3% return in 
FY 2023-24 and a 12.1% in FY 2024-25. Other increases to the City’s UAL are mostly due to updates to the actuarial 
assumptions. Given this history, the City’s overall UAL pension costs are estimated to gradually keep increasing through 
FY 2030-31, after which pension requirements are expected to gradually ramp down, provided CalPERS can achieve its 
6.8% annual investment return target for future years.

Aging City Facilities and Infrastructure  
It is well-acknowledged that resources such as parks, open space, playgrounds, libraries, vibrant recreation facilities, 
smooth and safe streets, storm and flood controls, and clean water infrastructure are the essence of a thriving community. 
The City is committed to continuing to invest in our infrastructure and improve our facilities to a level the community 
deserves. However, maintaining and improving our aged City facilities and infrastructure requires significant funding, and 
right now, our resources are limited.    
 
Streets and Transportation  
Currently, we rely on special revenue sources, including Gas Tax (state gas taxes), Measures A and W (countywide 
transportation focused sales taxes), Measure M (countywide vehicle license fee), and Road Maintenance and 
Rehabilitation Account (SB-1 state gas tax) funds, totaling about $9.2 million in FY 2026-27 to fund streets and 
transportation projects. However, the annual cost of maintaining our streets alone nears $8.0 million, leaving little for other 
critical projects. To address the funding gap, we often turn to the General Fund and Measure S for extra funding support. 
This may become more and more challenging as the General Fund itself is experiencing a significant budget deficit. 
 
Aging City Facilities  
The General Fund, which supports all City facility repairs and improvements, faces increasing pressure. Although General 
Fund and Measure S combined provide about $6.4 million annually to fund CIP projects, the FY 2026-27 contribution 
proposes a $2 million reduction in General Fund funding for capital projects to help offset some of the anticipated 
operating deficit. Additionally, the Measure S allocation is reduced to allow the fund to build, given that significant capital 
budgeting of Measure S funds over the last two years has committed much of the fund’s balance. At the same time, the 
budget recommends a one-time Measure CC contribution of $1.25 million to help address critical infrastructure needs and 
offset the lack of General Fund resources. Overall, the anticipated contribution totals from the General Fund, Measure S, 
and Measure CC are proposed at $4.2 million, with $2.25 million from the General Fund, $0.7 million from Measure S, and 
$1.25 million from Measure CC.

The forecast, based on the middle-ground or “practical” forecasting scenario, assumes that the State and the County will 
not reach an agreement to fix the VLF funding issue in time for any shortfall reimbursement in FY 2026-27. Beginning in 
FY 2027-28, the model assumes that the VLF shortfall will be backfilled at 67 percent. Under this scenario, the City can 
maintain its service levels for a few more years. However, if economic conditions do not improve and no actions are taken 
to address the budget imbalance, we may risk depleting two of the buckets that make up the City’s General Fund 
Reserves by FY 2028-29, including the City’s Budget Stabilization and Pension Stabilization Reserves.  Furthermore, by 
FY 2030-31, our General Fund balance may fall short of maintaining the 15% Contingency Reserve. 
 
To balance the budget, the twofold strategy is straightforward – increase revenues and/or reduce spending. Given the 
current economic uncertainty and unresolved VLF shortfall issue, our revenues are expected to remain flat with only 
moderate annual growth. This growth will be insufficient to keep pace with our expenditures. To maintain the City’s fiscal 
sustainability, we are implementing the measures outlined in the Financial Sustainability Plan as well as continuing to 
evaluate all the other potential revenue enhancement and expense reduction options. Revenue enhancement options 
include nurturing the economic health of our local businesses, supporting the development of new housing units, 
reviewing and optimizing fee increases, seeking grant funding, assessing new revenue opportunities, and considering 
potential future revenue ballot measures. At the same time, the City may employ citywide cost containment strategies to 
reduce expenses, such as reducing discretionary spending, cutting non-essential programs, implementing hiring freezes, 
eliminating current vacancies, reducing future vacancies through attrition, and/or implementing furloughs with 
corresponding service reductions. Additionally, investment in capital projects may be reduced or deferred.   
 
The practical scenario largely adheres to the status quo as we wait for economic conditions to develop. Several variables 
will impact our financial outlook in the coming years. If the housing market ramps up, our tax revenue forecast may 
improve. If inflation returns to the Federal Reserve’s 2% target, it will ease some of the rising cost pressures we are 
currently facing. Also, as long as the current economic uncertainties are managed and we continue to avoid a recession, 
the City has time to prepare for improved financial sustainability. And we will continue to advocate for VLF funding and 
look for a long-term solution. While we must be prepared for the worst, we hope that these factors, will turn in our favor. 
 
Future of San Mateo 
As the City manages this uncertain fiscal outlook, we anticipate greater clarity at mid-year regarding key factors, including 
VLF revenues, the status of Real Property Transfer Tax, and the development of global factors. In the interim, the City is 
fully committed to maintaining high-quality services and prioritizing strategic investments in infrastructure while upholding 
the Council’s Strategic Plan. Utilizing the information and resources available to us, we will act with intention, 
transparency, and prudence. The current year represents a period of careful monitoring and evaluation while the variables 
at hand evolve. As conditions unfold and we obtain additional insights, we anticipate providing more frequent updates to 
support timely, informed decisions that best serve the community.
