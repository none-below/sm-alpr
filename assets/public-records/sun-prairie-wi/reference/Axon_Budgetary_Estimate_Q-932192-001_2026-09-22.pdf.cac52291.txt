Axon ALPR is built around agency control, system safeguards,
and an audit record the agency can review and share to meet
the expectations of the communities it serves.
What Sets Us Apart
Purpose-built security
For nearly 2 decades, Axon has securely
managed agencies' most sensitive data,
from body camera footage to public safety
records. Our solutions are purpose built to
ensure security.
Agencies own and control their data
Agencies own their data outright, including
how it's accessed and exported. Data stays
with each agency, with access granted only
at their explicit direction.
Built responsibly, from the start
Axon engages with stakeholders from the
earliest stages of product design, including
its Ethics & Equity Advisory Council, public
safety professionals, academic researchers,
and community voices. Axon takes
the weight of that responsibility to
communities seriously.
Federal access: There is
no automatic, standing
or built-in federal access
to agency ALPR data
through Axon.
Lawful requests: If Axon
receives a lawful request, it
directs the request to the
agency as the data owner.
Access control: Recipient
agencies' access to data
they receive does not
include the ability to
extend that access to
others. The originating
agency retains full control
over who can access
its data.
Data security: Axon does
not access agency data,
other than for support
requests, which are time-
boxed, logged, and revoked
once resolved.
Agency ownership:
Agencies own all hardware
- from poles, to permits, to
cameras. Switching
providers or adding other
vendors' equipment to
that infrastructure is
entirely the agency's call.
KEY SAFEGUARDS
Plates, not people
Axon ALPR captures license plates, not identities — no facial recognition,
no biometric matching, no profiles of people or owners. Each detection logs
a plate image, a vehicle context image, basic attributes (color, make, type),
and time/location, checked only against hotlists an agency has authorized.
Access permissions and search controls
Separate permissions control who can search detections and hits, manage
hotlists and administer the system. Every ALPR search requires a stated
reason and an offense category the agency has authorized, and each
agency can choose whether to require a case number.
Agency-configured access & retention
Every data search is subject to the originating agency's authorized offense
categories, and recipients see only detections matching their own hotlists.
Agencies set retention independently for detections and hits, and data is
automatically removed when the retention period expires.
Audit logs and reporting
Agencies can generate and export audit reports covering ALPR activity,
data use, access controlled relationships and hotlist changes.
Public transparency
Agencies can publish aggregate ALPR information, including hit-alert
counts and current data partnerships. The portal does not publish plate
numbers, images, individual records or camera locations.
CJIS compliance & data security
Built for CJIS from the ground up. Axon Cloud Services meet the FBI's CJIS
Security Policy across all 13 policy areas, with tamper-proof audit trails on
every evidence access, and controls independently validated each year via
SOC 2 Type 2 with CJIS validation.
ONGOING ENHANCEMENTS
Additional access controls
Expands agencies' ability to configure access, building on the existing
safeguards, including requiring a full plate or case number before a search
proceeds, plus other field-level requirements agencies can define.
Automated anomaly flagging and reporting
Proactively flags unusual search patterns, such as the same plate searched
repeatedly or under different case numbers, for administrative review, and
adds a recurring audit digest summarizing activity automatically.
Outcome reporting
Records outcomes from ALPR searches and hotlist follow-ups into
standardized audit and transparency reporting, with more flexibility for
agencies to filter, view, and export outcome data.
Axon is committed to listening, learning, and continuing to
invest in additional safeguards to help agencies and
communities realize the full lifesaving potential of this
technology, while protecting the privacy of communities
at large. Axon's platform also helps operationalize
compliance with federal, state, and local policy —
automating retention schedules and generating audit-
ready reports mapped to each agency's specific
regulatory requirements.

Please see below court cases regarding automatic license plate reader technology on how it relates to the
4 amendment and privacy concerns.

Wisconsin
e City of Horicon v. Albert, No. 99-0729/99-0730 (Wis. Ct. App. Sept. 30, 1999) — Wisconsin Court of
Appeals held a random license-plate check was not a search and recognized no reasonable
expectation of privacy in a publicly displayed plate.
e State v. Folkman, No. 2013AP1363-CR (Wis. Ct. App. Dec. 17, 2013) — unpublished decision holding
that querying registration/license information did not invade a reasonable expectation of privacy.
Seventh Circuit
e United States v. Tuggle, 4 F.4th 505 (7th Cir. 2021) — prolonged pole-camera surveillance did not
constitute a search.
e United States v. House, 120 F.4th 1313 (7th Cir. 2024) — reaffirmed Tuggle and declined to adopt
the mosaic theory.
e United States v. Hammond, 996 F.3d 374 (7th Cir. 2021) — important Seventh Circuit treatment of
Carpenter and the comparison between Knotts-type surveillance and more comprehensive tracking.
e = Scholl v. illinois State Police, No. 25-1847 — direct ALPR appeal pending in the Seventh Circuit; oral
argument occurred September 29, 2026.
U.S. Supreme Court
e United States v. Knotts, 460 U.S. 276 (1983) — monitoring vehicle movements on public roads using
a beeper did not constitute a search.
e United States v. Jones, 565 U.S. 400 (2012) — physical installation and use of GPS on a vehicle
constituted a search.
e Carpenter v. United States, 585 U.S. 296 (2018) — seven days of historical CSLI constituted a search
because it provided an extensive chronicle of the defendant's movements.
Recent ALPR authority
e United States v. Porter, No. 25-60163 (5th Cir. Mar. 17, 2026) — direct appellate holding that the
ALPR system at issue did not constitute a Fourth Amendment search.
e Schmidt v. City of Norfolk, No. 2:24-cv-00621 (E.D. Va. Jan. 27, 2026) — summary judgment for the
City; Flock ALPR system did not constitute a search on the developed record.
