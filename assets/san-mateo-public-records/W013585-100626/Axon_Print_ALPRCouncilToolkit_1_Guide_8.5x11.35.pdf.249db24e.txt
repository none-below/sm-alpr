Why This Guide Exists
A council vote on an automated license plate recognition (ALPR) program is a governance decision before it is a 
technology decision. The questions that decide the room are not answered by detection rates or camera counts 
alone. They are about who controls the data, who can see it, what happens when someone outside the agency 
requests access, and what shows the agency is operating the way it said it would. An agency that can answer 
those questions with built-in controls and reviewable records is prepared to have a productive conversation 
about how the program is governed.
This guide is built for that. It helps agency leaders explain who controls the data, who can access it and how the 
program is reviewed. The companion toolkit helps agencies turn that foundation into local answers, checklists 
and proof sources.
The Questions That Decide the Room
Most ALPR hearings turn on a small set of questions. The questions below will help you prepare direct answers, 
and the next section shows the architecture behind each one.
What does the system collect, and what does it leave out?
Each detection captures a license plate image, a vehicle context image, basic vehicle attributes such as color, 
make and vehicle type, and the time and location. Context images provide vehicle context for review. They are 
not used for facial recognition, driver identification or behavioral profiling. The system does not perform live 
registered-owner lookups when a plate is read. 
Who owns the data, sets retention and controls access?
The agency does. The agency owns its ALPR data, sets retention periods, controls user access and decides 
whether and how sharing is enabled. Those are agency decisions. The system applies the configured controls, 
and the agency remains responsible for policy, training and oversight.
Can another agency see this data, and does sharing create a national pool?
Sharing is off by default and requires the owning agency’s deliberate approval. Another agency can access data 
only through a specific sharing relationship approved by the owning agency. That access is invite-only, visible 
and revocable. Sharing creates no national pool, no copy of agency ALPR data and no transfer of ownership. A 
recipient agency cannot re-share ALPR data it receives through a sharing relationship.

Can the system be used to follow people or vehicles over time?
The system does not monitor people or maintain a live location feed. When investigating a specific vehicle, an 
authorized user can review prior detections of that plate within the agency’s retention period. That search is tied 
to a named user and logged with the action, time and stated reason.
What happens if a federal agency or outside party seeks agency ALPR data through Axon or directly from 
the agency?
Through Axon, there is no automatic, standing or built-in federal access to agency ALPR data. Because the 
agency owns and controls the data, a lawful request goes to the agency and its counsel to handle. If Axon 
receives such a request, Axon directs it to the agency as the data owner and discloses data only where legally 
required to do so.
What record shows the program is operating within policy?
The audit record does. Access, searches, sharing activity where enabled and configuration changes are logged 
and available for agency review. When sharing is enabled, that record includes recipient activity against shared 
data and changes to sharing relationships over time. The companion Audit & Accountability Checklist helps 
agencies turn those logs into a repeatable oversight process.
The rest of this guide explains what makes these short answers enforceable, so the agency can point to the 
controls, records and decision rights behind each claim before the hearing forces the question.

How the Architecture Answers Them
This section shows the architecture behind each of those questions, the system controls that apply the agency’s 
decisions and the records that let the agency verify them.
What the system collects, and what it does not
The collection boundary is defined by what the system is designed to capture: vehicle detection information, 
not driver identity. The context image gives reviewers visual information around the vehicle. Because it is an 
image from the scene, it may incidentally include surroundings, signage or people in view. It is not analyzed 
for facial recognition or behavioral profiling, and its retention follows the agency’s policy for ALPR detection 
data. The system does not perform live registered-owner lookups when a plate is read, does not independently 
retrieve personal records about individuals and does not build a profile of a registered owner. Identifying who 
is associated with a vehicle is a separate, deliberate step an agency takes through its own authorized channels, 
outside this system. Agency policy governs that step. The system maintains that separation by design, while 
access and activity are logged so the agency has a record it can review.

Who owns the data, sets retention and controls access
Retention settings are applied by the system on the schedule the agency sets for ALPR detection data. Data 
subject to that policy is automatically removed when the applicable retention period expires. Detections 
preserved as evidence follow the agency’s evidence retention rules. Access is scoped to the users and roles the 
agency authorizes. The governance is the agency’s. The system applies those controls and records activity so 
the agency can review use against policy.
Why sharing does not create a national pool
In pooled or network-based models, exposure can expand when visibility, copies or downstream access extend 
beyond what the originating agency can see or control. The agency-controlled model is built around a different 
boundary. Visibility changes only when the owning agency changes it. The sharing relationship stays visible to 
the owner and revocable, and agency ALPR data is not copied into a shared repository or routed into a national 
pool. The owning agency keeps ownership throughout. Because sharing does not copy the data or transfer 
ownership, a recipient agency cannot re-share ALPR data it receives through a sharing relationship.

Why historical lookup is not live tracking
Detection is automated. Operational judgment remains human. Investigative search reaches backward into 
records the agency already holds under its own policy, for an authorized purpose, tied to a named user and 
bounded by the agency’s retention period. It is architecturally different from continuous tracking. There is no 
persistent watch on a person, no live feed that follows an individual across locations, and no system action that 
substitutes for human judgment.
These boundaries are enforced through system design, configuration controls and reviewable records. The next 
section explains how those records support the agency’s accountability case.
What happens when an outside or federal entity wants the data
Agency ALPR data is not placed into a national or pooled database that outside entities can access through 
Axon. The agency remains the data owner and decision-maker. Because the agency owns and controls the data, 
a lawful request goes to the agency and its counsel to handle. If Axon receives such a request, Axon directs it to 
the agency as the data owner and discloses data only where legally required to do so.

The Accountability Record
A governance case is only as strong as the record behind it. The boundaries in the previous section are 
supported by system controls, configuration choices and reviewable records an agency can use to show how 
the program is operating. That record is what helps separate a program governed in practice from a program 
described in policy alone.
The accountability record starts with the audit log. Access, searches, sharing activity where enabled and 
configuration changes are recorded and available to the agency. When sharing is enabled, the audit record 
includes recipient activity against shared data and changes to sharing relationships over time, so the agency can 
review who had access during a specific period. The log helps answer the questions an oversight body asks after 
a program is running: which user took which action, the timestamp and the stated reason for access. It is the 
difference between telling a council the program is used appropriately and being able to show the activity record 
behind that statement.
A log supports accountability when the agency reviews it consistently. That is the work the companion 
Audit & Accountability Checklist is built for. It gives the agency a repeatable model: who owns the audit function, 
how often access and activity are reviewed, what a review looks for and how exceptions are handled. An agency 
that can describe its review cadence and name its audit owner walks into a hearing with an operating oversight 
process, not just a system capability. The capability is in the system. The discipline of using it belongs to the 
agency.
The public transparency portal is a separate mechanism with a different job. Where the audit log is the agency’s 
internal accountability record, the portal is a public-facing disclosure tool that lets an agency publish information 
about its ALPR program for community visibility. It supports the agency’s public transparency approach, 
which is the agency’s decision to make. It is a way to show the public the shape of the program. It is not the 
accountability mechanism itself. The audit record is.
For current security and certification documentation, trust.axon.com is the source. It holds Axon’s published 
security and compliance materials and is the right reference when a council or city attorney asks about the
