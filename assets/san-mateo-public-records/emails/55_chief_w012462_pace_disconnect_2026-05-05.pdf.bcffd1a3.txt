After our discussion earlier today and the offer to schedule a meeting, it felt like we were engaging on these issues in good

At the current production pace (32 records produced in 33 days, against the Department's own 211,872-document scope), completing

This does not feel like a good-faith, diligent effort, and it stands in contrast to the tone of today's conversation.
Why this PRA exists
On more than one occasion, the Department has responded to a records request of mine with "no responsive records," and I have
subsequently discovered -- through an unrelated PRA -- that a responsive Flock email did in fact exist at the time of the initial
request. Due to this failure in the Department's own searching techniques, I requested all Flock emails sent to SMPD
(@flocksafety.com and @mail.flocksafety.com, January 2020 to present), so I could perform my own searches.
Two examples of the search-failure pattern
In March 2026, in response to a PRA seeking records of changes to SMPD's Flock sharing list -- including the addition and removal
of University of the Pacific, a private institution whose access to SMPD's ALPR data violates California Civil Code §
1798.90.55(b) (SB 34) -- the Department stated that no responsive records existed and that the Flock platform did not maintain a
log of such changes. In April 2026, a separate PRA produced the Flock platform's event log directly. The records existed at the
time of the Department's "no responsive records" answer.
In February 2026, in response to a PRA seeking records documenting how the Department determined which of 283 agencies to grant
access to its ALPR data, the Department stated that no responsive records existed. A subsequent PRA produced Flock-generated
"Camera Access Request" emails -- including a February 2023 request from a North Carolina sheriff's office to an SMPD lieutenant -
- that were in Department inboxes at the time of that "no responsive records" answer. These emails included details of the process
for granting or refusing access -- substantively responsive to what the original PRA had asked. The current W012462-040226
production has surfaced an additional Camera Access Request email this week. Each batch produces more records of the type that the
earlier search said did not exist.
To be clear about priority
This PRA is a backup, not my primary records request. Most of my open PRAs ask for records that Department policy already requires
to exist: audit memos, documented agency-access approvals, verification logs. Some of those records don't yet exist -- that's one
of the things these PRAs have surfaced. But the city will need them regardless of my requests, and producing them serves the
Department's own compliance posture, not just mine. The Flock email corpus exists only as a fallback.
The substantive PRAs ask about audits that state law has required since 2015 but for which no records exist before December 2025;
the authorization for adding University of the Pacific to the ALPR sharing list in apparent violation of SB 34 and without
documented authority from the required approver; and whether any hot-list alert verification records exist -- the 5/5/26 letter
says they don't, which is itself a finding the email production is positioned to test.
An apparently unauthorized change to law enforcement ALPR sharing configurations -- adding a private institution in violation of
state law -- is an internal governance matter the Department should want to investigate regardless of my PRAs.

What is the review process being applied to these records, and what accounts for the per-record time?
     * No redacted emails have appeared in any rolling batch, which suggests the 32 emails released are the full set the
Department has actually reviewed, not a subset drawn from a larger corpus with withheld exempt portions. Under § 7922.525(b), the

Dear Chief,
Thank you for the substantial time your records team -- Kelly O'Keefe and now Captain Maravillas -- has spent responding to my
Public Records Act requests over the past three months. The work has been real, and I want to acknowledge it. I should also be
candid that substantial work remains: open requests are on rolling production, and additional requests are likely as further
gaps surface.
Most of this work would not be necessary if the underlying records already existed. The PRA process is currently doing the work
the internal audit framework was supposed to do. SOP 205.1's posting requirement, Policy 462.8's external-agency approval files,
and SOP 205.5.1's quarterly operator audits -- performed and documented in real time -- would moot whole categories of the
requests the department is now spending substantial time on. The current volume reflects more than a decade of deferred audits,
documentation, and postings surfacing through public records requests all at once. I'd like to use this letter to identify where
that loop closes -- not by asking for any new policy or law, but by asking for consistent application of what the department has
already committed to and what state law already requires.
Representations and practice. For more than a decade, across multiple ALPR platforms, the department's presentations to Council
have described its program in terms of specific protective measures. At the September 2020 study session on police
accountability, SMPD represented that "SMPD conducts regular audits to ensure access to the ALPR Databases are within policy."
When Council approved the August 2023 Flock master services agreement, the staff report represented that the program uses
"strictest industry standards with respect to how data is accessed and by whom" and shares with "NCRIC and allied County law

enforcement agencies." Neither representation has been corrected on the record, and none of the stated measures has been
substantially implemented.
The records the department has since produced establish multiple gaps between law, policy, and practice:
* Audits. Policy 462.6(c), renumbered to 463.6(c) in January 2023, requires audits "on a regular basis, as described in SOP
205." No formal audit records existed before December 2025; the audits performed since conform to neither state law nor
department policy.
* External-agency approval. Policy 463.8(b) (Dec 2025) requires that external-agency access requests be "reviewed by the ALPR
Committee Captain or the authorized designee" before being fulfilled. An equivalent documented-review requirement has been on
the books continuously since the original Policy 462 in 2012. Agencies are added through the Flock platform without documented
review or retained records -- a process that, in February 2026, placed at least one agency on the list inconsistent with §
1798.90.55(b), identified only after a resident raised the issue.
* Sharing scope. The August 2023 staff report described sharing with "NCRIC and allied County law enforcement agencies." The
Flock transparency portal currently lists 283 organizations, spanning the state from Yuba County to San Diego and including ten
District Attorney offices (none in San Mateo County), eleven campus and education police departments (most located outside San
Mateo County), and -- until February 2026 -- at least one private university that does not qualify as a public agency under §
1798.90.5(f).
* SOP 205 posting. Section 205.1 and Civil Code § 1798.90.51(b) require conspicuous public posting. SOP 205 was acknowledged
unposted on March 3, 2026 -- meaning that for over six years, residents could not see the policy the department claimed to
operate under. It has since been published in a combined manual not linked from the License Plate Readers page, in a manner not
apparently compliant with SB 34.
* Council process. The June 2020 presentation was a non-budgetary informational item that did not request or receive any
approval; Council asked for a report-back "after a year of experience," not delivered in the nearly six years since. The first
Council vote on the program was the August 2023 master services agreement, placed on the consent calendar, nearly three years
after the system had been in operation and after the deployment had nearly doubled -- from twelve cameras to twenty-two --
without further Council action.
The compliance work that began in late 2025 -- the monthly audit memos, the new RTIC operational procedure (Procedure 1102), the
December 2025 policy revision restricting sharing to California public agencies, and the new "internal and external" framing in
the February and March memos -- represents real progress on each of these fronts. None of them is yet complete. Audits now
exist, but the implemented audits review sharing configuration -- required by neither policy -- rather than the audits required
by Policy 463.10 and SOP 205.5.1. The sharing scope has been narrowed by disabling out-of-state lookups, but the entity-type
composition of the existing list has not been reviewed. Two months after the city acknowledged the SOP 205 posting as overdue
and committed to publish it "as soon as possible," the document has been placed in a combined SOP manual on a section of the
city website that is not linked from the License Plate Readers page -- not the conspicuous, standalone form required by Section
205.1 and § 1798.90.51(b). There is still no documented approval process for the 283 agencies already on the list. Two unaligned
written ALPR policies are in force, neither one implemented as written, and the department's actual practices live outside both.
Even taken together, the two policies still don't fully meet state law. The questions below are about completing that work.
Audit scope.
Two parallel audit requirements apply to the program, and the cycle of audits that began in December 2025 satisfies neither as
written. Policy 463.10 -- titled "ALPR Data Search Audits," added December 2025 -- requires monthly audits of ALPR data searches
by internal users and quarterly audits of searches by external California public agency users, documented as memoranda to the
Chief of Police. SOP 205.5.1, in place since at least October 2019, requires quarterly audits of operator use of Flock and the
other ALPR platforms, submitted to the Support Services Captain. The audit memos produced to date are monthly memoranda to the
Chief, but they review Flock sharing configuration -- out-of-state toggles, network-lookup settings, user counts -- content
neither policy requires. The February and March 2026 memos added "internal and external" framing to the same monthly format; the
content remained sharing configuration. No audit responsive to either policy has been produced.
PRA W012160 returned "no responsive records" for the quarterly operator audits SOP 205.5.1 requires; PRA W012320 has returned
"at this time there are no responsive records to your request" for the related SOP 205 search-activity audit records, while
noting that "if any responsive records are located, we will further respond." If the required audits were being conducted, the
records would be easy to find. When and how does the department intend to perform the data search audits Policy 463.10 requires
and the operator audits SOP 205.5.1 requires?
Entity-type review.
University of the Pacific appeared on the department's Flock sharing list as of February 18, 2026, in apparent violation of
state law. UOP's own Chief of Police confirmed in writing that UOP "is a private institution and therefore not subject to the
CPRA." Civil Code § 1798.90.55(b) restricts sharing to "another public agency" (defined at § 1798.90.5(f)); Policy 463.8 adds
that the public agency must be a California state or local agency. The January 2026 audit memo verified that out-of-state
sharing was disabled -- a geographic check, not an entity-type check. The audit cycle covering the period when UOP was on the
list did not surface it. What review is the department undertaking, or planning, to confirm that each of the remaining 282
organizations on the access list qualifies as a public agency under § 1798.90.5(f), and what does the department understand its
continuing obligation under § 1798.90.55(b) to require?
SB 34 compliance plan.
Beyond the items above, the department also accesses data from privately-owned community cameras that falls outside the current
audit scope, and no published timeline or accountable owner has been identified for closing the remaining gaps. Does the city
intend to bring the ALPR program into full compliance with the department's own policies, AG Bulletin 2023-DLE-06, and SB 34,
and if so, on what timeline and under whose accountability?

Linking SOP 205 -- and the standalone Policy 463 PDF -- from the License Plate Readers page closes a PRA category. Documenting
an entity-type review closes another. Conducting the data search audits Policy 463.10 requires and the operator audits SOP
205.5.1 requires closes a third; committing to the quarterly cadence the SOP itself prescribes prevents that gap from re-
opening. Five San Mateo County peer cities -- Belmont, Foster City, Menlo Park, Pacifica, and South San Francisco -- already
publish their Flock search audit data through the platform's CSV download. The same option is available for SMPD.
A realistic first step is to commit to a line-by-line review of the department's own policies, AG Bulletin 2023-DLE-06, and
Civil Code §§ 1798.90.51-.55 against current practice -- with a documented process to ensure each requirement is met and stays
met on an ongoing basis. I have compiled a more detailed inventory of these gaps, organized against those same authorities, in a
findings document at https://github.com/none-below/sm-alpr/releases/latest/download/SMPD_ALPR_Findings.pdf. You are welcome to
use it as a guide.
I will continue to file monthly Public Records Act requests for the documents that city policy and state law require -- requests
that have largely returned "no responsive records" -- until the city's records show those policies are being followed.
The recent compliance work reads as an effort toward alignment with state law and the city's own policies. The gaps above and in
my report show the work is not finished. I am not writing to question the value of ALPR -- the documented cases where it has
helped solve serious crimes are real, and I want San Mateo to be a city tough on crime. Following the law is part of being tough
on crime, not in tension with it. The department holds residents to the law; residents should be able to expect a department
that holds itself to the laws protecting their privacy and rights. Closing the gaps above is what makes that standard real on
both sides.
